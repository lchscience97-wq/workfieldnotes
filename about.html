About

What this site is for

Work Field Notes is an independent reference site covering the practical questions that come up mid-task: what a role actually involves, which body has authority, what a system is for, and where the numbers come from.

Why it exists

Search for something like "health information professionals" and you get a professional association page, a university admissions page, a job board, and a government statistics table. Each is accurate. None of them answers the whole question, and none of them tells you which parts have changed recently.

This site fills that gap. Each guide takes one question and answers it completely — the definition, the numbers, the routes in, the things that commonly trip people up — and cites where each fact came from.

Who runs this site

Work Field Notes is published and edited by C. H. Lee. The site is independently owned and operated; it is not affiliated with, sponsored by, or endorsed by any government agency, professional association, employer or vendor named in these guides.

The editor's professional background is in cost accounting, management reporting and data analysis in a manufacturing company — work that consists largely of tracing where a number came from, which system produced it, which rule governs it, and whether it still holds. That is the same discipline this site applies to public reference material. It is not clinical, legal or financial expertise, and this site does not claim any. Where a question requires that kind of expertise, the guides say so and point to the body that has it.

Editorial responsibility for every page on this site rests with the editor. Questions, corrections and complaints go to hello@workfieldnotes.com and are answered personally.

Editorial standards

These are the rules every guide is held to. They are written down here so that you can check whether a given page follows them.

  1. Primary sources first. Figures come from the body that produced them — statistical agencies, regulators, standards organisations, professional certifying bodies — and are linked directly. Secondary aggregators are not used as the source of a number.
  2. Every figure carries a date. A wage band, an enforcement total or a growth projection is a fact about a period, not a permanent truth. The period is stated in the text or in the table caption.
  3. Each page shows when it was last verified. The record card at the top of every guide gives the verification date, the jurisdiction, the vintage of the underlying data, and the number of sources cited.
  4. The answer comes first. The question is answered in the opening paragraph. Context follows for readers who want it. Nothing is withheld to lengthen the page.
  5. Disagreement is stated, not smoothed over. Where sources conflict, the conflict is described rather than averaged away.
  6. Uncertainty is labelled. If something could not be verified, the guide says so instead of implying more confidence than the evidence supports.

How a guide is made

We are explicit about our process because you should be able to judge it.

  1. Question selection. Topics are chosen from questions people actually search for, not from what is easy to write.
  2. Source gathering. The relevant primary sources are located and read — agency handbooks, regulatory guidance, certifying-body requirements, official statistical releases.
  3. Drafting. Drafts are prepared with the assistance of AI language tools. We think readers are entitled to know that.
  4. Verification. Every figure, date, eligibility rule and citation in a draft is checked against the primary source by the editor before publication. Anything that cannot be verified is removed rather than softened. This step is the reason the process exists; the drafting tool does not decide what is true.
  5. Revision. Guides are revised when the underlying source changes, and the verification date at the top of the page is updated with it.

On AI assistance. Using a drafting tool does not lower the standard a page has to meet — it raises the amount of checking required. The value of a guide here is not that a human typed it; it is that every number in it is traceable to a named source with a date, and that you can click through and confirm it yourself.

What is covered

Eleven sections, listed on the home page. They range across health information, financial services regulation, cards and payments, cybersecurity, management systems, business services, domains and web infrastructure, marketing and research, mobile and apps, and cloud storage. The common thread is not an industry; it is the reader. These are the things working professionals in the United States look up while doing their job.

Corrections

If something here is wrong or out of date, tell us. Send the page address and what should be there instead — a link to the source helps. Corrections are made promptly, the verification date is updated, and where the change is material it is noted on the page. Write to hello@workfieldnotes.com with "Correction" in the subject line.
