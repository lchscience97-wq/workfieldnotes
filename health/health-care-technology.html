Health Care Technology: Two Regulators, One Phrase

The phrase spans two regulatory worlds that almost never appear on the same page. Medical devices answer to the FDA and a three-class risk system. Certified record systems answer to ASTP and a certification programme. Software can land in either, both, or neither.

What decides it is a statute: 21 U.S.C. §360j(o) lists the software functions Congress removed from the device definition. And one of those exclusions is conditional on certification under the health IT programme — which is where the two regulators are wired together in a single sentence.

One phrase, two worlds

Results for this term mix a vendor, a large technology company, a research agency and two universities. That spread is the finding: health care technology covers infusion pumps, imaging hardware, scheduling software, record systems, wearables and analytics platforms, and no single regulator sees all of them.

Two regimes account for most of the regulated surface. The FDA regulates devices, including software that meets the device definition. ASTP runs the health IT certification programme, which is voluntary in form and near-mandatory in effect for record systems. Our guides to HIT, HIE and EHR sorted and what a complete view would require cover the second world; this page connects it to the first.

Three classes, three burdens

FDA's classification system is written around how much control is needed to give reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness, and the definitions at 21 CFR 860.3 are unusually readable.

Class I is where general controls are sufficient to provide reasonable assurance of the safety and effectiveness of the device. Class II is where general controls alone are insufficient to provide reasonable assurance of its safety and effectiveness and there is sufficient information to establish special controls. Class III is where insufficient information exists to determine that general controls are sufficient and the device is life-supporting or life-sustaining or presents a potential unreasonable risk.

Read as a sequence, the three classes are not really about danger. They are about how much is known. Class III is the category for products where the evidence base does not yet support a lighter touch, which is why a familiar-looking product can sit there and an unfamiliar one need not.

How a device reaches the market

The class determines the route. For Class I and Class II devices that are not exempt, the route is a 510(k) premarket notification. FDA states that approximately 572 or 74% of the Class I devices are exempt from that requirement, so most of the lowest-risk category never files one.

Class III devices typically require a premarket approval application, the most demanding route.

The scale of the system is worth knowing when a vendor says a product is FDA cleared: FDA maintains classifications for approximately 1,700 different generic types of devices, organised into 16 medical specialty panels and codified across parts 862 to 892 of Title 21. A clearance is always a clearance for a specific generic type and a specific intended use, never for a company.

The software carve-outs

Congress removed several software functions from the device definition outright. The list sits at 21 U.S.C. §360j(o)(1), and each subparagraph is a different kind of product.

(A) Administrative support covers software for administrative support of a health care facility, including the processing and maintenance of financial records, claims or billing information, appointment schedules, business analytics, information about patient populations, admissions, practice and inventory management, analysis of historical claims data to predict future utilization or cost-effectiveness, determination of health benefit eligibility, population health management, and laboratory workflow.

(B) Wellness covers software for maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle that is unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition.

(D) Clinical data display covers transferring, storing, converting formats, or displaying clinical laboratory test or other device data and results — unless such function is intended to interpret or analyze them.

Notice the shape those share. The exclusion holds while the software moves, stores or presents. It stops at interpretation.

Where the two regulators meet

Subparagraph (C), on electronic patient records, is the one that connects the two worlds, and it is conditional on three things at once.

The exclusion covers software intended to serve as electronic patient records, including patient-provided information, to the extent that such records are intended to transfer, store, convert formats, or display the equivalent of a paper medical chart — but only so long as the records were created, stored, transferred, or reviewed by health care professionals, or by individuals working under supervision of such professionals; the records are part of health information technology that is certified under section 300jj–11(c)(5) of title 42; and the function is not intended to interpret or analyze patient records, including medical image data, for diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention or treatment.

That middle condition is the wiring. A record system escapes device regulation partly because it is certified under the health IT programme. Certification is not merely a procurement badge; for this exclusion it is a statutory prerequisite. An uncertified record system cannot rely on subparagraph (C).

Clinical decision support, and the independent-review test

Subparagraph (E) is the most argued-over provision in health software, and its structure is worth reading precisely.

The exclusion does not apply where the function is intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image or a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system. Where that gate is passed, the covered purposes are displaying, analyzing, or printing medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines); supporting or providing recommendations to a health care professional about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition; and enabling such health care professional to independently review the basis for such recommendations that such software presents so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision.

The last clause is the operative test and it is a design requirement, not a disclaimer. Software that shows its reasoning so a clinician can check it sits outside the device definition. Software whose output is meant to be relied on primarily does not, however it is labelled.

What to ask about a product

Three questions, in order. Does any function of this product meet the device definition, and if so which class and which route did it take — a 510(k), a premarket approval, or an exemption? If it does not, which subparagraph of 21 U.S.C. §360j(o)(1) is being relied on? And is the product certified health information technology, which is a separate question with a separate answer?

The second question is the one that surfaces the most. A vendor claiming a product is not a medical device is asserting an exclusion, and every exclusion has conditions. Asking which one converts a general reassurance into a checkable claim.

What neither regime promises

Neither is a guarantee of clinical benefit. A 510(k) clearance rests on comparison to an existing device rather than on a fresh demonstration of outcomes, and certification tests conformance to criteria rather than usefulness in a clinic.

Neither settles interoperability either, which is governed by the standards and information blocking rules covered in our guide to what a complete view would require. And neither addresses price, procurement or whether a product suits your setting.

What the two regimes do settle is which questions have already been asked of a product and by whom. For a phrase as elastic as health care technology, that is the only durable way to compare two things that share a name and nothing else.

Questions people actually ask

Which agency regulates health care technology?

It depends on the product. Medical devices, including some software, are regulated by the FDA under the device classification system. Certified health information technology sits with ASTP and the ONC Health IT Certification Program. A single product can touch both, and some software functions are excluded from the device definition by statute at 21 U.S.C. §360j(o).

What are the three device classes?

Class I is where general controls are sufficient to provide reasonable assurance of the safety and effectiveness of the device. Class II is where general controls alone are insufficient and there is sufficient information to establish special controls. Class III is where insufficient information exists to determine that general controls are sufficient and the device is life-supporting or life-sustaining, or presents a potential unreasonable risk. The definitions sit at 21 CFR 860.3.

How does a device reach the market?

For Class I and Class II devices that are not exempt, through a 510(k) premarket notification. FDA states that approximately 572 or 74 percent of Class I devices are exempt from that requirement. Class III devices typically require a premarket approval application. FDA maintains classifications for approximately 1,700 different generic types of devices across 16 medical specialty panels, in parts 862 to 892 of Title 21.

Is all health software a medical device?

No. Section 520(o) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, at 21 U.S.C. §360j(o), excludes several categories of software function from the device definition: administrative support of a health care facility, software for maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle unrelated to disease, electronic patient records meeting stated conditions, transferring, storing, converting formats or displaying clinical data, and certain clinical decision support.

What conditions apply to the electronic records exclusion?

Three. The records must have been created, stored, transferred or reviewed by health care professionals, or by individuals working under their supervision. The records must be part of health information technology certified under section 300jj–11(c)(5) of title 42. And the function must not be intended to interpret or analyse patient records, including medical image data, for the purpose of diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention or treatment.

What makes clinical decision support software non-device?

The exclusion turns on both what the software does and what it does not do. It does not apply where the function is intended to acquire, process or analyse a medical image, a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device, or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system. Where it does apply, the software displays or analyses medical information, supports or provides recommendations to a health care professional, and enables that professional to independently review the basis for the recommendations.

What should I ask a vendor?

Whether the product or any of its functions meets the device definition, and if so which class and which clearance or approval route it took. Whether it relies on an exclusion at 21 U.S.C. §360j(o), and which subparagraph. And whether it is certified health information technology, which is a separate question with a separate answer and, for the records exclusion, a statutory prerequisite.
