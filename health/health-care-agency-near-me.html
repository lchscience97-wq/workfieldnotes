The results are all companies, and they are not selling the same thing. Under this one phrase sit Medicare-certified home health agencies, hospices, and non-medical personal care businesses. The word agency does not distinguish them; certification does.

The distinction decides who pays. Medicare will not pay for custodial or personal care when that is the only care you need — which is exactly what most families searching this phrase are looking for. Knowing that before the first call saves a great deal of time.

Three businesses, one word

A Medicare-certified home health agency delivers skilled care at home under a physician's plan, operating under federal conditions of participation at 42 CFR Part 484.

A hospice delivers end-of-life care under its own separate conditions and its own benefit.

A non-medical personal care agency supplies help with bathing, dressing, meals, companionship and household tasks. It is generally licensed and regulated by the state rather than certified under Part 484, and it is usually paid privately or through Medicaid programmes rather than by Medicare home health.

All three market themselves with the same vocabulary, and the results for this search mix them freely. Our guide to what certification actually buys covers the certification question in general; this page covers what it means for the thing you are trying to arrange.

The Medicare gate

Two conditions must both hold before Medicare's home health benefit engages, and neither is negotiable at the agency's end.

First, certification after an examination. Medicare states that a health care provider (like a nurse practitioner) must assess you face-to-face before certifying that you need home health services.

Second, homebound status. Medicare frames it as two conditions together: leaving your home isn't recommended because of your condition or you have trouble leaving your home without help, and you're normally unable to leave your home and leaving takes a lot of effort.

Notice what the second is not. It is not a prohibition on ever leaving. It is a description of difficulty, and it is assessed rather than self-declared.

What is covered when the gate opens

The benefit covers part-time or intermittent skilled nursing care, including wound care and monitoring; physical and occupational therapy; speech-language pathology; medical social services; and part-time or intermittent home health aide care (only if you're also getting skilled nursing care or therapy) for assistance with bathing, feeding and similar activities.

That parenthesis is the structural point. Aide help is an accompaniment to skilled care under this benefit, never a standalone service. A family seeking only help with daily living is outside the benefit by design, not by refusal.

The ceiling nobody expects

Part-time or intermittent sounds like a scheduling word. It is a quantity.

Medicare describes it as typically allowing skilled nursing care and home health aide services up to 8 hours a day combined, for a maximum of 28 hours a week.

Read those two numbers together and the shape of the benefit becomes clear. Twenty-eight hours is four seven-hour days, or about one sixth of a week. It is support around a life largely managed by the household, not a substitute for that management.

What Medicare will not pay for

The exclusions are stated plainly and are worth reading before any agency conversation.

Medicare does not pay for 24-hour-a-day care at your home. It does not pay for home meal delivery. It does not pay for homemaker services (like shopping and cleaning) unrelated to your care plan. And it does not pay for custodial or personal care for daily living when that is the only care you need.

That last exclusion is the one that redirects most searches. The need families most often have — someone present, helping with the day — is precisely the need the Medicare home health benefit does not address. The third kind of agency exists because of that gap.

What certification brings with it

Where an agency is Medicare-certified, a written set of patient rights attaches under 42 CFR 484.50, and its procedural detail is unusually specific.

Under 484.50(a)(1) the agency must provide written notice of rights during the initial evaluation visit, in advance of furnishing care to the patient, covering rights and responsibilities, transfer and discharge policies and administrator contact information. Under (a)(2) it must obtain the patient's signature confirming receipt, and under (a)(4) provide notice to a patient-selected representative within 4 business days of the initial evaluation visit.

The rights at 484.50(c) include treatment with respect for property and person; freedom from verbal, mental, sexual, and physical abuse, including injuries of unknown source, neglect; the right to file complaints; to participate in and consent to or refuse care decisions; to confidential clinical records; to be informed of payment coverage and charges; and to receive notice of non-covered services before they are furnished.

Two more are worth naming. 484.50(c)(9) requires that a patient be advised of the state toll free home health telephone hot line, its contact information, its hours of operation. And 484.50(e)(1) requires the agency to investigate complaints including mistreatment, neglect, or verbal, mental, sexual, and physical abuse, document its findings, and take action to prevent further violations while the investigation proceeds.

Who comes to the door

Under a certified agency, the aide's preparation has a federal floor. 42 CFR 484.80(b)(1) requires classroom and supervised practical training totalling at least 75 hours, with (b)(2) requiring a minimum of 16 hours of classroom training to precede a minimum of 16 hours of supervised practical training. Under (d) an aide must receive at least 12 hours of in-service training in each 12-month period.

Outside that framework the floor is whatever the state sets. That is not an accusation against the third category — state requirements exist and many agencies exceed them — but it is a different question to ask, and asking it by name is more productive than asking whether staff are qualified. Our guide to who pays and who sets the hours works through the training rules.

The call, in order

Are you Medicare-certified, and for home health or hospice? If not, what state licence do you hold? Who pays for what you provide, in my situation? What are your rates and minimum hours? And will I receive a written notice of rights before care begins?

The first question sorts the three categories in one sentence. The last is a legal requirement for one of them and a good sign in any of them. If comparing certified agencies, our guide to the federal data behind the rankings covers the published measures.

What none of it settles

Certification does not tell you whether an agency is reliable on a Tuesday, whether the same aide comes each visit, or whether the schedule survives a staff shortage. Those are the questions families actually live with, and no federal category answers them.

Nor does any of this make the non-medical category lesser. For the need most people have, it is the right instrument, and the Medicare benefit is simply not built for it. What the distinctions do is stop a family spending weeks pursuing coverage for a service the programme has expressly excluded — which is the most common and most costly error in this whole area.

Questions people actually ask

What does Medicare require before it pays for home health?

Medicare states that a health care provider, such as a nurse practitioner, must assess you face-to-face before certifying that you need home health services. You must also be homebound, which Medicare describes as leaving your home not being recommended because of your condition or you having trouble leaving without help, and being normally unable to leave your home with leaving taking a lot of effort.

What does part-time or intermittent actually mean?

Medicare describes it as typically allowing skilled nursing care and home health aide services up to 8 hours a day combined, for a maximum of 28 hours a week. That is the ceiling most people are surprised by, because it defines the benefit as episodic support rather than continuous care.

What will Medicare not pay for at home?

Medicare states it does not pay for 24-hour-a-day care at your home, home meal delivery, homemaker services such as shopping and cleaning that are unrelated to your care plan, or custodial or personal care for daily living when that is the only care you need. That last exclusion is the one that sends most families to a different kind of agency entirely.

Can I get a home health aide without skilled nursing?

Not under the Medicare home health benefit. Medicare covers part-time or intermittent home health aide care only if you are also getting skilled nursing care or therapy. Aide help with bathing, feeding and similar activities is an accompaniment to skilled care under this benefit, not a standalone service.

What rights do I have with a certified home health agency?

A written set. Under 42 CFR 484.50(a)(1) the agency must give written notice of rights during the initial evaluation visit, in advance of furnishing care, and under (a)(2) must obtain your signature confirming receipt. The rights at (c) include freedom from verbal, mental, sexual and physical abuse and neglect, the right to file complaints, to participate in and consent to or refuse care decisions, to be informed of coverage and charges, and to receive notice of non-covered services before they are furnished.

Is there a complaint line?

Yes, and the agency must tell you about it. 42 CFR 484.50(c)(9) requires that a patient be advised of the state toll free home health telephone hot line, its contact information and its hours of operation. Separately, 42 CFR 484.50(e)(1) requires the agency itself to investigate complaints, including allegations of mistreatment, neglect or abuse, document its findings and act to prevent further violations while investigating.

How do I tell which kind of agency I am looking at?

Ask whether it is Medicare-certified, and for which programme. A Medicare-certified home health agency operates under the conditions of participation at 42 CFR Part 484 and can bill Medicare for covered home health. A non-medical personal care agency generally cannot, and is governed by state licensing rather than by those federal conditions. The word agency does not distinguish them; certification does.
