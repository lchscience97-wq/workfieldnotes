Health Care Center: One Phrase, One Federal Test

Last verified

Jurisdiction
United States, federal

The words on the sign are not regulated. A solo physician's office, a hospital outpatient department and a national retail chain may each call themselves a health care center, and none of them is breaking a rule by doing so.

Drop the middle word and the position changes. Health center, when it refers to an entity funded or designated under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act, is a federal category with four conditions attached to it. In 2025 those conditions applied to 1,356 award recipients and 170 look-alikes serving 34.4 million people. This page sets out what the conditions are and how to tell whether the building in front of you meets them.

Two words apart

Search this term and the first page is a mixture of individual clinics with the phrase in their trading name, a county government page, a hospital system and a social services directory. That mixture is not a failure of the search engine. It is an accurate reflection of the fact that the phrase has no owner.

There is no federal statute reserving the words health care center, no certification body that awards them, and no penalty for using them. The same is true of medical center and, for the most part, of clinic — although clinic does pick up specific legal meanings inside particular payment and licensing regimes, which is a separate matter covered in our guide to the four legal forms of the word clinic.

What exists instead is a federal programme whose name sits two letters away. Its participants often trade under names containing the longer phrase, so the two get conflated constantly — and the conflation costs money, because the obligations attached to the federal category are exactly the ones most people are searching for when they type the phrase.

What the statute actually defines

The operative text is 42 U.S.C. §254b. Subsection (a)(1) defines a health center as an entity that serves a population that is medically underserved by providing required primary health services and, where appropriate, additional health services, for all residents of the area served by the center.

Two features of that sentence do the work. The first is medically underserved: the designation is tied to a population and a place, not to an organisation's preferences. The second is all residents. The statute does not describe a clinic for poor people. It describes a clinic obliged to serve everybody in its service area, with the discount machinery below existing to make that obligation survivable.

Subsection (b)(1)(A) then fixes what must be on offer. Required primary health services include basic health services covering family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics, obstetrics and gynecology; diagnostic laboratory and radiologic services; preventive health services including prenatal care, cancer screening, immunizations and dental screenings; emergency medical services; and pharmaceutical services. HRSA's compliance manual adds that additional services beyond the required list are permitted but are subject to review and approval by the agency, so a centre's service menu is not purely a local business decision.

Four programmes under one section

Section 330 is not a single grant. It is a family of them, and the subsection letter tells you which population a given award is aimed at.

Subsection (e) funds community health centers serving a general medically underserved population. Subsection (g) funds centres for migratory and seasonal agricultural workers and members of their families. Subsection (h) funds care for homeless individuals, including homeless children and youth and homeless veterans. Subsection (i) funds primary care for residents of public housing and for people in areas immediately accessible to such housing.

The distinctions are not cosmetic. HRSA's compliance manual notes that a centre funded under subsection (h) must additionally provide substance use disorder services — a requirement that does not attach to the other streams. A single organisation can hold awards under more than one subsection, which is why a centre's obligations are best read from its award package rather than inferred from its name. Our guide to what the homeless health care programme covers works through that stream in detail.

One point rarely makes it into consumer explanations: the applicant need not be a charity. HRSA recognises three eligible organisation types — private non-profits, public agencies including city and county health departments, and tribal or urban Indian organisations. City-run health departments therefore sit in the programme alongside independent non-profits.

The obligations attached to the money

Four requirements are worth knowing by heart, because they are the ones a patient can actually observe.

A schedule of fees and a schedule of discounts. Subsection (k)(3)(G) requires a centre to prepare a schedule of fees designed to cover its costs and a corresponding schedule of discounts adjusted on the basis of the patient's ability to pay. Note that this is two documents. A price list alone does not satisfy the provision.

A governing board with a patient majority. Subsection (k)(3)(H) requires a board composed of individuals, a majority of whom are being served by the centre, and requires that board to meet at least once a month, select services and approve budgets. This is the structural feature with no analogue in ordinary health care governance: the people receiving the service hold formal control of the entity providing it.

Service to all residents of the area. This flows from the definition in subsection (a)(1) rather than from a separate clause, and it is the reason a health center cannot simply decline a patient who cannot pay.

A defined scope of project. What a centre does, where, and for whom is fixed in its approved scope, and changes require HRSA action — which is why a centre may decline to add a service that seems obviously useful.

Deeming: the row nobody advertises

The Federally Supported Health Centers Assistance Acts of 1992 and 1995 allow HRSA to deem a health center and its people to be employees of the Public Health Service for liability purposes. Coverage extends to governing board members, officers, employees and certain individual contractors, for acts or omissions in the performance of medical, surgical, dental or related functions occurring within the scope of employment.

HRSA states that a covered entity or individual is immune and will not be financially liable for claims arising from covered activities. Congress's stated purpose was to increase the funds available for care by reducing or eliminating malpractice premiums.

For a patient the consequence is procedural: a claim against a deemed centre runs against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act, with that regime's administrative process and deadlines, rather than against a private defendant in state court. That is not a reason to avoid a health center; it is a reason to take advice early, because the two routes run on different clocks. Our guide to which kind of health care lawyer you actually need covers the routing question.

How Medicare pays, and why that reaches you

Federally qualified health centers are paid under a prospective payment system. CMS set the base rate at $207.72 per visit for calendar year 2026, a 2.5 percent increase over the 2025 rate of $202.65, driven by an FQHC market basket of 2.5 percent based on historical data through the second quarter of 2025. Each centre's rate is then adjusted by a geographic adjustment factor.

Rural health clinics work differently. Under 42 CFR 405.2462 they receive an all-inclusive per-visit rate subject to a statutory limit that steps up to $190 per visit by 2028 and rises with the Medicare Economic Index thereafter. Medicare pays 80 percent of that rate, with the deductible applying. For FQHC services Medicare pays 80 percent of the lesser of the centre's actual charge or the encounter rate, and the deductible does not apply.

That last clause is the one that reaches a patient's wallet. A Medicare beneficiary who has not met the annual deductible pays it at a rural health clinic and does not pay it for FQHC services. Two buildings a mile apart, both plausibly signed health care center, can therefore produce different bills on the same visit for reasons that have nothing to do with the medicine.

Who actually uses these centres

The 2025 Uniform Data System figures contradict the common assumption that these are clinics of last resort.

Award recipients reported 32,746,392 patients and look-alikes reported 1,650,551, for a combined 34,396,943. Among award recipients, 17.21 percent had no insurance and 47.92 percent were covered by Medicaid or CHIP — meaning roughly five in six patients arrived with a payer. Just under 90 percent had incomes at or below 200 percent of the federal poverty level. HRSA separately reports 144.1 million total visits in 2025, one in eight children in the country, one in five rural residents, and 414,000 veterans.

Look-alikes differ measurably: 10.22 percent uninsured against 17.21 percent, and 85.93 percent at or below 200 percent of poverty against 89.97 percent. The two categories carry the same obligations but do not serve identical populations.

Checking the centre in front of you

Three questions and one lookup settle it.

Ask whether the organisation holds a section 330 award or a look-alike designation. Ask to see the sliding fee discount schedule — not the price list, the discount schedule. Ask whether the governing board has a patient majority. Any organisation that genuinely holds the status will answer all three immediately, because each is a condition of keeping it. Then confirm the specific site in HRSA's own locator, which lists approved service delivery sites rather than organisations — a centre's main office appearing is not the same as your nearest branch appearing.

What the label still cannot tell you

Federal status is a floor, not a ranking. It guarantees that a discount schedule exists, that patients hold board seats, that the required service list is offered somewhere in the organisation's scope, and that nobody in the service area can be refused for inability to pay. It guarantees nothing about waiting times, clinician continuity, evening hours, whether your specific branch does dentistry, or whether the physician you want is accepting patients.

It says nothing about quality relative to a private practice down the road. The statute is an access instrument, not a quality mark. The category's correct use is narrow and valuable: it tells you what the organisation is obliged to do for you before you have paid anything — precisely the information a sign reading health care center withholds.

Questions people actually ask

Is health care center a protected term?

No. Nothing in federal law reserves the words health care center, and a private practice, a hospital outpatient department or a retail clinic may all use them. The nearly identical phrase health center does carry a federal meaning when it refers to an entity funded or designated under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act, codified at 42 U.S.C. §254b. The label on the door is not the test; the funding and designation behind it are.

What is the difference between an FQHC and a look-alike?

A Health Center Program awardee receives a direct federal award under section 330. A look-alike meets the same programme requirements and is designated as a federally qualified health center on the recommendation of the Health Resources and Services Administration, but receives no direct Health Center Program award. HRSA states a look-alike must already deliver primary health care in its service area and must be independently owned, controlled and operated. In 2025 there were 1,356 awardees and 170 look-alikes.

Do I have to be uninsured to use a health center?

No. Health centers serve insured and uninsured patients alike, and most of their patients are insured. In 2025 Health Center Program awardees reported 17.21 percent of patients with no insurance and 47.92 percent covered by Medicaid or CHIP. The obligation created by the statute is that the centre must serve the residents of its area and must discount charges by ability to pay, not that it must turn away people who can pay.

What does the sliding fee scale actually mean?

Section 330 requires a centre to prepare a schedule of fees designed to cover its reasonable costs, and a corresponding schedule of discounts adjusted on the basis of the patient's ability to pay. In practice that means two documents, not one: a price list, and a discount table keyed to household income and family size. A centre that has the first without the second is not meeting the requirement.

Is a rural health clinic the same as a health center?

No. A rural health clinic is a Medicare and Medicaid payment category, not a grant programme. It has no patient-majority board requirement, no section 330 award and no automatic Federal Tort Claims Act coverage. Medicare pays rural health clinics an all-inclusive per-visit rate subject to a statutory cap that rises to $190 per visit by 2028, while federally qualified health centers are paid under a prospective payment system set at $207.72 per visit for calendar year 2026.

Who can be sued for malpractice at a health center?

At a deemed health center, the United States is substituted as defendant for covered claims. Under the Federally Supported Health Centers Assistance Acts of 1992 and 1995, governing board members, officers, employees and certain individual contractors are treated as employees of the Public Health Service for acts or omissions within the scope of their employment. HRSA states the covered entity or individual is immune and will not be financially liable for claims arising from covered activities.

How do I confirm a centre is federally qualified?

Ask three questions at the front desk and check one federal source. Ask whether the centre is a section 330 grantee or a look-alike, ask to see the sliding fee discount schedule, and ask whether the governing board has a patient majority. Then confirm the site appears in HRSA's own health centre locator. A centre that meets the statute will answer all three without hesitation, because each answer is a condition of its funding.
