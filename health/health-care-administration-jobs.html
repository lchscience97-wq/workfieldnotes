Health Care Administration Jobs: Where They Actually Are

Last verified
5 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Data sources
BLS OOH May 2024; BLS projections 2024–34
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

A job board shows you today's vacancies. It does not show you the occupation. The federal count for medical and health services managers is 616,200 jobs in 2024, projected to reach 759,100 by 2034 — a 23 per cent increase against 3.1 per cent for United States employment as a whole.

Where those jobs sit is the part postings cannot tell you. 29 per cent are in hospitals. The best-paying employer is not a hospital but government, at a May 2024 median of $132,620. And the number that predicts how often a role opens is not the growth rate but the 10.1 per cent annual turnover implied by 62,100 projected openings.

What the job boards can and cannot show

Every page ranking above this one for this search is an aggregator of live postings. That is a genuinely useful product and this page is not a substitute for it. But a posting exists because one employer has one unfilled seat at one moment, which makes the aggregate view systematically skewed in three ways.

It over-represents settings with high turnover, because a seat that empties often is advertised often. It over-represents employers with recruitment budgets and applicant-tracking integrations. And it under-represents every organisation that fills management roles internally — which, in an occupation where BLS records prior healthcare experience as typical, is a large share of them.

There is a fourth distortion worth naming, because it affects pay expectations rather than location. Aggregators derive salary estimates from the postings and submissions they hold, which is a self-selected sample of the labour market rather than a survey of it. The federal wage estimates come from an establishment survey with a defined frame. The two are measuring different populations, so a discrepancy between them is not evidence that one is wrong — it is what you should expect, and the direction of the gap is usually predictable from which settings advertise most.

None of that is visible from inside a search results page. The federal employment distribution is the stable map underneath the churn, and reading the two together tells you considerably more than either alone.

The size and shape of the occupation

BLS records 616,200 jobs for medical and health services managers in 2024, with a projection of 759,100 for 2034. That is a numeric change of 142,900 and a growth rate of 23 per cent, which BLS classifies as much faster than average. Projected annual openings run at about 62,100.

The openings figure is the one worth internalising, because it is not the same as growth. Over a decade, 142,900 new jobs against 621,000 cumulative openings means roughly 77 per cent of the vacancies you will ever see advertised exist because somebody left, retired or moved, not because the post is new. Growth determines whether the field is expanding; turnover determines how often a door opens.

Where the jobs are, against where the money is

BLS publishes two separate industry tables for this occupation — one for share of employment, one for median wage. Almost nobody sets them side by side, and the comparison is the whole point.

Three things jump out. The best-paying industry is the second-smallest employer: government pays the highest median in the list at $132,620 while holding only 7 per cent of the jobs. The largest employer, hospitals, pays nearly as well at $130,690 and holds four times as many posts, which makes it the only cell in the table that is both large and well paid. And nursing and residential care facilities pay the lowest median at $99,250 while employing more people than government does.

The five listed industries add to 66 per cent of employment, which leaves 34 per cent — roughly 209,500 jobs — distributed across settings BLS does not break out: insurers, group practices, home health, public health agencies, vendors and consultancies. That unlisted third is where most of the roles that never reach a public job board sit.

How 23 per cent compares with everything else

Growth figures mean nothing without the baseline. BLS's Employment Projections release of 28 August 2025 projects total United States employment to grow 3.1 per cent from 2024 to 2034, adding 5.2 million jobs, with healthcare and social assistance projected to have the largest job growth of any sector at 8.4 per cent.

Against that, 23 per cent is roughly 7.4 times the all-occupation rate and 2.7 times the rate for the healthcare sector as a whole. In other words, this occupation is not merely riding a growing industry; it is growing considerably faster than the industry it sits inside, which is what you would expect from an administrative layer thickening relative to the operations it manages.

The number that predicts how often a role opens

Projected annual openings divided by current employment gives an annual turnover rate, and it is a far better guide to how frequently you will see a suitable posting than the growth rate is. Three comparable management occupations, on the same 2024–34 basis:

The contrast in the middle row is the useful one. Administrative services and facilities managers is an adjacent occupation with the same bachelor's-degree entry requirement, similar median pay — $108,390 for administrative services managers and $104,690 for facilities managers in May 2024 — and a growth rate of 4 per cent against 23. Two jobs that look interchangeable on a posting have projections nearly six times apart.

The lawyers row sets the floor: at 3.6 per cent turnover, a professional occupation can be large and still advertise rarely. Lawyers outnumber medical and health services managers by 248,600 jobs yet generate roughly half as many openings a year. Headcount does not predict opportunity; churn does.

One caveat on how to use these rates. Annual openings as BLS publishes them include exits from the labour force and transfers to other occupations as well as growth, so the figure describes vacancy creation rather than net hiring, and it says nothing about how many applicants each vacancy attracts. It is a supply-side count. Read alongside the industry table above, its practical use is comparative: it tells you which of two similar-looking occupations puts more doors in front of you per year, not how easy any individual door is to walk through.

What the entry requirement really screens for

BLS records the entry profile as a bachelor's degree, less than 5 years of work experience in a related occupation, and no on-the-job training.

That third item is the screen. An occupation with no structured entry training is one that expects competence on arrival, which is why BLS describes employers as typically wanting prior work in a healthcare setting in either an administrative or a clinical role in a hospital or other healthcare facility, naming medical records specialist, administrative assistant and financial clerk as entry points, and noting that some nursing home administrators advance from registered nurse posts.

The practical reading for anyone planning a route in: the clinical or clerical job you take first is not a detour, it is the qualifying experience. Which degree, if any, to pair it with is a separate question, treated in five paths, one title. One corner of the field is genuinely gated — BLS states that all states require licensure for nursing home administrators, with requirements varying by state.

What the pay distribution looks like

The May 2024 median annual wage is $117,960, or $56.71 an hour. The lowest 10 per cent earned less than $69,680 and the highest 10 per cent more than $219,080.

That is a spread of $149,400 between the two tails, with the median sitting $48,280 above the tenth percentile and $101,120 below the ninetieth. The distribution is right-skewed by a factor of roughly 2.1, which is the arithmetic reason average-based figures on aggregator sites sit above BLS medians and describe fewer people than they appear to. The full picture by setting is in the full salary spread, and the day-to-day content of the role in what the job actually involves.

Reading a posting against the map

Four questions convert the tables above into a decision about a specific advert.

Which industry row is this employer in? The gap between the top and bottom of the five is $33,370 at the median — larger than most of what a posting will attribute to seniority.

Is this a growth seat or a replacement seat? Roughly 77 per cent of openings are replacements. A replacement seat comes with an incumbent's expectations already formed; a growth seat usually comes with an undefined scope.

Does the posting require a licence? Only the nursing home administrator family does, and it is state-specific. Everything else is employer preference.

Where is the employer against the unlisted 34 per cent? If the setting is not one of the five BLS breaks out — an insurer, a public health agency, a vendor — the published industry medians do not describe it, and neither does the aggregator's estimate. If the role touches records, release and compliance, the credential landscape is mapped in roles, pay and credentials, and the patient-facing dispute channels in route before you search.

Questions people actually ask

How many health care administration jobs are there?

BLS counted 616,200 jobs for medical and health services managers in 2024 and projects 759,100 by 2034. That is a numeric change of 142,900 and 23 per cent growth, described by BLS as much faster than average. About 62,100 openings a year are projected over the decade, which includes replacement as well as new positions.

Which employer type has the most of these jobs?

Hospitals, at 29 per cent of the occupation. Offices of physicians follow at 14 per cent, nursing and residential care facilities at 9 per cent, and government and outpatient care centres at 7 per cent each. Those five account for 66 per cent of employment, leaving 34 per cent spread across everything else.

Where does the job pay the most?

Government excluding state and local education and hospitals, at a May 2024 median of $132,620, followed by hospitals at $130,690. The lowest of the five listed industries is nursing and residential care facilities at $99,250. The gap between the top and bottom of that list is $33,370.

Is 23 per cent growth unusually high?

Yes, by a wide margin. BLS projects total United States employment to grow 3.1 per cent between 2024 and 2034, adding 5.2 million jobs, with healthcare and social assistance projected to grow 8.4 per cent. At 23 per cent, this occupation is growing at roughly 7.4 times the all-occupation rate.

How does it compare with administrative services managers?

Both need a bachelor's degree, but the trajectories diverge sharply. Administrative services and facilities managers held 422,600 jobs in 2024 with 4 per cent projected growth and about 36,400 annual openings. Medical and health services managers held 616,200 with 23 per cent growth and about 62,100 openings.

What share of the occupation turns over each year?

About 62,100 projected annual openings against 616,200 jobs works out at roughly 10.1 per cent a year. For administrative services and facilities managers the equivalent is about 8.6 per cent, and for lawyers about 3.6 per cent. That figure is a better guide to how often a role appears than the growth rate is.

Do job boards show the whole market?

They show current postings, which is a different thing from the occupation. A posting exists because a specific employer has an unfilled seat now, so boards over-represent settings with high turnover and active recruitment budgets and under-represent settings that fill roles internally. The federal employment distribution is the stable map underneath.
