The settings that appear for this search are not interchangeable, and only one of them owes you anything by law. Under EMTALA, a Medicare-participating hospital offering emergency services must screen you when you ask for treatment of an emergency condition, and stabilise you if one is found — regardless of ability to pay.

Everything else is a contract or a grant condition. Federally funded health centres must serve everyone, even if they cannot pay, and adjust fees by income; 32.7 million people used them in 2025. Private urgent care and physician offices owe you nothing until they accept you. Sorting your situation into the right row matters more than the distance.

Why the results are all brands

The first page for this phrase is hospital systems and urgent care chains. That is not a ranking anomaly; it is an accurate reflection of who spends money on the query. Health systems have marketing departments and location schema. Federally funded clinics, county programmes and public health departments generally do not.

The consequence is a quiet inversion: the settings that appear first are the ones under the fewest obligations to you, and the settings with statutory or grant-condition duties to serve are the hardest to find. This page does not name or recommend any facility. It sets out what each type of setting must do, so you can search for the right category first and the nearest example second.

What EMTALA actually guarantees

Enacted in 1986, Section 1867 of the Social Security Act imposes obligations on Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services. Those hospitals must provide a medical screening examination (MSE) when a request is made for examination or treatment for an emergency medical condition (EMC), including active labor. Where an emergency condition is found, hospitals are then required to provide stabilizing treatment, and where a hospital is unable to stabilize a patient within its capability, or if the patient requests, an appropriate transfer should be implemented.

Three limits are worth stating plainly, because they are where expectations break.

It is an access rule, not a payment rule. EMTALA governs whether you are screened and stabilised. It says nothing about the bill that follows.

It applies to hospitals that participate in Medicare and offer emergency services. A standalone urgent care clinic is not covered by it, whatever its signage suggests.

The duty attaches to screening and stabilisation. It is not a general right to any treatment you request. How the wider system routes and funds care around that narrow guarantee is set out in how the system actually works.

The safety net that does not advertise

The HRSA Health Center Program is the part of the system that is designed for exactly the situation most people are in when they type this search, and it is close to invisible in the results.

HRSA describes these centres as local clinics that treat people's medical, dental, mental health, substance use, and other health care needs. To receive funding they must have a governing board where a majority of its members are patients, serve everyone, even if they cannot pay, and adjust their fees based on income and family size.

The scale is not marginal. In 2025, more than 32.7 million people used HRSA-funded health centers for care, a group that included 1 in 8 children and 25 million uninsured, Medicaid, and Medicare patients. HRSA also reports that about 90% of these patients had incomes at or below 200% of the federal poverty level.

That last figure is worth converting, because the percentage on its own conceals how far up the income range the sliding scale reaches. Against the 2026 poverty guidelines for the 48 contiguous states and DC — $15,960 for one person and $33,000 for a household of four, adding $5,680 per additional person — 200 per cent works out as follows.

What the visit data says about where care happens

Two federal surveys put the settings in proportion. CDC recorded 155.4 million emergency department visits in 2022 — 47.3 per 100 persons — of which 11.5 per cent resulted in hospital admission. Physician offices recorded about 1.0 billion visits in 2019, or 320.7 per 100 persons, with 50.3 per cent of those going to primary care physicians.

On the per-person rates, office visits outnumber emergency visits by roughly 6.8 to 1. And the admission figure cuts the other way: if 11.5 per cent of emergency visits end in admission, then 88.5 per cent do not, which means the overwhelming majority of emergency department encounters conclude with the patient going home. That is not an argument for avoiding the emergency department when the situation calls for it — it is context for the fact that the setting absorbs a great deal of care that another door could have handled at a different price.

Where the cost actually comes from

For most people the deciding variable is not the facility's list price but whether it is inside their plan's network. HealthCare.gov defines a network as the facilities, providers and suppliers your health insurer or plan has contracted with to provide health care services, and out-of-network coinsurance as the percentage (for example, 40%) you pay of the allowed amount for covered health care services to providers who don't contract with your health insurance or plan.

Emergencies are the exception, and it is a substantial one. Under the No Surprises Act, in force since 2022, out-of-network cost-sharing is banned for most emergency services, even if you get them out-of-network, and you can't be charged more than in-network cost-sharing for these services. The same protection extends to non-emergency services from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities and to services from out-of-network air ambulance service providers.

So the network question bites hardest in the non-emergency rows of the table: urgent care, physician offices and telehealth. What a network is, and why the same phrase means something entirely different when it appears in a clinic's name, is unpicked in two meanings, one search box.

Who is on the other side of the door

One more definitional trap sits underneath this search. The word "provider" in a plan document is not a synonym for "doctor", and the difference determines whether a given facility is covered. Under Medicare's own regulations a hospital is a provider while a physician is a supplier, which is why plan language often says "providers and suppliers" rather than either alone. The full breakdown is in a legal term, not a synonym.

The practical version: when you check whether somewhere is in-network, check the entity you will actually be billed by, which at a hospital is frequently more than one.

Four checks before you go

Is this an emergency? If yes, the screening obligation applies at any Medicare-participating hospital offering emergency services, and the surprise-billing protection covers most emergency services regardless of network status.

Is there a federally funded health centre serving your area? Its sliding fee scale is a grant condition, not a discretionary discount, and the 200 per cent band reaches to $66,000 for a household of four.

Which network is the facility in — and the people inside it? The building and the clinicians billing from it can have different network status.

Is what you need actually clinical? Coverage disputes, records problems and billing errors route to agencies rather than clinics; those channels are mapped in route before you search, with the official federal telephone lines listed in the official lines, by task. For durable medical equipment specifically, the vetting steps are in how to vet a supplier.

Questions people actually ask

Can an emergency department turn me away if I cannot pay?

Not for the screening. Section 1867 of the Social Security Act, enacted in 1986 and known as EMTALA, requires Medicare-participating hospitals offering emergency services to provide a medical screening examination when a request is made for examination or treatment of an emergency medical condition, and to provide stabilising treatment where one is found. The law ensures public access to emergency services regardless of ability to pay.

Does EMTALA mean emergency care is free?

No. It governs whether you are screened and stabilised, not whether you are billed afterwards. It is an access rule, not a payment rule. What it does interact with is surprise billing: the No Surprises Act bans out-of-network cost-sharing for most emergency services, so you cannot be charged more than in-network cost-sharing for them.

What is a federally funded health center?

A local clinic funded under the HRSA Health Center Program. HRSA states that these centres must have a governing board where a majority of members are patients, must serve everyone even if they cannot pay, and must adjust their fees based on income and family size. In 2025 more than 32.7 million people used HRSA-funded health centres.

How busy are emergency departments compared with doctors' offices?

Very differently. CDC recorded 155.4 million emergency department visits in 2022, or 47.3 per 100 persons, with 11.5 per cent resulting in hospital admission. Physician offices recorded about 1.0 billion visits in 2019, or 320.7 per 100 persons. Office visits outnumber emergency visits by roughly 6.8 to 1 on the per-person rates.

Who pays sliding-scale fees at a health center?

HRSA reports that about 90 per cent of health centre patients have incomes at or below 200 per cent of the federal poverty level. Against the 2026 guidelines for the 48 contiguous states, 200 per cent is $31,920 for one person and $66,000 for a household of four, so the scale reaches considerably further up the income range than most people assume.

Why are the search results all hospital brands?

Because hospital systems and urgent care chains buy and optimise for this phrase, and safety-net clinics generally do not. The result over-represents the settings with marketing budgets and under-represents the ones with legal obligations to serve you, which is close to an inversion of what the search is usually for.

What should I check before going anywhere?

Three things: whether the situation is an emergency, in which case the screening obligation applies and the surprise-billing protection covers most emergency services; whether the facility is in your plan's network, since network status drives cost-sharing; and whether a federally funded health centre serves your area, since its sliding fee scale is not conditional on insurance.
