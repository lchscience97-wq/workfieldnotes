Health Care Facilities: The Federal List, and What Binds Each

Last verified
13 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources
CMS; 42 CFR 482, 488, 489
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

Federal law has no category called "health care facility." What it has is a list of certified provider and supplier types, each with its own rule book, its own inspection and its own identifier.

The information is scattered: the enumeration sits on one CMS page, the rule books in different parts of 42 CFR, the survey machinery in Part 488, and the emergency obligation in Part 489. This page puts the structure in one place — how a building becomes federally recognised, who inspects it, what deemed status means, and what certification does and does not tell you.

A folk word and a federal one

In ordinary speech a health care facility is any building where care happens. That usage is fine, and it is what the government's own consumer-facing health information pages use when addressing the public.

In the regulatory sense the word does no work at all. What exists instead is a set of enumerated provider and supplier types, each defined by the Conditions of Participation or Conditions for Coverage written for it. CMS describes these as conditions health care organizations must meet in order to begin and continue participating in the Medicare and Medicaid programs.

That difference explains a common frustration. Asking whether a place is "a licensed facility" produces an answer that means very little, because it merges a state licence, a federal certification and a private accreditation into one word. Asking which provider type it is certified as produces an answer you can look up.

How a building becomes federally recognised

Three things have to line up, and they are separable.

First, a provider agreement with Medicare. Second, compliance with the conditions published for that provider type — hospitals under 42 CFR Part 482, and so on down the list. Third, a survey and certification: 42 CFR 488.1 defines certification as a determination made by the State survey agency that providers and suppliers are in compliance with the applicable Medicare requirements, and defines a full review as a survey for compliance with all of the conditions or requirements applicable.

A state licence is a fourth, separate thing, granted by the state and governing whether the organisation may operate at all. A facility can hold a state licence without federal certification; what it cannot then do is bill Medicare for the services in question.

Who actually inspects

Under 42 CFR 488.11 the State survey agency surveys providers and suppliers for Medicare compliance, conducts validation surveys of accredited providers, certifies its findings to CMS and recommends effective dates for provider agreements. Section 488.12 is the clause people miss: those certifications are recommendations to CMS, which then determines final eligibility and participation status.

So the inspector at the door usually works for the state, but the decision that follows is federal. That two-step is why a survey finding and a change in participation status can be separated by time, and why the presence of inspectors does not by itself tell you an outcome.

Deemed status: when an accreditor stands in

Most large hospitals are not routinely surveyed by the state for Medicare purposes. They are accredited by a national accrediting organisation and deemed to meet the conditions.

42 CFR 488.1 defines deemed status as a determination by CMS resting on four elements: the provider voluntarily applies for accreditation, receives accreditation from an approved organisation, that organisation recommends deeming to CMS, and CMS accepts. The same section defines a national accrediting organisation as one that accredits provider entities under a specific programme and whose accredited entities are widely located geographically across the United States. Under 488.4(a)(2) CMS may then deem the provider or supplier to be in compliance with the applicable conditions — subject to validation surveys under 488.9.

The practical reading: accreditation is voluntary and private, but once CMS approves the programme and deems the provider, it substitutes for the federal survey. The validation survey is the check that keeps the substitution honest.

Two features of that list are worth pausing on. Small clinics appear on it alongside hospitals, each with its own rule book rather than a diluted version of the hospital one — our guide to what the federal health centre test actually is covers that branch. And several entries are not buildings at all: an organ procurement organisation and a portable x-ray supplier are certified operations, which is a reminder that the federal category is about what is done and under what conditions, not about premises.

What a hospital's conditions actually require

42 CFR Part 482 is the longest of these rule books, and reading its section headings gives a fair picture of what certification covers.

Subpart B carries the administrative conditions: §482.11 compliance with federal, state and local laws, §482.12 governing body, §482.13 patient's rights, §482.15 emergency preparedness. Subpart C carries the operational ones: §482.21 quality assessment and performance improvement, §482.22 medical staff, §482.23 nursing services, §482.24 medical record services, §482.25 pharmaceutical services, §482.26 radiologic services, §482.27 laboratory services, §482.30 utilization review, §482.41 physical environment, §482.42 infection prevention and antibiotic stewardship, §482.43 discharge planning, and §482.45 organ, tissue and eye procurement.

Notice what kind of list that is. It is structure and process — who governs, who is credentialed, how records are kept, how infections are controlled, how discharge is planned. It is not a list of clinical outcomes. Certification tells you the machinery was in place when it was inspected. Our guide to who moves your record covers what §482.24 implies for access.

The obligation attached to the emergency department

One condition of participation reaches further than the others, because it applies regardless of payment, insurance or immigration status.

Under 42 CFR 489.24, a hospital with a Medicare provider agreement and a dedicated emergency department must provide an appropriate medical screening examination within the capability of the hospital's emergency department, including ancillary services routinely available to the emergency department, to any individual who comes there. If an emergency medical condition is found, the hospital must provide any necessary stabilizing treatment or an appropriate transfer, and under 489.24(e)(1) it may not transfer the individual unless the transfer is appropriate and one of the stated conditions is met.

The definition of a dedicated emergency department at 489.24(b) is broader than a single room: any department or facility of the hospital, whether on or off the main campus, that meets the licensing, public-holding-out or volume tests. That breadth is deliberate, and it is why the obligation cannot be avoided by relabelling a location.

The number, not the name

Every certified provider and supplier carries a CMS Certification Number, and that number attaches to the certification rather than to the signage. Ownership changes, brand changes and management changes do not automatically change what has been certified.

For anyone checking a facility this is the practical lever. Look the organisation up by its official certified name rather than its trading name, and check the provider type it is certified as. A place presenting itself as a hospital may be certified as an ambulatory surgical centre; a place presenting itself as a nursing home may be certified as a long term care facility with a specific bed configuration. The certified type determines which rule book applies to it.

Checking a facility before you use it

Three questions do most of the work. Which provider type is it certified as? Is it certified through a state survey or deemed through an accrediting organisation? And does it have a dedicated emergency department, which decides whether the 489.24 obligations attach?

The answers change what you can expect. A certified hospital with an emergency department owes you a screening examination whatever your coverage. An ambulatory surgical centre does not. A federally qualified health centre owes you a sliding fee discount. A private urgent care clinic outside all of these owes you nothing beyond its own posted policy — a distinction our guide to the four legal forms of the word clinic works through.

What certification does not tell you

It does not tell you the facility is good. It tells you that on the day it was surveyed, the applicable conditions were met — a floor, and a structural one at that.

It does not tell you what a visit will cost, which is governed by coverage rather than by certification. It does not tell you whether the specific service you need is offered at the specific site you are looking at, because certification attaches to the certified operation rather than to every activity in the building. And it says nothing about staffing on the day you arrive.

Used narrowly, though, the category is genuinely useful. It tells you which of twenty rule books applies to the organisation in front of you, which federal obligations attach automatically, and where to look when something goes wrong. That is more than the word facility has ever conveyed on its own.

Questions people actually ask

What makes something a health care facility federally?

Federal law does not use the word facility as a category. What it recognises is a certified provider or supplier: an organisation that has a Medicare provider agreement, meets the Conditions of Participation or Conditions for Coverage written for its type, and has been surveyed and certified. CMS states these are conditions health care organizations must meet in order to begin and continue participating in the Medicare and Medicaid programs.

Who actually inspects a health care facility?

In the first instance the State survey agency. Under 42 CFR 488.11 the State agency surveys providers and suppliers for Medicare compliance, conducts validation surveys of accredited providers, certifies its findings to CMS and recommends effective dates for provider agreements. Section 488.12 makes clear that these certifications are recommendations to CMS, which makes the final determination on eligibility and participation.

What is deemed status?

It is a determination by CMS that a provider accredited by an approved national accrediting organization may be treated as meeting the Medicare conditions. 42 CFR 488.1 defines it around four elements: the provider applies voluntarily for accreditation, receives accreditation from an approved organization, that organization recommends deeming to CMS, and CMS accepts. Under 488.4(a)(2) CMS may then deem the provider to be in compliance, still subject to validation surveys.

Do all hospitals have to treat emergencies?

A hospital with a Medicare provider agreement and a dedicated emergency department must, under 42 CFR 489.24, provide an appropriate medical screening examination within the capability of its emergency department to any individual who comes there. If an emergency medical condition is found, it must provide necessary stabilizing treatment or an appropriate transfer, and it may not transfer the individual unless the specific conditions in 489.24(e)(1) are met.

Are federally qualified health centers and rural health clinics facilities?

Yes, in the sense that matters: both appear on CMS's enumeration of provider and supplier types with published conditions. That places a small clinic in the same certification framework as a hospital, with its own rule book rather than a lesser version of the hospital one. The scale differs enormously; the structure does not.

What is a CMS Certification Number?

It is the identifier CMS assigns to a certified provider or supplier, and it is tied to the certification rather than to the building or the brand. That is why a change of ownership or a rebrand does not necessarily change what has been certified, and why looking a facility up by its official name rather than its trading name is the reliable route.

Does certification mean a facility is good?

No. Certification means a facility met the applicable conditions at the time it was surveyed, which is a floor rather than a ranking. The conditions cover structure and process — governing body, medical staff organisation, nursing services, medical records, infection prevention, discharge planning — not outcomes for your particular condition. Quality comparison is a separate exercise from certification status.
