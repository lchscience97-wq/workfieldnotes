This number fails for a documentable reason. The City names the same network two ways in two of its own documents: the public roster lists Health Centers 1 to 6 and 10 plus two centres named rather than numbered, while its federally qualified health centre handout refers to AHS Health Centers numbered 1 through 10.

Behind that naming sits something worth more than the number: these centres are 340B covered entities. HRSA requires manufacturers to supply them outpatient drugs at significantly reduced prices. What that does and does not mean for your prescription is the substance of this page.

Two documents, two naming schemes

The City of Philadelphia publishes its health centres in more than one place, and the places do not agree on how to name them.

The public roster of City health centers lists sites numbered 1 through 6 and 10, alongside the Mattie L. Humphrey Health Center on E. Chelten Ave. and the Strawberry Mansion Health Center on West Dauphin St. — two centres identified by name rather than by number.

The Department of Public Health's Find a Clinic handout, listing federally qualified health centers in Philadelphia, refers instead to AHS Health Centers numbered 1 through 10 along with the Strawberry Mansion Health Center.

Both documents are the City's own and both are current. The consequence for anyone searching a number in the gap is immediate: the query matches no City page, so directories, review sites and social listings fill the results instead. That is not a search failure; it is a naming mismatch surfacing.

Finding the centre anyway

The reliable identifiers are the street address and the printed name, both of which the City publishes for every site with telephone numbers alongside. Our guide to the full City roster in one place reproduces it.

If you were given a number that does not appear, do not keep searching it. Call any listed centre and ask to be routed — the City's own instruction before any visit is to call ahead to make sure the service you need is available when you plan to visit, and that call answers the identification question at the same time. Our guide to which operator, and which privacy rule covers the same problem where the operator, rather than the number, is what is unclear.

What 340B is

Now the part that is worth more than the address.

The 340B Drug Pricing Program requires manufacturers participating in Medicaid to provide outpatient drugs to covered entities at significantly reduced prices. HRSA states its purpose directly: the programme enables covered entities to stretch scarce federal resources as far as possible, reaching more eligible patients and providing more comprehensive services.

The entities that qualify are listed in section 340B(a)(4) of the Public Health Service Act, and HRSA names them as including HRSA-supported health centers and look-alikes, Ryan White clinics, State AIDS Drug Assistance programmes, Medicare and Medicaid disproportionate share hospitals, children's hospitals and other safety net providers.

So a federally qualified health centre — which the City's own handout says its centres are — sits inside a programme that changes what it pays for medicines. Our guide to what the federal test actually is sets out how that designation works.

What it does and does not reach

This is the distinction that most discussion of 340B loses, and it matters at the counter.

340B governs the price at which the covered entity buys the drug. It does not set the price at which a patient is charged. The programme creates room; it does not dictate how the room is used.

What HRSA says the room is for is explicit — stretching scarce federal resources to reach more patients and provide more comprehensive services. In practice an entity may direct the savings into wider services, into lower charges, or into both, and the mix is the entity's decision.

For a patient at a health centre, that converts into one useful question rather than an assumption: does this centre pass 340B pricing through to patients, and under what policy? A centre with a pharmacy will have an answer. So will one that contracts pharmacy services out.

There is a second reason to ask rather than assume, and it is the sliding fee schedule. A federally qualified health centre already owes every patient a schedule of fees with a corresponding schedule of discounts adjusted on the basis of ability to pay, under 42 U.S.C. §254b(k)(3)(G). That obligation exists whether or not any 340B savings are passed through, and for a low-income patient it may do more work than the drug pricing programme does. Asking about both, in the same conversation, is how you find out which one is actually setting your price.

The patient requirement

One condition constrains the whole programme, and HRSA states it plainly: a person receiving 340B drugs must be a patient of the covered entity.

That is a programme integrity rule rather than an access rule, and it explains a situation people meet without understanding it. A prescription written by an unaffiliated clinician is not automatically fillable at 340B pricing by a centre you have not established care with. Becoming a patient of the centre is the step that opens it, which is the same registered patient question the City's roster raises for services generally.

Eligibility is checked on a clock

Participation is not a permanent badge. Registration runs quarterly, and the windows are fixed.

The lag between window and purchasing — a full quarter in each case — is the operational fact behind that table. A newly eligible site does not begin buying at 340B prices the day it qualifies, and an organisation adding a location plans around that calendar.

Why this belongs to the number question

The two halves of this page are connected, and the connection is practical rather than rhetorical.

340B status attaches to a covered entity and its registered sites, not to a building someone can identify by a number that appears in one City document and not another. Getting the identification right — the operating organisation, the specific site, the name it is actually listed under — is what lets you ask the pricing question of the right party.

It is also why the scope machinery matters here. As our guide to why services vary by building explains, a health centre's approved sites are recorded separately from its approved services, and site-level status is the level at which most practical questions resolve.

What to ask, in order

Which organisation operates this centre, and what is this site's listed name and address? Is the organisation a federally qualified health centre? Is it a 340B covered entity, and does this site participate? Does it apply 340B pricing to patient charges, and under what policy? And do I need to be a registered patient before a prescription can be filled here?

Five questions, all factual, none of which requires the number you started with. That is the honest resolution of a search that cannot succeed on its own terms.

What this does not settle

It does not tell you the price of a specific medicine, which depends on the drug, the entity's policy, your coverage and your income. It does not guarantee a centre operates a pharmacy at all.

And it does not resolve the numbering, because only the City can do that. What it does is replace a failed query with a set of answerable ones, and attach to them the federal programme that makes a health centre's pharmacy economics different from a private practice's. That difference is the reason the question was worth asking in the first place, even when the number that prompted it does not exist on the page you expected.

Questions people actually ask

Why does searching this number fail?

Because the City names the same network two ways in two documents. Its public roster of City health centers lists numbers 1 through 6 and 10 alongside two centres named rather than numbered, while its Find a Clinic handout on federally qualified health centers refers to AHS Health Centers numbered 1 through 10. A number that appears in one document and not the other returns third-party directories rather than a City page.

How do I find the right centre?

Search by street address or by the name printed on the roster rather than by the number. The City publishes each site with its address and telephone numbers, and the addresses are stable in a way the numbering is not. If you were given a number that does not appear, calling the nearest listed centre and asking to be routed is faster than searching again.

What is 340B?

A federal drug pricing programme. HRSA describes it as requiring manufacturers participating in Medicaid to provide outpatient drugs to covered entities at significantly reduced prices, and states that the programme enables covered entities to stretch scarce federal resources as far as possible, reaching more eligible patients and providing more comprehensive services. The covered entities are listed at section 340B(a)(4) of the Public Health Service Act.

Do health centres qualify for 340B?

Yes. HRSA lists HRSA-supported health centers and look-alikes among the beneficiaries of the programme, alongside Ryan White clinics, State AIDS Drug Assistance programmes, Medicare and Medicaid disproportionate share hospitals, children's hospitals and other safety net providers. Eligible organisations must register, obtain a 340B identification number and comply with programme requirements.

Does 340B mean my prescription is cheaper?

Not automatically, and this is the most misunderstood part. 340B governs the price at which the covered entity buys the drug, not the price at which you are charged. What it creates is room — savings the entity may use to widen services or reduce what patients pay. Whether any of that reaches your counter depends on the entity's own policies, which is a question to ask directly.

Who counts as a patient for 340B purposes?

HRSA states plainly that a person receiving 340B drugs must be a patient of the covered entity. That is a programme integrity requirement rather than an access rule, and it is one reason a health centre may be unable to fill a prescription written elsewhere at its 340B price.

How often is eligibility checked?

Continuously and formally. Registration runs on a quarterly cycle: registering between 1 and 15 January starts purchasing on 1 April, April registration starts on 1 July, July on 1 October and October on 1 January. Entities must recertify their eligibility every year and must notify the Office of Pharmacy Affairs when there is a change in their eligibility.
