Health Care Equipment Near Me: How to Vet a Supplier

This page does not rank shops. It gives you the yardstick the federal government already uses, so you can measure any of them yourself.

A supplier enrolled in Medicare has agreed to thirty numbered standards at 42 CFR 424.57(c). Several are physically observable from the pavement: a permanent location of at least 200 square feet, open to the public a minimum of 30 hours a week, carrying liability cover of at least $300,000, separately accredited, and backed by a $50,000 surety bond per National Provider Identifier. A shop that fails the visible ones is unlikely to be passing the invisible ones.

What the rules actually mean by equipment

Medicare's category is durable medical equipment, and its definition is narrower than the everyday phrase. To qualify, an item must be durable enough to withstand repeated use, used for a medical reason, typically only useful to someone who is sick or injured, used in your home, and expected to last at least three years.

That five-part test does real work. It is why walkers, wheelchairs, hospital beds, oxygen equipment and blood sugar monitors sit inside the category while most single-use supplies sit outside it under different rules and different payment. When a shop tells you an item "is covered", the first question is which category it is being billed under, because the coverage rules, the paperwork and your share of the cost all change with it.

A separate point that catches people out: the equipment must be for use in your home. Items intended for use only outside the home fall outside the definition regardless of medical necessity.

The standards a supplier has already agreed to

Competitor pages for this search are almost entirely shop listings and review aggregators. None of them tell you that a federal rulebook already exists and that most of it is checkable by an ordinary customer. Below, the standards are paired with what each one looks like from outside.

Four categories are exempt from the bond: government-operated suppliers already covered by a comparable state bond; state-licensed orthotic and prosthetic personnel in sole private practice; physicians and non-physician practitioners supplying only their own patients; and physical or occupational therapists in sole private practice supplying only their own patients. If a shop claims an exemption, it should be able to say which one.

A ten-minute check, in order

One. Confirm enrolment through Medicare's own supplier directory rather than the shop's claim about itself. The tool is the official route and it is free.

Two. Ask a single question on the phone: "Do you accept assignment on this item?" The answer changes your bill materially, and it is explained in the next section.

Three. Visit the address. The 200-square-foot and 30-hour standards exist precisely because equipment fraud historically operated from addresses that could not meet them.

Four. Ask for the NPI, the accreditor's name, and the certificate of liability insurance. Three routine requests; a compliant supplier answers all three without friction.

Five. Get the return, repair and replacement terms in writing before any signature, and keep the proof of delivery you are given.

Assignment: the word that decides your bill

Medicare draws a line between suppliers that participate and accept assignment and those that do not. A supplier that participates and accepts assignment can charge you only the coinsurance and the Part B deductible calculated on the Medicare-approved amount. A supplier that does not may charge more than the approved amount, and may require you to pay the entire cost up front and claim reimbursement afterwards.

Both your doctor and your supplier must be enrolled in Medicare for the claim to work at all. That is two checks, not one, and the doctor half is the one people forget.

What the 2026 numbers work out to

The rule is simple; the arithmetic is where people are surprised. For 2026 the standard Part B premium is $202.90 a month, which is $2,434.80 over a year, and the Part B annual deductible is $283. Equipment coinsurance is 20 per cent of the Medicare-approved amount.

The gap between the first two rows is $226.40 on the same item, decided entirely by the month in which you buy. The gap between the second and third is not calculable in advance, which is the point of asking about assignment before anything else.

Rent, buy, or neither

Medicare covers some equipment by rental, some by purchase, and for some categories lets you choose. Certain rented items become your property once you have made a set number of rental payments. Which route applies is a function of the item's category, not of the supplier's preference.

That matters commercially. A rental stream and a one-off sale have very different value to a shop, so a supplier steering you firmly toward one is worth a direct question: is this item classified as rental-only, purchase-only, or a choice? If the answer is a choice, ask what happens at the end of the rental sequence.

Why most unsolicited equipment calls break a rule

Supplier standard 11 states that a supplier must not contact a beneficiary by telephone unless the beneficiary has given written permission, the contact concerns coordinating delivery of an item already ordered, or the supplier has furnished the beneficiary an item during the previous fifteen months.

Medicare puts the consumer-facing version more bluntly: it will never call you to sell you anything or visit you at your home. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on government impersonation runs parallel — agencies do not call, email, text or message you on social media to ask for money or personal information, do not demand payment by wire transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency or payment app, and do not threaten arrest or suspension of benefits. Its shortest instruction is the most useful one: don't trust your caller ID.

The HHS Office of Inspector General has issued its own alert about scammers spoofing its numbers, and its advice inverts the usual instinct: do not verify your name or any other personal information to an unexpected caller. Hang up and dial a number you looked up yourself. The same principle applies to anyone claiming to be from the health insurance marketplace — the official contact lines are published and finite, and checking a caller against them takes under a minute.

Where to report it

Four routes, depending on what the problem is.

1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227), TTY 1-877-486-2048, available 24 hours a day, seven days a week except some federal holidays, for anything to do with a Medicare claim or a suspicious Medicare bill.

HHS Office of Inspector General hotline: 1-800-HHS-TIPS (1-800-447-8477), TTY 1-800-377-4950, fax 1-800-223-8164, for suspected fraud against federal health programmes.

I-MEDIC: 1-877-7SAFERX (1-877-772-3379) for Medicare Advantage and Part D matters specifically.

ReportFraud.ftc.gov for consumer complaints that are not about a federal health programme, plus your state attorney general.

The single most effective habit is unglamorous: compare the dates and services on your own calendar against your Medicare statements. Equipment billed but never delivered is one of the more common patterns, and it is invisible unless someone reconciles the two.

If you are not on Medicare

The standards above are Medicare enrolment conditions, so they do not bind a shop that serves only cash and commercial customers. They remain the best available proxy, because a supplier that meets them is demonstrably running a permanent, insured, accredited, bonded business, and one that cannot is asking you to take more on trust.

For commercially insured buyers there is a second question underneath the first: whether your plan is administered by an issuer or by a third-party administrator on behalf of your employer, since that determines the coverage rules and the appeal route for a denial. That distinction is set out in our guide to who pays your claim, and the wider structure it sits inside is covered in how US health care actually works.

Questions people actually ask

How do I know if a medical equipment supplier near me is legitimate?

Check it against the standards a Medicare-enrolled supplier has already agreed to at 42 CFR 424.57(c): a permanent location of at least 200 square feet that is open to the public a minimum of 30 hours a week, comprehensive liability insurance of at least $300,000, separate accreditation by a CMS-approved organisation, and a $50,000 surety bond per National Provider Identifier. Confirm enrolment using the official Medicare supplier directory rather than the shop's own claim.

What counts as durable medical equipment?

Medicare defines it as equipment that is durable enough to withstand repeated use, used for a medical reason, typically only useful to someone who is sick or injured, used in your home, and expected to last at least three years. Items that fail any of those tests, such as most disposable supplies, are handled under different rules.

How much does Medicare pay for durable medical equipment in 2026?

After you meet the Part B deductible, which is $283 in 2026, you pay 20 per cent of the Medicare-approved amount and Medicare pays the other 80 per cent, provided the supplier accepts assignment. On an item with an approved amount of $1,200, someone who has not yet met the deductible pays $466.40 and Medicare pays $733.60.

What does it mean when a supplier accepts assignment?

A supplier that participates in Medicare and accepts assignment can charge you only the coinsurance and the Part B deductible on the Medicare-approved amount. A supplier that does not accept assignment may charge more than the approved amount, and may require you to pay the whole cost up front and wait for reimbursement.

Is it legal for an equipment supplier to call me out of the blue?

Generally no. Supplier standard 11 at 42 CFR 424.57(c) states that a supplier must not contact a beneficiary by telephone unless the beneficiary has given written permission, the call concerns coordinating delivery of an already ordered item, or the supplier has furnished the beneficiary an item within the previous 15 months. Medicare also states plainly that it will never call to sell you anything or visit your home.

Do I rent or buy equipment on Medicare?

It depends on the item. Medicare covers some equipment by rental only, some by purchase, and for some categories you may choose. Some rented items become your property after a set number of rental payments. The decision is set by the item's category rather than by the supplier, which is why a supplier pushing you toward one route is worth questioning.

Where do I report a medical equipment supplier for fraud?

Call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227), TTY 1-877-486-2048, or contact the HHS Office of Inspector General hotline on 1-800-HHS-TIPS (1-800-447-8477), TTY 1-800-377-4950. For a Medicare Advantage or Part D issue, the I-MEDIC hotline is 1-877-7SAFERX (1-877-772-3379). Non-Medicare consumer complaints go to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
