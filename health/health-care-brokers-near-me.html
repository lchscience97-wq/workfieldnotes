Health Care Brokers Near Me: Who May Recommend a Plan

Three different kinds of helper can sit down with you and your application, and federal rules give each of them a different job. Search results mix them together with directory listings, which is exactly the confusion worth clearing.

A broker is paid by issuers and may recommend a specific plan. A navigator may not take issuer money at all and is bound to be impartial. A certified application counsellor sits closer to the navigator and may not charge you a fee. Knowing which one is in front of you tells you what their advice is and is not.

What the search is really asking

Nearly everyone typing this is asking one of two questions: who can help me choose coverage, or who can help me finish an application I have got stuck in. Those are different tasks, and the three categories below are not equally suited to them.

The results are dominated by a government enrollment site, a general business directory and several agency sites. That mixture is the problem in miniature: a directory listing tells you a business exists and is nearby; it tells you nothing about which of the three roles that business performs, or what rules bind it.

What a broker must do before touching your application

Agents and brokers who assist with enrollment through an Exchange are not simply salespeople with a licence. 45 CFR 155.220(d) requires them to register with the Exchange in advance of assisting, complete training on qualified health plan options and insurance affordability programmes, and execute a signed agreement complying with the Exchange's privacy and security standards.

Conduct standards follow at 155.220(j)(2), which requires them to provide consumers with correct information, without omission of material fact, and to refrain from conduct that is misleading or that discriminates on the basis of race, colour, national origin, disability, age or sex.

The consent rule most people do not know

Two paragraphs of the same rule create rights that are useful precisely when something has gone wrong.

Under 155.220(j)(2)(iii), an agent or broker must obtain documented consent prior to assisting with or facilitating enrollment, and that consent must specify its scope, purpose and duration. Under (j)(2)(ii) they must document that eligibility application information has been reviewed by and confirmed to be accurate by the consumer, and must maintain that documentation for ten years.

The practical consequence: if an application was submitted, altered or switched without your confirmation, there is a record that should exist and can be asked for. The absence of it is itself informative. This is the single most useful thing in this area that almost nobody knows.

The helper who cannot take issuer money

Navigators are a different instrument with a different constraint written into the rule.

Under 45 CFR 155.210(e)(2) a navigator must provide information and services in a fair, accurate, and impartial manner, and under (e)(3) must facilitate selection of a QHP. They must also maintain expertise in eligibility, enrollment and programme specifications under (e)(1), and provide information in a manner that is culturally and linguistically appropriate to the population served under (e)(5).

The conflict-of-interest standards at 155.210(d) are the structural part. A navigator may not be a health insurance issuer or issuer of stop loss insurance, may not be a subsidiary of one, and may not receive any consideration directly or indirectly from any health insurance issuer in connection with enrollment.

Certified application counsellors

The third category is designated rather than granted: an Exchange may designate organisations to certify staff or volunteers, or may certify individuals directly, under 45 CFR 155.225.

Counsellors must complete Exchange-approved training on qualified health plan options, insurance affordability programmes, eligibility and benefits rules, must pass certification examinations before functioning, and must recertify annually. Their duties include providing information about the full range of QHP options and insurance affordability programmes for which a person is eligible, and assisting with application and enrollment.

Their restrictions mirror the navigator's in substance: disclose relationships with QHPs or affordability programmes, take no consideration from issuers in connection with enrollment, charge applicants no fee, and act in the best interest of the applicants assisted. In federally facilitated Exchanges they also may not be paid per application or per enrollment.

Who may actually recommend

This is the row that decides which one you want. A broker may say take this plan. A navigator is bound to impartiality, so their function is to make sure you understand the options and complete the application correctly, not to pick for you. A counsellor is required to present the full range for which you are eligible.

Neither posture is superior. A recommendation from someone paid by one side is still useful if you know it is a recommendation from someone paid by one side. Impartial help that stops short of a recommendation is useful if what you needed was clarity rather than a decision. The failure mode is mistaking one for the other.

Why a broker's list is a subset

A broker can only place business with issuers they hold an appointment with. So the menu you are shown is a subset of the market, and two brokers a mile apart can honestly present different best options because their subsets differ.

Federal rules address the ordering problem rather than the subset problem. For web-brokers, 45 CFR 155.220(c)(3)(i) requires display of all QHP data provided by the Exchange, prohibits financial incentives such as rebates or giveaways, requires that plan placement not be based on compensation received from issuers, and requires a clear explanation of the rationale for recommendations to be prominently displayed. None of that obliges any broker to represent every issuer.

The question that resolves it is blunt and fair: which issuers are you appointed with, and which are you not? Our guide to who pays your claim covers the companies behind those appointments, and which regulator is yours covers what happens after you enrol.

Choosing between them

If you want someone to narrow a field and say what they would do, a broker is the instrument, with the appointment question asked first. If you want unbiased help completing an application, checking eligibility for financial help, or untangling a rejected submission, a navigator or counsellor is the instrument and costs nothing.

If you are in the middle of an appeal or a coverage dispute rather than an enrollment, none of the three is the right tool — those run on separate deadlines and processes. And if the coverage question is really an access question, our guides to six routes and their clocks and what the federal health centre test is may be the faster route.

What none of them can do

None can change your eligibility, because eligibility is determined by rules rather than by advocacy. None can create a special enrollment period that the facts do not support. None can make an issuer offer a plan in a county where it does not operate, or add a provider to a network.

And none of them is your regulator. If something goes wrong with a broker's conduct, the route is the state insurance department that licensed them and the Exchange they registered with — the same two bodies whose rules are quoted above. Knowing that before you start is worth more than knowing which office is closest.

Questions people actually ask

Does a broker cost me anything?

Not directly. Agents and brokers assisting with Marketplace enrollment are compensated by issuers rather than by consumers. Certified application counselors are separately barred from charging applicants a fee for assistance under 45 CFR 155.225. The cost is not a fee you pay; it is that a broker's compensation comes from the companies whose plans they sell.

What must a broker do before touching my application?

Under 45 CFR 155.220(d) an agent, broker or web-broker must register with the Exchange in advance of assisting, complete training on qualified health plan options and insurance affordability programmes, and execute a signed agreement complying with the Exchange's privacy and security standards. Under 155.220(j)(2)(iii) they must also obtain documented consent from you before assisting with or facilitating enrollment, specifying the scope, purpose and duration of that consent.

Can a navigator tell me which plan to pick?

A navigator must provide information and services in a fair, accurate and impartial manner under 45 CFR 155.210(e)(2), and must facilitate selection of a qualified health plan under (e)(3). Impartiality is the operative constraint: a navigator helps you compare and enroll rather than steering you to a particular product, which is a different service from a broker's recommendation.

Can a navigator be paid by an insurance company?

No. Under 45 CFR 155.210(d) a navigator may not be a health insurance issuer or an issuer of stop loss insurance, may not be a subsidiary of one, and may not receive any consideration directly or indirectly from any health insurance issuer in connection with enrollment. That prohibition is the structural difference between a navigator and a broker.

What is a certified application counselor?

A third category, designated by the Exchange or by an Exchange-designated organisation. Under 45 CFR 155.225 counselors must complete Exchange-approved training and pass certification examinations before functioning, and must recertify annually. They must disclose relationships with qualified health plans or insurance affordability programmes, must not receive consideration from issuers in connection with enrollment, must not charge applicants a fee, and must act in the best interest of the applicants they assist.

Why can two brokers show me different plans?

Because a broker can only sell plans from issuers they have an appointment with, so the set of options each one presents is a subset of the market rather than the whole of it. Federal rules address the ordering problem for web-brokers — under 45 CFR 155.220(c)(3)(i) plan placement must not be based on compensation received from issuers, and the rationale for recommendations must be prominently displayed — but they do not require any broker to carry every issuer.

How long is my information kept?

Under 45 CFR 155.220(j)(2)(ii) an agent or broker must document that eligibility application information has been reviewed by and confirmed to be accurate by the consumer, and must maintain that documentation for ten years. That record exists partly for your benefit: if an application was submitted or changed without your confirmation, the absence of the documentation is itself evidence.
