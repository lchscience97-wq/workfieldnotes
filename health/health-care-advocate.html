Health Care Advocate: What the Free Ones May Not Be

Last verified
26 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources

No federal rule licenses a private patient advocate, sets a scope of practice for one, or limits who may pay them. The advocates federal law does define are the free ones, and it defines them almost entirely by prohibition.

For the long-term care ombudsman programme the count is exact: fifteen disqualifying conditions for the organisation that houses the office, and ten for the individual doing the work. Twenty-five named ways of being the wrong person for the job. Read as a list of what to ask a private advocate, it is the most useful document in this whole subject.

Where the word lands

The term is doing three jobs at once. It names a paid service you can hire, it names several free programmes created by statute, and it appears in the trading name of large hospital systems. None of the three is a credential.

Rather than sort the taxonomy, this guide follows the only part of it that federal text actually specifies. The statutory advocates come with a definition, and the definition is unusual: it says far more about what the advocate may not be than about what the advocate must do.

The advocate the law does define

45 CFR 1324.11(a) requires an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman, headed by a State Long-Term Care Ombudsman. Its representatives are the people a resident of a nursing home or similar facility actually meets.

Their central duty is at 45 CFR 1324.19(b)(1). A representative must investigate a complaint, including but not limited to a complaint related to abuse, neglect, or exploitation, for the purposes of resolving the complaint to the resident's satisfaction and of protecting the health, welfare, and rights of the resident. Note the standard: to the resident's satisfaction, not to the agency's.

Two structural powers make that possible. 1324.11(e)(1) provides access to enter all long-term care facilities at any time during a facility's regular business hours, which no private advocate has. And 1324.19(a)(3) requires that residents have regular and timely access to the programme and receive timely responses to requests for information and complaints.

Above the individual case sits a policy function. 1324.11(e)(2) requires the Ombudsman to analyze, comment on, and monitor the development and implementation of Federal, State, and local laws, regulations, and other government policies affecting long-term care. The same office handles one resident's laundry complaint and the state's licensing rule.

The resident decides, not the advocate

Under 1324.19(b)(2)(ii) the representative must discuss the complaint with the resident (and, if the resident is unable to communicate informed consent, the resident's representative) and must request informed consent before investigating. Consent, not suspicion, starts the work.

Confidentiality follows the same logic. Under 1324.11(e)(3), files, records and information maintained by the programme may be disclosed only at the discretion of the Ombudsman, subject to the consent requirements and to court order. The resident's account does not travel to a regulator by default.

This is what makes the word advocate a term of art rather than a compliment. A surveyor acts because the state has an interest. An advocate acts because the resident said so, and stops when the resident says stop. Everything in the next two sections exists to keep that relationship from being quietly bought.

Fifteen things the office may not be

45 CFR 1324.21(a) lists organisational conflicts. The office may not sit inside an entity that is responsible for licensing, surveying, or certifying long-term care services, that is an association (or an affiliate of such an association) of long-term care facilities, that has any ownership or investment interest in a facility, or whose governing board members hold such interests.

Nor inside one that provides long-term care to residents of long-term care facilities, provides long-term care services under Medicaid programmes, provides long-term care coordination or case management, sets reimbursement rates for facilities or for services, or provides adult protective services.

Nor inside one responsible for Medicaid or public benefit eligibility determinations, one that conducts preadmission screening for long-term care facility admission, one that makes decisions regarding admission or discharge, or one that provides guardianship, conservatorship or other fiduciary services.

Read that list backwards and it is a roster of everyone else in long-term care: the licensor, the trade association, the owner, the operator, the case manager, the rate-setter, the protective services agency, the eligibility office, the admissions screener, the guardian. The rule defines the advocate as the one party at the table holding none of those interests. That is the whole design.

Ten more for the person

1324.21(c)(2) applies the same method to individuals. The listed conflicts include direct involvement in the licensing or certification of a long-term care facility; ownership, operational or investment interest; employment of an individual by, or participation in the management of a facility; and receipt of, or a right to receive, directly or indirectly, remuneration in cash or in kind under a compensation arrangement with facility owners or operators.

It continues with accepting gifts or gratuities of significant value from a facility; accepting money or other consideration from anyone other than the employing agency for performing the work; serving as guardian, conservator or in another fiduciary or surrogate capacity; serving residents of a facility where an immediate family member resides; and holding management responsibility for, or working under the supervision of, an adult protective services programme.

Paragraph (d) adds a cooling-off period that is rare in this field: the programme may not hire someone employed by a long-term care facility within the previous twelve months. Where the conflict is financial, divestment within a reasonable period may be treated as an adequate remedy, which tells you the rule is about live interests rather than past sins.

Fifteen organisational conditions and ten individual ones make twenty-five named disqualifications for a job whose affirmative duties fit in a paragraph. The proportion is the point. Impartiality here is not an aspiration in a mission statement; it is an enumerated list with a hiring bar attached.

A second free advocate, for a different population

The same architecture appears in Medicare. Under 42 U.S.C. 1395b-4(a) the Secretary makes grants to states for the purpose of providing information, counseling, and assistance relating to the procurement of adequate and appropriate health insurance coverage to individuals who are eligible to receive benefits under this subchapter.

The conflict clause is at (b)(2)(D): a state must provide assurances that staff members (including volunteer staff members) of the health insurance information, counseling, and assistance program have no conflict of interest in providing the counseling. The parenthesis matters. Unpaid volunteers are inside the rule, because the risk being managed is the incentive, not the salary.

Marketplace navigators are built the same way. Under 45 CFR 155.210(d)(4) a navigator may not receive any consideration directly or indirectly from any health insurance issuer in connection with enrolment. The contrast with a commissioned intermediary is covered in our guide to how brokers are paid, and what that forbids, and the question of who may lawfully recommend a plan in who may recommend a plan.

What you are buying when you hire one

A private advocate can be excellent. What they cannot be is defined by regulation. There is no federal licence, no scope of practice, no bar on who may pay them and no disclosure duty. Voluntary certifications exist and may say something real about training, but they grant no access rights and no standing.

So borrow the list. Ask who pays you besides me. Ask whether you hold any ownership, investment or employment interest in a facility, agency or insurer you might refer me to. Ask whether you have worked for one recently, since the statutory programme treats twelve months as the relevant window. Ask whether you accept gifts, gratuities or referral fees from anyone in the chain. Ask whether you would ever act as my guardian or fiduciary. Ask who holds my records and on whose consent they are released.

Two practical limits are worth stating plainly. A private advocate has no right to enter a facility on your behalf, and no automatic standing over your medical record: access flows from your own right of access or from a written authorisation naming them, which is the mechanism described in our guide to patient portals and your right of access. Neither point is a criticism. They are the reason the free statutory advocates were created with powers attached.

When the word is a company name

Several large hospital systems carry the word in their trading name. That is branding, and it carries no regulatory meaning whatever. A system that treats patients is a provider, in the sense set out in our guide to a legal term, not a synonym, and what a provider is bound by is covered in the federal list, and what binds each.

The regulation makes the distinction unusually cleanly. Providing long-term care to residents is one of the fifteen conditions that disqualifies an organisation from housing the statutory advocate. In the one place federal text weighs the two senses of the word against each other, being a care provider is the reason you cannot be the advocate. Nothing here rates or recommends any system.

What an advocate cannot do

No advocate licenses, fines or closes a facility; those powers sit with the survey and certification agencies the conflict rule keeps at arm's length. None can order a plan to pay a claim, which runs through appeal procedures instead — the routes summarised in what comes after the call.

None can practise law, and where the question is a contract, an estate or a guardianship, the distinctions in which kind you actually need apply. And an advocate cannot override the person they act for. That constraint is the source of the authority, not a gap in it.

What the statutory advocate has instead is access, consent-bound confidentiality, a duty to answer, a channel into policy, and twenty-five reasons to believe nobody else is paying them. When you hire privately, you are buying the service without the guarantees — which is worth doing with the list in your hand.

Questions people actually ask

Is a patient advocate licensed?

No federal rule licenses private patient advocates, sets a scope of practice for them or restricts who may take the payments. Voluntary private certifications exist, but they confer no legal authority and no right of access to a facility or a record. The advocates federal law does define are the free statutory ones, and it defines them mainly by listing what they may not be.

Who is the long-term care ombudsman?

A free statutory advocate for residents of long-term care facilities. 45 CFR 1324.11 establishes an Office of the State Long-Term Care Ombudsman, and 45 CFR 1324.19 sets the duties of its representatives, who must investigate a complaint, including but not limited to a complaint related to abuse, neglect, or exploitation, for the purposes of resolving the complaint to the resident's satisfaction and of protecting the health, welfare, and rights of the resident.

Does the ombudsman need my permission to act?

Yes, and that is the defining feature. Under 45 CFR 1324.19(b)(2)(ii) the representative must discuss the complaint with the resident, and if the resident is unable to communicate informed consent, the resident's representative, and request informed consent before investigating. Under 45 CFR 1324.11(e)(3) files, records and information maintained by the programme may be disclosed only at the discretion of the Ombudsman, subject to consent requirements and to court order.

Can an ombudsman office be run by a nursing home or a state licensing agency?

No. 45 CFR 1324.21(a) lists fifteen organisational conditions that create a conflict, including an organisation that is responsible for licensing, surveying, or certifying long-term care services, that is an association of long-term care facilities, that has any ownership or investment interest in a facility, that provides long-term care to residents, that sets reimbursement rates, that provides adult protective services, that conducts preadmission screening, or that makes decisions regarding admission or discharge.

Is there a free advocate for Medicare questions?

Yes. Under 42 U.S.C. 1395b-4 the Secretary makes grants to states for the purpose of providing information, counseling, and assistance relating to the procurement of adequate and appropriate health insurance coverage to eligible individuals. The state programme must provide assurances that staff members, including volunteer staff members, have no conflict of interest in providing the counseling.

How is an advocate different from adult protective services?

Their duties run to different parties. An advocate acts on the resident's instruction and consent; a protective services agency acts on a public duty. The regulation treats them as incompatible rather than similar: providing adult protective services is one of the fifteen organisational conflicts at 45 CFR 1324.21(a), and management responsibility for or supervision by such a programme is one of the individual conflicts at 1324.21(c)(2).

What should I ask before hiring a private advocate?

Use the statutory prohibitions as your questionnaire. Who pays you besides me. Do you hold any ownership, investment or employment interest in a facility, agency or insurer you might refer me to. Have you been employed by one recently, given that 45 CFR 1324.21(d) bars the statutory programme from hiring someone employed by a facility within the previous twelve months. Do you accept gifts, gratuities or referral fees. Will you ever act as my guardian or fiduciary. Who holds my records, and on whose consent are they released.
