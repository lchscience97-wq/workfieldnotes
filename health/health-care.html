Health Care in the United States: How the System Actually Works

Last verified
2 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Spending data
CMS, 2024
Sources

Reading time
10 min

The United States does not have one health care system. It has four large financing routes — employer insurance, Medicare, Medicaid and directly purchased plans — layered over a mostly private delivery system, with a residual group who have no coverage at all.

Almost everything confusing about American health care follows from that one fact. Which route you are on determines what is covered, who sets the rules, what you pay, and which agency you complain to when it goes wrong.

The scale of it

US health spending reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, which the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services put at $15,474 per person and 18.0 per cent of gross domestic product. Spending grew 7.2 per cent that year while the economy grew 5.3 per cent, which is why the health share of GDP keeps rising rather than holding steady.

Two things are worth holding onto from that. The per-person figure is an average across everyone, including the majority who use very little care in a given year, so it is not a typical household bill. And the growth rate matters more than the level: CMS actuaries project national health spending to approach $9.0 trillion and about 20.6 per cent of GDP by 2034.

The four routes to coverage

The Census Bureau measures coverage over a calendar year. Because people move between plans mid-year, the categories overlap and the percentages sum to more than 100.

Private coverage as a whole accounted for 66.1 per cent of the population and public coverage 35.5 per cent. Coverage is close to universal at the top of the age range — only about 0.6 per cent of adults aged 65 and over were uninsured — and 94.9 per cent of children aged 0 to 17 had coverage. The gap sits almost entirely among working-age adults.

The uninsured rate held at 8.0 per cent in 2024, statistically unchanged from 2023 and near a historic low. Medicaid enrolment fell as pandemic-era continuous-enrolment rules ended, and Marketplace enrolment rose by roughly the same amount — the two movements cancelled out.

Medicare and Medicaid are not variations of each other

They are routinely confused because the names rhyme and both are government programmes. Structurally they have little in common.

Medicare is federal, uniform across states, and keyed mainly to age. It is built in parts: hospital insurance, medical insurance, private plan alternatives that bundle those together, and prescription drug coverage. Eligibility is largely a function of age and work history rather than income.

Medicaid is a federal–state partnership. The federal government sets a floor and shares the cost; each state runs its own programme within that. This is why two people with identical incomes in different states can get different answers, and why "does Medicaid cover this?" has no national answer.

People can hold both. Those who qualify for Medicare and Medicaid simultaneously are usually called dual-eligible, and they are a disproportionately expensive group because eligibility for both generally signals both age and low income.

How care is actually delivered

Financing is fragmented; delivery is mostly private. Hospitals are predominantly non-profit or for-profit corporations rather than government facilities, and most physicians work for those systems or for practices contracted to them. The main exceptions are the Veterans Health Administration, the Indian Health Service, military treatment facilities and public hospital systems — large in absolute terms, small as a share of the whole.

The practical consequence is the network. An insurer negotiates prices with a set of hospitals and clinicians; that set is the network; care outside it is priced differently or not covered. Almost every surprise bill in the American system traces back to a network boundary rather than to a coverage decision about the treatment itself.

What it costs a household

Households encounter four separate charges, and conflating them is the most common source of confusion about American health costs.

  • Premium — the monthly cost of holding the plan, paid whether or not care is used. In employer coverage most of it is invisible because the employer pays a share directly.
  • Deductible — the amount paid out of pocket before the plan starts paying for most services.
  • Copayment or coinsurance — the fixed amount or percentage owed per service after the deductible.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum — the annual ceiling on the previous two, after which the plan covers in-network care in full.

A plan with a low premium and a high deductible is not cheaper; it moves cost from the certain column to the contingent one. That trade is the central choice in almost every American plan comparison.

The six words that cause most of the confusion

American health coverage has a vocabulary that is used precisely by insurers and loosely by everyone else. Six terms account for most misunderstandings.

Plain-language definitions. Your own plan documents govern the specific meaning in your contract.
TermWhat it means in practice
In-network A hospital or clinician that has a price agreement with your insurer. Going outside the network usually means a higher share of the bill, and sometimes no coverage at all.
Prior authorisation Approval the insurer requires before a service, or it will not pay. It is a coverage decision, not a clinical one, and it can be appealed.
Explanation of benefits (EOB) The statement the insurer sends after a claim showing what was billed, what it allowed and what you owe. It is not a bill, and it frequently arrives before the bill does.
Formulary The list of drugs a plan covers, usually arranged in tiers with different costs. A drug being prescribed does not mean it is on the formulary.
Allowed amount The negotiated price the insurer recognises for a service. Your share is calculated from this, not from the provider's list charge.
Self-funded plan An employer plan where the employer pays claims itself and an insurer only administers them. These are governed mainly by federal law, which is why state insurance rules often do not apply to them.

The last one explains a common dead end. Someone with a complaint about an employer plan contacts their state insurance department and is told the department has no jurisdiction. That usually means the plan is self-funded and the route runs through federal law and the plan's own appeal process instead.

The one place coverage is not the gatekeeper

Federal law requires hospitals that participate in Medicare and operate an emergency department to provide a medical screening examination to anyone who comes in requesting one, and to stabilise an emergency condition before transfer or discharge, regardless of insurance status or ability to pay. That obligation attaches to the hospital, not to the patient's coverage.

It is frequently described as a right to free emergency care, which it is not. The hospital may still bill afterwards, and the bill is often larger than it would have been in a lower-acuity setting. What the law guarantees is that the screening and stabilisation happen first and payment is settled second.

Who regulates what

There is no single regulator, and this is the second structural fact worth holding onto.

Simplified. Many activities are regulated at more than one level simultaneously.
LayerTypical authority
Federal — HHS and its agencies Medicare and Medicaid rules, health privacy under HIPAA, drug and device approval, public health, ACA Marketplace standards
State Licensing of clinicians and facilities, scope of practice, most insurance sold in the state, public health powers
Private accreditation Hospital and programme accreditation, frequently a practical condition of participating in federal programmes

For anyone trying to work out who has authority over a specific question, the useful first test is whether the question is about a programme (usually federal), a licence (usually state), or an insurance contract (usually state, unless the plan is a self-funded employer plan, in which case federal law generally governs).

The record behind the bill

Every one of those payment routes runs on the same underlying artefact: the clinical record. A visit is documented, the documentation is translated into standardised diagnosis and procedure codes, and the coded claim is what the payer actually adjudicates. The people who maintain that translation layer are health information professionals, and the accuracy of their work determines whether a claim is paid, denied or audited.

It also determines what you see. When you open your patient portal, you are looking at a filtered view of that same record.

What is changing

Three shifts are visible in the official projections rather than in commentary. The population is ageing, which moves people from private coverage onto Medicare and raises the public share of spending. Coverage policy is in flux: CMS actuaries project the insured share of the population to fall from 91.8 per cent in 2024 to about 90.5 per cent in 2034, reflecting expected changes to Medicaid rules and Marketplace subsidies. And spending continues to outgrow the economy, which is why cost control reappears in every policy cycle regardless of which party is proposing it.

Questions people actually ask

How do most Americans get health care coverage?

Through an employer. Census Bureau data for 2024 shows employment-based insurance covered 53.8 per cent of the population at some point in the year, ahead of Medicare at 19.1 per cent, Medicaid at 17.8 per cent and directly purchased plans at 10.7 per cent.

How much does the United States spend on health care?

US health spending reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person, according to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. That was 18.0 per cent of gross domestic product.

How many Americans are uninsured?

The Census Bureau reported an uninsured rate of 8.0 per cent for 2024, statistically unchanged from 2023 and near a historic low. Coverage is close to universal above age 65 because of Medicare.

What is the difference between Medicare and Medicaid?

Medicare is a federal programme tied mainly to age 65 and to certain disabilities. Medicaid is a joint federal–state programme tied to income and category, so its eligibility rules and benefits vary by state.

Who regulates health care in the United States?

Authority is split. The federal Department of Health and Human Services and its agencies set national rules on programmes, privacy and products. States license clinicians and facilities and regulate most insurance sold in the state. Accreditation bodies add a private layer on top.

Is health care free for anyone in the United States?

Not as a general entitlement. Medicaid and some programmes for veterans and Native American populations carry little or no cost at the point of use for those who qualify, and hospitals must provide emergency screening and stabilising treatment regardless of ability to pay. None of that is the same as universal free care.

Why are American health prices so hard to find in advance?

Because the price is a negotiated rate between one insurer and one provider rather than a list price, and the same procedure has many such rates at the same hospital. Federal price-transparency rules now require hospitals and plans to publish those rates, but the files are large and the published data is not consistently usable by patients.
