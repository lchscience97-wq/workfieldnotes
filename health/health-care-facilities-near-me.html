Health Care Facilities Near Me: The Federal Data Behind the Rankings

The results for this search are hospital marketing sites and a ranking publisher. Both are answering a question about reputation. Neither is the free federal dataset that already exists.

CMS publishes inspection results, staffing figures and quality measures for certified facilities — over 150 quality measures for hospitals alone. And the nursing home star rating everyone quotes is not a judgement: it is arithmetic, starting from inspections and adjusted by two other components, with one part ranked within your state only. This page decodes it.

Who writes the results

Search this term and you get two kinds of page. The first is a hospital system's own site, which is accurate about location and services and is not attempting neutrality about quality. The second is a commercial ranking publisher applying its own methodology.

Neither is dishonest. Both are downstream of a question the federal government already answers with primary data, published free, and updated on a schedule. The reason people do not use it is that it is not optimised to rank first for near me.

The comparison data that already exists

CMS publishes provider comparison information through Care Compare and the Provider Data Catalog, covering certified provider types — the same taxonomy of hospitals, nursing homes, home health agencies, hospices and others set out in our guide to the federal facility list.

Two features distinguish it from a ranking. It is measurement rather than editorial: the inputs are inspection findings, administrative claims and standardised patient surveys. And it is the same data everywhere, so a facility cannot opt out of appearing badly by declining to participate.

What is published about hospitals

CMS reports over 150 hospital quality measures on Care Compare and the Provider Data Catalog. They fall into recognisable families.

Process of care measures capture whether or not a health care provider gives recommended care based on guidelines. Outcome measures are designed to reflect the results of care, rather than whether or not a specific treatment or intervention was performed. Patient experience is measured through HCAHPS, the standardised patient survey. Alongside those sit imaging efficiency patterns, care transitions, emergency department throughput efficiency, care coordination and patient safety.

CMS added an Overall Hospital Quality Star Rating in July 2016; Veterans Health Administration hospitals became eligible for those ratings in 2023, and patient-reported outcomes were added in 2024.

The distinction between process and outcome is the one to hold. A hospital can score well on doing recommended things and less well on results, and the two answer different questions about the same building.

The five-star system, decoded

Nursing homes carry an overall rating and three component ratings: health inspections, staffing and quality measures. CMS describes homes with 5 stars as having much above average quality and homes with 1 star as quality much below average.

The health inspection rating rests on points assigned to the results of surveys over the past three years, together with complaint surveys from the past three years, with more recent surveys weighted more heavily. Ratings are recalculated monthly.

The staffing rating uses two measures contributing equally: registered nurse hours per resident day, and total staffing hours per resident day across registered nurses, licensed practical nurses and nurse aides. Since 2022 the domain has also reflected staff turnover and weekend staffing.

The overall rating is arithmetic

This is the part almost nobody knows, and it changes how the number should be read.

The overall rating starts from the health inspection rating. Then CMS adds one star if the staffing rating is 4 or 5 stars and also greater than the health inspection rating, and subtracts one star if the staffing rating is 1 star. Then it adds one star if the quality measure rating is 5 stars and subtracts one star if the quality measure is 1 star. The result cannot go above 5 stars or lower than 1 star.

Two constraints sit on top. Where the health inspection rating is 1 star, upgrades from staffing and quality measures cannot exceed one additional star in total. And Special Focus Facilities that have not graduated from that programme cannot exceed 3 stars.

The consequence: inspections dominate. A home cannot rise above three stars on staffing and quality alone if inspections placed it at one. Reading the three component ratings tells you far more than the single overall number, because the overall number is a compressed function of them.

The comparison caveat almost nobody applies

The health inspection component — the starting point for everything above — is scored relative to other homes within the same state.

So two homes in different states holding the same overall rating have been measured against different peer groups. A four-star home in a state with strict surveying and a four-star home in a state with a milder distribution are not equivalent, and no amount of staring at the stars will surface that.

Within a metropolitan area that crosses a state line — and many do — this is not a theoretical problem. Comparing two facilities twenty minutes apart can mean comparing two different scales.

There is a way around it that costs nothing. The component ratings sit on top of underlying findings, and those findings are published: the actual deficiencies cited at a survey, their scope and severity, and the staffing hours reported. Two homes whose stars are not comparable across a state line have inspection reports that are, because a deficiency describes what an inspector found rather than where the home ranked among its neighbours. When the comparison matters and the border is in the way, read down to the findings rather than across at the stars.

What a ranking publisher measures instead

A commercial ranking is a defensible product with an undefended premise: that a single ordering exists. Its methodology is its own, its weightings are editorial choices, and it usually mixes federal measures with reputation surveys.

That is not a reason to ignore it. It is a reason to read the methodology page before the list, because the list is the output of choices made on that page. Where a ranking and the federal component ratings disagree, the disagreement is informative: it usually means the publisher weighted something CMS does not, or the other way round.

Using both without confusing them

Start from what the facility is certified as, because that determines which rule book and which dataset apply — a hospital, an ambulatory surgical centre and a long term care facility are not compared on the same measures. Then read the component ratings rather than the overall one. Then check whether the comparison you are making crosses a state line.

Then, if a ranking is in play, read what it measured. And for the questions no dataset answers — whether the place feels well run on a Tuesday afternoon — CMS's own advice is to visit and to consult state or local organisations such as local advocacy groups and the State Ombudsman programme.

What no dataset settles

None of it tells you what a stay will cost, which is a coverage question rather than a quality one; our guides to which regulator is yours and the estimate you can demand cover that side. None of it tells you whether the specific service you need is offered at the specific site, which for health centres in particular is a scope question covered in why services vary by building.

And CMS is explicit about the limit of its own instrument: no rating system can address all of the important considerations that go into a decision about which nursing home may be best for a particular person. That sentence is on a government page, not a critic's. Treating the stars as a shortlist tool rather than a verdict is what the publisher of the stars recommends.

Questions people actually ask

Is there free federal data comparing facilities near me?

Yes. CMS publishes provider comparison data through Care Compare and the Provider Data Catalog, covering hospitals, nursing homes and other certified provider types. For hospitals alone CMS reports over 150 quality measures, including process of care measures, outcome measures, patient experience through HCAHPS, imaging efficiency, care transitions, emergency department throughput, care coordination and patient safety.

How is a nursing home overall star rating calculated?

It starts from the health inspection rating and is then adjusted. CMS adds one star if the staffing rating is 4 or 5 stars and also greater than the health inspection rating, and subtracts one star if the staffing rating is 1 star. It then adds one star if the quality measure rating is 5 stars and subtracts one if it is 1 star. The result cannot go above 5 stars or below 1 star.

What is the health inspection rating based on?

Points assigned to the results of nursing home surveys over the past three years, together with complaint surveys from the past three years, with more recent surveys weighted more heavily. Ratings are recalculated monthly, so a rating you looked at last month may not be the rating today.

Can I compare star ratings between states?

Not straightforwardly. The health inspection component, which is the starting point for the overall rating, is scored relative to other homes within the same state. Two homes in different states with the same number of stars have been measured against different peer groups, so a cross-state comparison of overall stars is not comparing like with like.

What does the staffing rating actually measure?

Two measures contributing equally: registered nurse hours per resident day, and total staffing hours per resident day covering registered nurses, licensed practical nurses and nurse aides. Since 2022 the staffing domain has also reflected staff turnover and weekend staffing levels.

Is a facility with a low rating penalised?

There is one structural constraint worth knowing. Where the health inspection rating is 1 star, upgrades from the staffing and quality measure components are capped at one additional star in total. Special Focus Facilities that have not graduated from that programme cannot exceed 3 stars regardless of their other components.

Should I rely on the star rating alone?

CMS says not to. Its own guidance states that no rating system can address all of the important considerations that go into a decision about which nursing home may be best for a particular person, and recommends visiting facilities and consulting state or local organisations such as local advocacy groups and the State Ombudsman programme.
