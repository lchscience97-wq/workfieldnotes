Health Care Solutions: What Vendors Actually Mean, and What to Check

Last verified
3 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Legal basis
HIPAA · 45 CFR 160.103
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

"Health care solutions" is marketing language, not a defined category. No regulator uses it, no statute defines it, and no industry body maintains a standard for it. Search the phrase and you get companies, not an answer.

Underneath the phrase sit six genuinely different businesses. And one legal question — whether the vendor will touch patient data — determines more about the contract than the category does.

Why the term tells you nothing

The phrase is unregulated and describes no particular service, which makes it a convenient name for almost any company selling into healthcare. A firm supplying home oxygen equipment, a firm providing hospital dining and housekeeping staff, and a firm selling analytics software can all describe themselves with the same three words and none of them is being misleading.

That is not a complaint about vendors. It is a practical warning about research: if you are comparing options, the phrase cannot be your search unit. You have to translate it into the actual work first.

The six things the phrase usually means

Nearly every company using the term falls into one of these. They differ in who buys them, how they are priced, and whether patient data is involved.

Categorisation by the buying department and the data involved. Where a vendor spans two rows, the contract usually needs to address both.
CategoryWhat is actually soldWho buys itTouches patient data?

The right-hand column is the one that changes your obligations. Four of the six categories involve protected health information as a matter of course. That moves the relationship out of ordinary commercial law and into federal health privacy law.

The question that decides the contract

Under HIPAA, a business associate is a person or entity that creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity, or provides certain services to a covered entity involving that information. The definition sits at 45 CFR 160.103.

Where a covered entity engages a business associate, HHS requires a written business associate contract that establishes specifically what the business associate has been engaged to do and requires it to comply with the Rules' requirements for protecting privacy and security. This is not optional and it is not satisfied by a standard non-disclosure agreement.

Three consequences are worth stating precisely, because vendor materials rarely do.

Business associates are directly liable. HHS states that in addition to their contractual obligations, business associates are directly liable for compliance with certain provisions of the HIPAA Rules. Enforcement is not limited to the provider who hired them.

Cloud providers are included. HHS guidance is explicit that a cloud service provider receiving or maintaining electronic protected health information for a covered entity or another business associate requires a compliant business associate agreement — and that this holds even in arrangements where the provider only stores encrypted data it cannot read.

Due diligence is your obligation, not theirs. A covered entity that fails to check a vendor's compliance before engaging it may still be held responsible for a subsequent breach of unsecured protected health information. Signing the agreement does not, on its own, transfer the risk.

Practical test before any contract: will this vendor, or anyone it subcontracts to, be able to see or store anything that identifies a patient? If yes, a business associate agreement is required, and it must reflect the actual services rather than being a template pasted in unchanged.

Who regulates what

There is no single regulator for this space, which is part of why the term stays vague.

Simplified. A single vendor can sit under more than one of these at once.
If the vendor…Then it falls under…

Sells certified health ITThe federal health IT certification programme and information-blocking rules
Places clinical staffState professional licensure applying to each placed worker
Does none of the aboveOrdinary state commercial and contract law

Note the asymmetry: a company can be entirely legitimate and still fall under nothing more than ordinary contract law. "Regulated" is not a quality signal here, and its absence is not a warning — it depends on what is being sold.

How these things are priced

Pricing units differ by category and are the single most common source of surprise after signing.

  • Health IT — per user per month, per provider, or per bed, frequently with separate implementation, interface and training fees that exceed the first year of subscription.
  • Revenue cycle — usually a percentage of collections, which aligns incentives on volume but not necessarily on the accuracy of what is coded.
  • Staffing — a bill rate that includes the worker's pay plus a markup; the markup, not the bill rate, is the negotiable number.
  • Home medical equipment — billed to the payer under programme rules rather than to the facility, so the provider's exposure is administrative rather than financial.
  • Facility services — per square foot, per meal or per bed day, generally the most transparent of the six.

Where the price is a percentage of something you also control, ask what happens if that something changes. Where the price is per unit, ask what counts as a unit.

The clause people forget

The exit terms matter more in healthcare than in most industries, because the data is a legal record with retention obligations that outlive the contract.

Three questions belong in every one of these agreements. Who owns the data during and after the term? In what format and on what timetable is it returned or destroyed at termination? And who bears the cost of migration if you leave? A vendor that answers the first two easily and the third vaguely is telling you something useful.

The underlying record is the same artefact patients have a right to reach, described in our guide to patient portals and your right of access, and the same one that determines whether a claim is paid, described in how US health care works.

What a straight answer looks like

The fastest way to sort vendors is to ask four questions that have factual answers and listen for whether the answer is specific.

Questions worth asking in a first call, and how to read the reply.
QuestionA workable answerA warning sign
Will you handle protected health information? A direct yes or no, followed by which systems and which staff roles have access "We're HIPAA compliant" with no description of the data flow
Will you sign our business associate agreement, or do we sign yours? Either, with a named person who reviews it Reluctance to put the answer in writing, or a template that does not name the actual services
Who do you subcontract to? A list, and confirmation that agreements flow down to each one "We handle everything in-house" for work that plainly requires infrastructure they do not own
What happens to our data on the last day of the contract? A format, a timetable and who pays Referral to a clause nobody can quote from memory

None of these require technical expertise to evaluate. They require only noticing whether the reply contains nouns. "HIPAA compliant" is not a fact about a system; it is a claim about a system, and the two are distinguishable in a single question.

One further note on subcontractors. A business associate that engages a subcontractor to handle protected health information on its behalf must obtain the same satisfactory assurances from that subcontractor. The chain does not stop at your direct vendor, which is why the third question matters more than its brevity suggests.

A short evaluation sequence

In the order that saves the most time:

  1. Name the category. Which of the six is this actually? If the vendor spans two, price and contract them separately.
  2. Answer the data question. Protected health information involved, yes or no. That determines whether a business associate agreement is required.
  3. Identify the pricing unit and what changes it.
  4. Read the exit clause before reading the feature list.
  5. Ask for the security documentation before engagement rather than after, since due diligence is your obligation.
  6. Then compare features.

Most buying processes run this list in reverse, which is why the contract stage takes longer than the selection stage.

Questions people actually ask

What does health care solutions mean?

It is a marketing umbrella rather than a defined category. In practice it covers six distinct businesses: health IT and electronic records, revenue cycle and billing, staffing, home medical equipment and home care, facility support services such as dining and housekeeping, and care management or population health.

Why do so many companies have health care solutions in their name?

Because the phrase is unregulated and describes no particular service, it works as a name for almost any business selling to healthcare providers. Searching the term returns companies rather than a definition, which is why the results look unrelated to each other.

Does a health care solutions vendor need a HIPAA business associate agreement?

If the vendor creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity, then yes. HHS requires a written business associate contract, and business associates are directly liable for compliance with certain HIPAA provisions.

Is a cloud provider a business associate?

Yes, where it receives and maintains electronic protected health information for a covered entity or another business associate. HHS guidance states that a compliant business associate agreement is required with the cloud service provider, and that this holds even where the provider only stores encrypted data.

What should you check before signing with a health care solutions vendor?

Which of the six categories the work actually falls into; whether protected health information will be touched and therefore whether a business associate agreement is required; what the pricing unit is; who owns the data at exit; and what happens to your data when the contract ends.

Who regulates health care solutions vendors?

No single body. Vendors handling protected health information fall under HIPAA enforcement by the HHS Office for Civil Rights. Medicare suppliers must meet programme enrolment standards. Health IT products may be certified under the federal certification programme. Everything else is ordinary commercial law.

Is the covered entity still liable if the vendor causes a breach?

Potentially. Business associates can be penalised directly, but a covered entity that failed to conduct reasonable due diligence before engaging a vendor may still be held responsible for a resulting breach of unsecured protected health information.
