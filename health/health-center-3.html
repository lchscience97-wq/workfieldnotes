Health Center 3: Which Operator, and Which Privacy Rule

The number identifies nothing on its own. Municipal health departments number their district centres. Universities number theirs. The systems are unrelated, and a search for the bare number returns whichever one ranks highest rather than the one you meant.

The operator is the identifier — and it decides something people rarely think to check: which privacy law governs your record. A city health centre is a HIPAA covered entity. A university student health clinic generally is not, because its records sit in FERPA's treatment records category instead.

A number is not an identifier

Numbered health centres are an internal filing convention. A city assigns numbers to district clinics in the order it opened them; a university does the same on its campus; neither consulted the other, and neither is authoritative outside its own system.

That means the number carries no information across systems. Two buildings a thousand miles apart can both be Health Center 3, run by unrelated organisations, under different laws, offering different services at different prices.

The resolution is always the same and always cheap: pair the number with the operator, or skip the number and use the street address. Our guide to why services vary by building covers the sibling problem inside a single system.

Search results make this worse rather than better, and predictably so. A bare number carries no distinguishing terms, so ranking falls back on general authority: whichever operator has the strongest site wins the query for everybody. Directory listings and social pages fill the rest, often describing a location that has moved, merged or closed. None of that is a failure of the search engine; it is what happens when the query contains no information about which system is meant.

The municipal case

In Philadelphia, Health Center 3 is a City health centre at 555 S. 43rd St., Philadelphia, PA 19104, with published telephone numbers (215) 685-7504 and (215) 685-7506, run by the Philadelphia Department of Public Health.

The City's own Find a Clinic handout lists its AHS Health Centers among the federally qualified health centers in Philadelphia, which places them inside the federal Health Center Program. Two consequences follow immediately. The centre is a HIPAA covered entity. And section 330 of the Public Health Service Act applies, requiring service to all residents of the area served by the center under 42 U.S.C. §254b(a)(1) and a schedule of fees with a corresponding schedule of discounts adjusted on the basis of ability to pay under (k)(3)(G).

Our guide to the full City roster in one place lists every site with its address and published number.

The campus case, and the privacy surprise

Now the same number on a university campus, where the answer inverts.

HHS states that FERPA applies to records on students at health clinics run by postsecondary institutions, and that those records are excluded from HIPAA because they qualify as education records or treatment records under FERPA.

Most people assume the opposite. A clinic is a clinic, a doctor is a doctor, so surely the health privacy law applies. It generally does not, and the reason is statutory rather than accidental: the HIPAA Privacy Rule steps back where FERPA already occupies the ground.

It is worth being precise about what that does and does not mean, because the shorthand version of this fact circulates in a misleading form. It does not mean the records are unprotected. FERPA is a privacy statute with its own restrictions on disclosure, and state law and professional licensing obligations continue to apply to the clinician regardless of which federal regime is in play. What changes is the machinery: the definitions, the permitted disclosures, the request route and the complaint route all belong to a different statute administered by a different department. Someone who arrives at a campus clinic expecting the HIPAA process is not wrong about being protected; they are looking for the wrong form.

What a treatment record is

The category comes from 20 U.S.C. §1232g(a)(4)(B)(iv), and HHS describes it as records on a student who is eighteen years or older, or attending postsecondary education, which are made or maintained by a physician, psychiatrist, psychologist, or other recognized professional acting in their professional capacity, and which are made, maintained, or used only in connection with providing treatment to the student, and are not available to anyone other than persons providing such treatment.

Read the last clause closely. The exclusion is conditional on the record staying inside the treating circle. It is not a permanent property of the document; it is a description of how the document is being handled.

The condition that flips it

HHS is explicit about what happens when that condition breaks: treatment records remain excluded from FERPA's education records definition only when maintained solely for treatment purposes. If a school discloses a student's treatment records for any other purpose, those records become subject to all FERPA requirements, including the student's rights to inspect and review them.

That produces a result worth stating plainly. While the record is held tightly for treatment, the student's inspection right is narrower than most people expect. Once it is disclosed for another purpose, the record moves into the education records regime and the inspection right attaches. Disclosure changes the category, and the category changes the rights.

One building, two rule books

The fourth row is not hypothetical. HHS states that institutions serving both students and non-students must comply with FERPA for student patients and HIPAA for non-student patients.

So a campus clinic that also treats staff, dependants or the public is running two regimes at once, through the same clinicians and often the same software. Which law protects a given record depends on who the patient was, not on where the visit happened.

For anyone trying to obtain their own record from such a place, that is the question to ask first: was I seen as a student, or as a member of the public? The answer selects the request route.

The status can also change over time for the same person. A student who graduates and keeps using the clinic, a member of staff who enrols in a course, a dependant who becomes a student — each transition moves later records into a different regime while leaving earlier ones where they were. A request that spans such a boundary can legitimately return two sets of material under two processes, which looks like obstruction and usually is not.

Resolving your own case

Three steps. Identify the operator by name, not by number — a city department, a non-profit, a university, a private practice. Establish whether it is a federally qualified health centre, because that brings the section 330 obligations with it; our guide to what the federal test actually is sets out how to check. Then ask which privacy regime it says it operates under, which any competently run organisation can answer in one sentence.

If the answer is HIPAA, the record request goes to the covered entity. If the answer is FERPA, it goes to the institution, and the treatment records condition above determines what you can see. Our guide to your record and your right of access covers the HIPAA route in detail.

What the number still cannot tell you

It cannot tell you services, hours, or whether the site takes your insurance, all of which vary by operator and by building. It cannot tell you price, which depends on whether a sliding fee obligation attaches.

And it cannot tell you the thing this page is really about: which body of law is standing behind the record created when you walk in. That question has a clean answer, it is free to ask, and almost nobody asks it — because the number on the door looks like an identifier and behaves like a filing code.

Questions people actually ask

Does the number identify a specific health center?

No. Numbered health centers exist in several unrelated systems — municipal health department districts, university campuses and others — and the numbering is internal to each. A search for the bare number returns whichever operator ranks highest rather than the one you meant. The reliable identifier is the operator plus the street address.

Which Health Center 3 does a city usually mean?

In Philadelphia, Health Center 3 is a City health center at 555 S. 43rd St., Philadelphia, PA 19104, with published telephone numbers (215) 685-7504 and (215) 685-7506, operated by the Philadelphia Department of Public Health. The City's own Find a Clinic handout lists its AHS Health Centers among the federally qualified health centers in Philadelphia.

Does HIPAA cover a university student health center?

Usually not. HHS states that FERPA applies to records on students at health clinics run by postsecondary institutions, and that those records are excluded from HIPAA because they qualify as education records or treatment records under FERPA. The statutory basis for the treatment records category is 20 U.S.C. §1232g(a)(4)(B)(iv).

What is a treatment record?

HHS describes it as a record on a student who is eighteen years or older, or attending postsecondary education, made or maintained by a physician, psychiatrist, psychologist or other recognized professional acting in their professional capacity, made, maintained or used only in connection with providing treatment to the student, and not available to anyone other than persons providing such treatment.

Can a treatment record lose that status?

Yes, and the trigger is disclosure. Treatment records are excluded from FERPA's education records definition only while maintained solely for treatment purposes. If a school discloses a student's treatment records for any other purpose, the records become subject to all FERPA requirements, including the student's right to inspect and review them.

What if a campus clinic also treats non-students?

Then it can be operating under two rule books at once. HHS states that institutions serving both students and non-students must comply with FERPA for student patients and HIPAA for non-student patients. The same building, the same clinician and the same software can produce records governed by different laws depending on who the patient is.

How do I work out which rules apply to me?

Identify the operator first. A city or non-profit federally qualified health center is a HIPAA covered entity and carries section 330 obligations including a sliding fee discount schedule. A postsecondary institution's student health clinic is generally in the FERPA treatment records position instead. Those are different rights with different request routes, and the number on the door tells you nothing about which one you are in.
