For 2026 the limit is $3,400 and the maximum carryover is $680. That second figure is exactly one fifth of the first, and it is the only part of an unspent balance a plan may let you keep — if it offers a carryover at all.

Two relief mechanisms exist and a plan may offer only one: a grace period of up to two and a half months, or a carryover. It may also offer neither. And a general purpose health FSA closes the HSA door for the year, which for 2026 is a $4,400 door for self-only coverage.

What the account is

A health flexible spending arrangement is an employer arrangement funded by salary reduction. You elect an amount before the year starts, it comes out of pay before tax, and you draw on it for qualified medical expenses.

The results for this term are a government site and several administrators — the companies that run these accounts for employers. Their pages are accurate about mechanics and quiet about the part that costs people money, which is what happens at the end of the year.

The word doing the most work in the name is arrangement. This is not an account you open, own and take with you. It exists because an employer created it, it operates on that employer's plan year, and its terms are set in a plan document you did not negotiate. Two people at different employers can both have a health FSA and face materially different rules on the same December afternoon, because the federal provisions set ceilings and permissions rather than a single design. That is why almost every practical question about one of these accounts resolves to the same instruction: read the plan document, not the general explanation.

The 2026 figures

The IRS sets the employee salary reduction contribution limit for a health FSA at $3,400 for 2026, with a maximum carryover amount of $680.

Those two numbers should be read together. The carryover ceiling is 20 percent of the contribution ceiling exactly, which is a useful way to remember the shape of the risk: in the worst case a plan with the maximum carryover still leaves four fifths of an over-election exposed to forfeiture.

Grace period or carryover, never both

The default is unforgiving. The IRS describes these as use-it-or-lose-it plans in which amounts in the account at the end of the plan year can't generally be carried over to the next year.

Two mechanisms soften it. A plan may offer a grace period of up to 2 1/2 months after the end of the plan year, during which any qualified medical expenses incurred in that period can be paid from any amounts left in the account. Or a plan may offer a carryover, moving unused amounts up to the annual ceiling into the following plan year.

They are alternatives, not layers. The IRS is explicit: a plan adopting a carryover provision is not permitted to also provide a grace period with respect to health FSAs.

The two behave differently in practice. A grace period extends the spending window for the whole balance, whatever its size. A carryover preserves a capped amount but gives you a full further year to use it. Which is better depends on whether your leftover is large and your calendar tight, or small and your calendar open. Neither is automatic, and a plan may offer neither.

What qualifies

The IRS describes qualified medical expenses as those that would generally qualify for the medical and dental expenses deduction, together with over-the-counter medicine (whether or not prescribed) and menstrual care products.

The people covered are broader than many elections assume: the employee, a spouse, dependants, and children under age 27. That last category is worth flagging, because a child can be over the age at which they stopped being a tax dependant and still be inside the FSA definition.

The over-the-counter inclusion deserves its own note, because it changes how an unspent balance can be cleared. Medicine bought without a prescription qualifies, as do menstrual care products, which means a December balance is not confined to appointments and copays. That is a genuine relief valve, and it is also the reason the forfeiture rule bites less often than the horror stories suggest. It does not make an over-election free — buying supplies you would not otherwise have bought is spending, not saving — but it does widen the exit.

Why you cannot pair it with an HSA

This is the trade-off that costs the most and appears in no enrolment interface.

The IRS states that an employee covered by a high deductible health plan and a health FSA can't generally make contributions to an HSA. A general purpose health FSA therefore forecloses HSA contributions for that year.

Put the ceilings next to each other and the size of the decision becomes visible. For 2026 the HSA contribution limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage — against an FSA limit of $3,400. The self-only gap alone is $1,000, and it runs in the direction most people do not expect.

The eligibility gate is separate again. An HSA requires coverage under a high deductible health plan, which for 2026 means a minimum annual deductible of $1,700 self-only and $3,400 family, with maximum out-of-pocket expenses of $8,500 and $17,000. Sizing an election

Work upward from expenses you are confident you will incur, not downward from the ceiling. Because the default is forfeiture, every dollar elected above certain spending is a dollar wagered on future need, and the payoff on winning is only the tax saving.

Then establish which relief your plan offers, in writing, before electing. A plan with a $680 carryover supports a modestly larger election than a plan with nothing, because the top slice has somewhere to go. A plan with a two-and-a-half-month grace period supports a larger one still, since the whole balance stays spendable into the new year — but only for expenses incurred inside that window.

Finally, decide the HSA question first if you have the choice, because it is the larger number and it is binary. An FSA election is not reversible mid-year on a change of mind.

One structural asymmetry is worth carrying into that decision. An FSA ceiling is a ceiling on a single plan year and nothing survives it beyond the carryover, if any. An HSA balance is not bounded by a plan year at all, so the annual limit is a contribution cap rather than a spending deadline. Comparing $3,400 with $4,400 as though they were the same kind of number understates the gap, because only one of them has to be spent by a date.

Three expensive misreadings

Treating the carryover as automatic. It exists only if the plan adopted it, and a plan that offers a grace period cannot also offer it.

Treating the grace period as extra money. It extends the window for spending what is already there; it does not add to the balance, and expenses must be incurred inside the period.

Treating the FSA and HSA as complementary. A general purpose health FSA blocks HSA contributions, so electing a small FSA can quietly cost access to a larger account. Our guide to which regulator is yours covers the plan-side questions that sit behind that choice.

What the account does not do

It does not lower a bill; it changes the tax treatment of paying one. It does not travel with you in the way an HSA does, because it is an employer arrangement rather than a personal account. And it does not protect against a bad year, because it is capped at $3,400 while the annual limitation on cost sharing for 2026 sits far above that.

What it does well is narrow and real: it makes predictable, known, in-network spending cheaper by moving it before tax. Used at the size of your certain expenses rather than your possible ones, and with the plan's relief mechanism established in advance, it is one of the few uncomplicated wins in American health financing. Used at the ceiling on optimism, it is a forfeiture waiting for December.

Questions people actually ask

How much can I put in a health FSA in 2026?

The IRS sets the 2026 employee salary reduction contribution limit for a health flexible spending arrangement at $3,400, with a maximum carryover amount of $680. The carryover ceiling is exactly one fifth of the contribution limit.

What happens to money I do not spend?

By default it is forfeited. The IRS describes health FSAs as use-it-or-lose-it plans in which amounts in the account at the end of the plan year cannot generally be carried over to the next year. A plan may soften that with either a grace period or a carryover, but it is not obliged to offer either.

Can a plan have both a grace period and a carryover?

No. The IRS states that a plan adopting a carryover provision is not permitted to also provide a grace period with respect to health FSAs. A grace period allows qualified medical expenses incurred in up to two and a half months after the plan year to be paid from amounts left in the account; a carryover moves a capped amount into the following plan year instead.

What counts as a qualified medical expense?

The IRS describes them as expenses that would generally qualify for the medical and dental expenses deduction, plus over-the-counter medicine, whether or not prescribed, and menstrual care products. They cover medical costs for the employee, a spouse, dependants, and children under age 27.

Can I have a health FSA and an HSA at the same time?

Not with a general purpose health FSA. The IRS states that an employee covered by a high deductible health plan and a health FSA cannot generally make contributions to an HSA. That matters because the 2026 HSA contribution limits are $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage, which is more than the $3,400 FSA limit.

Is a health FSA the same as a health savings account?

No, and the differences are structural rather than cosmetic. A health FSA is an employer arrangement funded by salary reduction with a use-it-or-lose-it default and a capped carryover. An HSA requires coverage under a high deductible health plan, which for 2026 means a minimum annual deductible of $1,700 for self-only and $3,400 for family coverage.

How should I size my election?

Work from expenses you are confident you will incur rather than from the ceiling. Because the default is forfeiture, an election above your certain spending converts a tax saving into a loss. Then ask which of the two relief mechanisms your plan offers, because a plan with a carryover of up to $680 is a materially different proposition from a plan with neither.
