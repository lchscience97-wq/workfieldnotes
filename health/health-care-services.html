Health Care Services: What the Law Requires Covered

This phrase points at three different lists, and the search results only show the third. There is a legal list — the ten essential health benefit categories every Marketplace plan must cover. There is a statistical list — the four subsectors of NAICS 62, employing about 23.9 million people. And there are company names.

The first two are the ones that decide what you can claim and where the work happens. They also do not line up with each other, which is the point most worth knowing: prescription drugs are a required benefit, but pharmacies are not in the health care industry sector at all.

Three lists behind one phrase

Every domain ranking for this term is a company whose legal or trading name contains it. That is a reasonable outcome for a brand search and a useless one for anybody trying to work out what the phrase denotes. This page does not name or assess any of those companies. It sets out the two lists that are defined by rule rather than by trademark.

The ten categories, listed exactly

HealthCare.gov states that all Marketplace plans must cover the following, and the wording is worth reading rather than paraphrasing because each phrase is doing work:

1. Ambulatory patient services (outpatient care you get without being admitted to a hospital). 2. Emergency services. 3. Hospitalization (like surgery and overnight stays). 4. Pregnancy, maternity, and newborn care (both before and after birth). 5. Mental health and substance use disorder services, including behavioral health treatment.

6. Prescription drugs. 7. Rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices. 8. Laboratory services. 9. Preventive and wellness services and chronic disease management. 10. Pediatric services, including oral and vision care.

Two further requirements sit alongside the ten: plans must also provide birth control coverage and breastfeeding coverage.

Category 7 is the one most people cannot parse. Rehabilitative services help recover a function that was lost; habilitative services help acquire a function that was never gained. The distinction matters because plans document them separately and can apply different limits to each.

What "essential" does not mean

Three limits are worth stating, because the word oversells itself.

It does not mean free. The categories define what a plan must include, not what you pay. Deductibles, copayments and coinsurance operate inside each one.

It does not mean unlimited. A plan can satisfy a category and still cap visits, require prior authorisation, or maintain a formulary that excludes a particular drug.

It does not mean network-blind. A covered service delivered out of network is still charged at out-of-network rates unless a specific protection applies. That interaction is set out in two meanings, one search box.

Those three limits compound in a way that catches people out. A service can be inside a required category, delivered by a licensed clinician, and still produce a bill of a size the word "essential" does not prepare anybody for — because the category governed whether the plan had to include the benefit at all, and every other variable was decided elsewhere. Reading a denial letter usefully starts with working out which of the three limits it is invoking, since the three lead to different arguments and different appeal routes.

The industry definition, and its odd fourth member

The statistical sense of the phrase is NAICS sector 62. BLS describes it plainly: the sector comprises establishments providing health care and social assistance for individuals. It contains four subsectors: Ambulatory Health Care Services (621), Hospitals (622), Nursing and Residential Care Facilities (623) and Social Assistance (624).

That fourth subsector surprises people. Social assistance — child day care, vocational rehabilitation, community food and housing services, individual and family services — is counted inside the same sector as hospitals. It is not a drafting accident: the classification groups establishments by the similarity of what they do, and the boundary between clinical care and social support is genuinely porous at the delivery end.

The practical consequence is that any headline figure for "health care employment" needs to be read for whether it includes 624. Most sector-level figures do.

The other three subsectors are worth reading for what they group rather than what they are called. 621, Ambulatory Health Care Services, is the broadest: physician and dental offices, outpatient centres, medical and diagnostic laboratories, home health agencies and ambulance services all sit inside it, which is why a single subsector code can cover a laboratory and a house call. 622, Hospitals, is narrow and institutional. 623, Nursing and Residential Care Facilities, is defined by the residential element rather than by clinical intensity, so it holds both skilled nursing and forms of supported living.

That structure explains a recurring confusion. People assume the sector is organised by what is wrong with the patient. It is organised by the type of establishment delivering the service — whether the patient stays overnight, whether the site is residential, whether the work is clinical at all. Two services that feel identical to a patient can sit in different subsectors purely because of where they happen.

The scale, with the date attached

BLS put employment in the Health Care and Social Assistance sector at approximately 23.9 million as of July 2026, on preliminary data.

Two cautions on using that number. It is preliminary and subject to revision, so it should be quoted with its month rather than as a standing fact. And it is establishment-based employment across all four subsectors, which is a different population from the occupational counts used in career guides — those classify people by what they do rather than by where their employer is filed. The occupational view of the same territory, and the two-tier wage structure inside it, is in two tiers, $45,910 apart.

Where the two lists disagree

This is the comparison nobody runs, and it is the reason the phrase causes so much confusion. Mapping each required benefit onto the subsector that typically delivers it produces four rows that do not fit.

Count the rows that are not a clean yes: three of ten. The most striking is prescription drugs. It is one of the ten benefits a Marketplace plan must cover, and the establishments that dispense it are not counted in the health care industry sector at all. Any argument that reasons from industry employment to coverage adequacy, or the reverse, crosses that gap without noticing it.

The rehabilitative row carries a second version of the same problem. The category covers services and devices, and devices reach the patient through suppliers whose classification is different again — a distinction with practical consequences, covered in how to vet a supplier.

The third meaning, briefly

The organisations ranking for this phrase are a mix of insurers, facility-services contractors, home health companies and community clinics. Sharing a phrase in their name tells you nothing about what they do, and this site does not rate or recommend any of them.

What is worth knowing is that the same words appear in two structurally different kinds of organisation: commercial firms selling into the sector, and nonprofit or federally supported clinics delivering care in it. The obligations attached to those two are not comparable. How to tell them apart, and what each owes you, is set out in which door, and what it must do. The parallel vocabulary problem on the vendor side is in what vendors actually mean.

What to check on your own plan

Is it a Marketplace plan? The ten categories attach to Marketplace coverage. Other arrangements are governed by other rules, and "covered services" in those documents is a narrower promise.

What is the cost-sharing inside the category you will use? Inclusion in a category and affordability within it are separate questions.

Which entities will bill you? A covered service delivered by an out-of-network entity is charged differently, and at a hospital more than one entity bills. The reason those words are kept separate in plan documents is explained in a legal term, not a synonym.

Is the limit a category exclusion or a plan limit? A refusal because something falls outside the ten and a refusal because a plan applied a visit cap are different disputes with different routes; those routes are mapped in who pays your claim.

Questions people actually ask

What counts as an essential health benefit?

Ten categories. Ambulatory patient services; emergency services; hospitalization; pregnancy, maternity and newborn care; mental health and substance use disorder services including behavioral health treatment; prescription drugs; rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices; laboratory services; preventive and wellness services and chronic disease management; and pediatric services including oral and vision care.

Does every health plan have to cover all ten?

Marketplace plans do. HealthCare.gov states that all Marketplace plans must cover the ten essential health benefit categories, and separately that plans must provide birth control coverage and breastfeeding coverage. Plans obtained outside the Marketplace are governed by different rules, which is why the phrase covered services means something narrower there.

Does covered mean free?

No. The ten categories define what a plan must include, not what it costs you. Deductibles, copayments and coinsurance still apply within each category, and network status still determines your rate. A benefit can be an essential health benefit and still generate a large bill.

What is NAICS 62?

The industry sector called Health Care and Social Assistance, which BLS describes as comprising establishments providing health care and social assistance for individuals. It has four subsectors: Ambulatory Health Care Services (621), Hospitals (622), Nursing and Residential Care Facilities (623), and Social Assistance (624).

How many people work in health care services?

BLS put employment in the Health Care and Social Assistance sector at about 23.9 million as of July 2026, on preliminary data. That figure covers all four subsectors, including social assistance, which is why it is larger than counts limited to clinical settings.

Why do the coverage list and the industry list not line up?

Because they were built for different purposes. The benefit categories classify what a plan must pay for; the industry codes classify where establishments sit for statistical reporting. Prescription drugs are an essential health benefit but pharmacies are classified in retail, not in sector 62, so a covered service can be delivered entirely outside the health care industry.

What should I check on my own plan?

Check three things in order: whether the plan is a Marketplace plan, since that determines whether the ten categories apply; what the cost-sharing is inside each category you expect to use; and the network status of the specific entities that will bill you, since a covered service delivered out of network is charged differently.
