The Health Insurance Marketplace Call Center is 1-800-318-2596, TTY 1-855-889-4325, open 24 hours a day, seven days a week except holidays.

That number is the right answer to a narrower question than most people are asking. It covers individual and family Marketplace coverage in the 30 states that use HealthCare.gov. Twenty states and the District of Columbia run their own marketplace with its own call centre and its own deadlines, and six adjacent questions — employers, Medicare, Medicaid, children's coverage, enrolment records, fraud — each go somewhere else. The table below routes all of them.

The number, and the two things it cannot do

HealthCare.gov publishes two call centres, not one. The Marketplace Call Center handles individuals and families. The SHOP Call Center, 1-800-706-7893, TTY 1-888-201-6445, handles small employers and their employees, which is a separate programme with separate rules.

Two limits are stated by HealthCare.gov itself and are worth committing to memory. Neither call centre can accept questions or documents by email or fax. There is no official email address to send a document to. That single fact resolves a large share of the impersonation attempts described later on this page, because the fake version almost always asks you to email something.

The second limit is jurisdictional, and it is the reason people report the number "not working".

The fork most callers miss

There is no single national marketplace. For plan year 2026, states fall into three groups, and the group decides which website holds your application and which call centre can see it.

The split is not marginal. For the 2026 plan year, CMS reported 23.0 million plan selections nationally: 15.8 million through HealthCare.gov across those 30 states and 7.2 million through the state-based marketplaces across 20 states and DC. Roughly 31 per cent of all Marketplace enrolees are therefore people for whom the federal number is the wrong number.

If you are in one of the 21 state-based jurisdictions, the federal call centre cannot look up your application, change your plan or take your document, because it does not hold your record. It can tell you which marketplace does. Starting at HealthCare.gov still works as a routing step; it will send you to your state's own site.

When the Marketplace is the wrong department

Marketplace and Medicare are separate programmes with separate call centres, and the boundary between them is where a large share of misrouted calls land, usually around the age of 65.

Medicare is 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227), TTY 1-877-486-2048, available 24 hours a day, seven days a week except some federal holidays. Signing up for Medicare, and any question about your Social Security record behind it, goes to the Social Security Administration on 1-800-772-1213.

There is also a free counselling service that most people have never heard of: the State Health Insurance Assistance Programs, which Medicare directs beneficiaries to at shiphelp.org. It is independent, free, and staffed by people whose job is one-to-one help rather than call handling.

Medicaid and CHIP go through your state

Medicaid is state-administered, so there is no single national Medicaid phone number. Medicare.gov points people to Medicaid.gov for state-by-state contact details, and that is the correct route.

For children specifically there is a national front door: Insure Kids Now, 1-877-KIDS-NOW (1-877-543-7669), which routes callers to their own state's Medicaid or CHIP programme. If you are unsure whether your household qualifies, that number is a faster first call than the Marketplace line, because a Marketplace application that turns out to be a Medicaid case gets referred to the state anyway.

The dates that decide whether calling helps

Outside that window you generally need a qualifying life event to enrol, which is a documented change rather than a decision. The 2026 cycle gives a sense of the traffic behind those dates: of the 23.0 million plan selections, 3.4 million were new consumers and 19.6 million were returning — people who either actively re-selected or were automatically re-enrolled. The returning majority is why mid-December is the worst week of the year to need a long call, and why checking an auto-renewal in early November is the cheapest possible use of the line.

The free in-person route people forget

Phone is not the only official channel, and for a complicated household it is often the worst one. Trained assisters — navigators, certified application counsellors, and licensed agents and brokers — provide help with Marketplace applications, and the federal platform publishes a locator for finding them locally.

The distinction worth knowing is that navigators and certified application counsellors are required to be impartial, while agents and brokers may be paid by issuers for enrolments. All are legitimate; they are simply not the same relationship. If someone offering help declines to say which category they fall into, that is itself the answer.

Telling a real call from an impersonation

Health coverage is a favoured setting for government impersonation because the caller can reference a real deadline and a real programme. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance is categorical about what agencies do not do: they do not call, email, text or message you on social media to ask for money or personal information; they do not demand payment by wire transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency or payment app; and they do not threaten arrest or the suspension of your benefits. Its shortest instruction is the most useful: don't trust your caller ID, which can be faked.

The HHS Office of Inspector General has published an alert about scammers spoofing its own hotline numbers, and its advice inverts the polite instinct: do not verify your name or any other personal information to an unexpected caller. Not your Social Security number, date of birth, card details, driving licence number, bank details or mother's maiden name — and not confirmation of information the caller already claims to hold.

Combine that with the email rule from the top of this page and you have a two-question test that resolves nearly every case. Did they contact you first? Are they asking you to email a document or make a payment in an unusual form? Hang up, then dial a number you looked up yourself from the table above. A genuine case survives the callback; an impersonation does not.

The same logic applies to unsolicited calls about equipment and supplies, where federal rules restrict cold contact outright — covered in our guide to vetting a medical equipment supplier.

Where to report it

Suspected fraud against Medicare, Medicaid or the Marketplace goes to the HHS Office of Inspector General hotline, 1-800-HHS-TIPS (1-800-447-8477), TTY 1-800-377-4950, fax 1-800-223-8164, or by post to OIG Hotline Operations, PO Box 23489, Washington, DC 20026. Medicare Advantage and Part D matters go to I-MEDIC on 1-877-7SAFERX (1-877-772-3379).

Everything else goes to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or 1-877-382-4357, plus your state attorney general. If personal information was actually handed over, IdentityTheft.gov is the recovery route rather than the reporting one, and it is worth using the same day.

When you report, include the date, the time and what was said. Those three details are what make a report usable rather than merely counted.

What is behind the number

One last piece of context, because it changes what you should expect from the call. The Marketplace call centre is a federal contact centre operated for CMS. It can see and change your Marketplace application. It cannot change your plan's benefits, adjudicate a claim, or overrule your insurer, because those decisions belong to the entity carrying the coverage — and that entity is not always the company whose name is on your card. Which body actually holds that power, and which regulator hears a complaint about it, is set out in our guide to who pays your claim. The wider structure sits in how US health care actually works.

Questions people actually ask

What is the phone number for HealthCare.gov?

The Marketplace Call Center is 1-800-318-2596, TTY 1-855-889-4325, available 24 hours a day, seven days a week except holidays. It handles individual and family coverage. Employers and their employees use the separate SHOP Call Center on 1-800-706-7893, TTY 1-888-201-6445.

Why does the HealthCare.gov number not work for my state?

Because 20 states and the District of Columbia run their own State-Based Marketplace with their own call centre and their own deadlines. For plan year 2026, 30 states used HealthCare.gov: 27 through the federally-facilitated marketplace and three — Arkansas, Oklahoma and Oregon — as state-based marketplaces on the federal platform. If you live in one of the other 21 jurisdictions, the federal line cannot see your application.

Can I email documents to HealthCare.gov instead of calling?

No. HealthCare.gov states that it cannot accept questions or documents by email or fax, for either the Marketplace Call Center or the SHOP Call Center. This is worth remembering because it makes any message claiming to be an official marketplace channel and asking you to email documents an immediate warning sign.

What is the phone number for Medicare, and is it the same?

It is a different number and a different programme. Medicare is 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227), TTY 1-877-486-2048, available 24 hours a day, seven days a week except some federal holidays. The Marketplace call centre cannot answer Medicare questions and Medicare cannot answer Marketplace ones.

Who do I call about Medicaid or CHIP?

Your state, because Medicaid and CHIP are state-administered. State Medicaid contact details are published at Medicaid.gov. For children's coverage there is a national help line, Insure Kids Now, on 1-877-KIDS-NOW (1-877-543-7669), which routes callers to their state programme.

When is the Marketplace open enrollment period?

On HealthCare.gov, open enrollment starts 1 November and ends 15 January. Enrolling or changing plans by 15 December gives coverage starting 1 January; enrolling between 16 December and 15 January gives coverage starting 1 February. State-Based Marketplaces set their own deadlines, which is another reason to confirm which marketplace serves you.

How do I know a call about my health coverage is genuine?

Assume it is not, hang up, and dial a number you looked up yourself. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance is that government agencies do not call, email, text or message you on social media to ask for money or personal information, do not demand payment by wire transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency or payment app, and do not threaten arrest or suspension of benefits. Its shortest instruction is not to trust caller ID, which can be faked.
