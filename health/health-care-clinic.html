Health Care Clinic: The Word Has Four Legal Forms

Last verified
9 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Data sources
42 CFR 400.202; CMS FQHC guidance 2026; HRSA
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

"Clinic" is an everyday word attached to at least four different legal positions, and the one a site holds decides what it owes you and what you pay.

Medicare's own definition is startlingly narrow: under 42 CFR 400.202 a clinic counts as a provider only for outpatient physical therapy or speech pathology. A federally qualified health center is a different designation entirely, paid on an encounter rate whose 2026 base is $207.72 and carrying a sliding-fee obligation. A private urgent care clinic usually holds no federal designation at all.

Why this search returns one city

The results for this phrase are dominated by New York institutions and municipal programmes. That is not an error in those pages; large urban systems optimise hard for generic clinical terms, and a national query ends up with a metropolitan answer.

It is unhelpful for anyone searching from anywhere else, though, and it obscures the thing that actually transfers between places: the categories. A clinic in Ohio and a clinic in Arizona can hold the same federal designation, and that designation predicts more about cost and obligation than any local branding does. This page sets out the categories and names no facility.

What Medicare means by "clinic"

Most people assume "clinic" is a general category in federal rules. It is not.

42 CFR 400.202 defines provider as a specific list: a hospital, a critical access hospital, a skilled nursing facility, a comprehensive outpatient rehabilitation facility, a home health agency, a hospice, and — for defined services only — a clinic, rehabilitation agency or public health agency for outpatient physical therapy or speech pathology services, plus a community mental health centre for partial hospitalisation services.

Read the qualifier carefully. A clinic is on the provider list, but only in respect of two service types. A walk-in medical clinic treating a sore throat is not a "provider" in that sense; the physician working inside it is a supplier, since 400.202 defines supplier as a physician or other practitioner, or an entity other than a provider, that furnishes health care services. Why those two words are kept apart across federal rulebooks is set out in a legal term, not a synonym.

The fourth row is the one most likely to be the place nearest to you, and it is the one with no federal obligations attached. That is not an accusation — plenty of excellent care happens there — but it means the questions you can ask before arriving are different, and the answers are contractual rather than regulatory.

The designation that changes the price

CMS describes federally qualified health centers as safety net providers that provide services in an outpatient clinic setting, operating in rural or urban locations and including community health centers, migrant health centers, homeless health centers, and tribal or urban Indian organisations.

Four routes qualify an organisation. Receiving a grant under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act. Being a HRSA-designated look-alike, meeting all programme requirements without the federal funding. Being treated as a comprehensively funded health center since 1 January 1990. Or operating as a tribal or urban Indian organisation under federal Indian health law.

The look-alike route is the one worth knowing about, because it means a site can carry the full obligation set without receiving grant money — so "is it funded" and "is it an FQHC" are different questions with potentially different answers.

The list of what the designation covers is broader than the word "clinic" suggests, too. CMS names community health centers, migrant health centers and homeless health centers inside the same category, which means a site set up for a specific population is operating under the same payment and obligation rules as a general outpatient centre. Somebody who has been told they do not qualify for "a clinic" because their circumstances are unusual is frequently being told something about one private site rather than about the category.

What the designation does not do is guarantee that a particular service is available at a particular location. Designations attach to organisations and to the obligations they carry; scope of service is a local operational question. So the two things worth establishing before travelling are separate: whether the site holds the designation, and whether it does the thing you need.

What the encounter rate means for you

FQHCs are paid on a prospective per-visit basis rather than per item. CMS states: We pay claims at 80% of the lesser of the FQHC charges or the FQHC PPS rate for the specific payment code. The base rate for 2026 is $207.72 per encounter, with coinsurance at 20 per cent except for certain preventive services where cost sharing is waived.

Applied to the base rate, the arithmetic is exact and worth seeing:

Medicare's 80 per cent share is $166.18. The patient's 20 per cent coinsurance is $41.54. Together they reconstruct the $207.72 base rate.

Two features follow that are unusual in United States health billing. The unit is the encounter, not the itemised service, so the number of things done during a visit does not multiply the rate. And CMS pays the lesser of the charge or the rate, which caps the programme's exposure at the published figure. Neither is true of ordinary fee-for-service billing, where the itemisation is exactly what drives the total.

What comes attached to the designation

CMS lists the conditions as serving medically underserved populations, offering sliding-scale fees for lower-income patients, and maintaining board governance with patient representation.

HRSA states the parallel programme requirements for the centres it funds: they must have a governing board where a majority of its members are patients, serve everyone, even if they cannot pay, and adjust their fees based on income and family size.

That second obligation is the practically decisive one and it is not conditional on insurance. It is a grant or designation condition, not a discount policy, and it does not depend on the individual site's goodwill. Where those centres sit relative to every other door — and how far up the income range the sliding scale reaches — is set out in which door, and what it must do.

The category with nothing attached

A private clinic or urgent care site with no federal designation owes you no screening, no sliding scale and no published encounter rate. Whether it accepts your insurance is a contracting question, and what you pay if it does not is governed by your plan's out-of-network terms rather than by any regulation specific to clinics.

Two protections still reach into that space. Emergency services carry the surprise-billing protection regardless of network status, and so do out-of-network clinicians billing from inside an in-network facility. Where those lines fall is examined in two meanings, one search box. Everything else in the undesignated category is priced by contract, which is why asking about cost before the visit is a reasonable and answerable question there in a way it often is not elsewhere.

Checking before you book

One structural point ties the four rows together. The federal categories were built to answer a payment question — who may bill which programme, at what rate, under what conditions — and not to describe what a building looks like from the pavement. That is why a site with a large sign reading "clinic" may hold no designation while a small storefront practice holds a significant one. Nothing about the frontage carries the information, and the designation is the part that survives a change of ownership or a rebrand.

Which designation, if any? An FQHC or HRSA-funded centre carries a sliding-fee obligation. An undesignated clinic does not.

Is the encounter rate relevant to you? The $207.72 base applies to Medicare FQHC claims, not to every patient at every site.

Who will bill you? The site and the clinicians inside it can have separate network status, which is a distinct question from designation.

Is what you need actually clinical? Coverage disputes and records requests route to agencies rather than clinics; those channels are mapped in route before you search, and the wider structure in how the system actually works. For care delivered at home rather than at a site, the certification and rating questions are different again — set out in what certification actually buys.

Questions people actually ask

Does clinic mean anything in federal law?

Something much narrower than people expect. Under 42 CFR 400.202 a clinic counts as a Medicare provider only for outpatient physical therapy or speech pathology services. Every other kind of clinic is either a differently designated entity, such as a federally qualified health center, or has no federal designation at all.

What is a federally qualified health center?

CMS describes FQHCs as safety net providers that provide services in an outpatient clinic setting. They qualify by receiving a grant under section 330 of the Public Health Service Act, by being a HRSA-designated look-alike meeting the programme requirements without the funding, by being treated as a comprehensively funded health center since 1 January 1990, or as a tribal or urban Indian organisation under federal Indian health law.

How much does Medicare pay an FQHC per visit?

CMS pays claims at 80 per cent of the lesser of the FQHC's charges or the FQHC prospective payment system rate for the payment code. The base rate for 2026 is $207.72 per encounter. On that base rate, Medicare's 80 per cent share is $166.18 and the 20 per cent coinsurance is $41.54, with cost sharing waived for certain preventive services.

What obligations come with the FQHC designation?

CMS lists serving medically underserved populations, offering sliding-scale fees for lower-income patients, and maintaining board governance with patient representation. HRSA states the parallel programme requirements: a governing board where a majority of members are patients, serving everyone even if they cannot pay, and adjusting fees based on income and family size.

Is an urgent care clinic a designated entity?

Generally not. A private urgent care clinic typically holds no federal designation of the kinds described here, which means no sliding-fee obligation, no duty to see you, and no federal encounter rate. Its price and its acceptance of your insurance are contractual matters rather than regulatory ones.

Why are the results for this search all from one city?

Because large urban health systems and municipal programmes optimise heavily for it, so a national query returns a metropolitan answer. Nothing on those pages is wrong; it simply describes one city's institutions rather than the categories that exist everywhere, which is what a person searching from elsewhere needs.

What should I check before booking?

Which designation the site holds, because that determines whether a sliding fee scale applies and whether an encounter rate governs the bill. Then whether the entities billing you are in your plan's network, since a designation controls obligations rather than your cost-sharing.
