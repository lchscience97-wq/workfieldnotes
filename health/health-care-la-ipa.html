Health Care LA IPA: What an IPA Is, and Who Stays Responsible

The searchable part of this phrase is the last three letters. An IPA — independent practice association — is a contracting layer between independent physicians and health plans. It is not a clinic and not an insurer, and most patients whose care runs through one never learn it exists.

It matters for one reason. A plan may delegate activities to an IPA, but under 42 CFR 422.504(i)(1) the plan maintains ultimate responsibility regardless. So when a decision comes back from a name you do not recognise, the accountable party is still the plan.

A layer, not a party to your coverage

Your coverage contract is with a plan. The plan builds a network. Between those two facts sits a set of contracting arrangements that the enrolment paperwork rarely names.

An IPA is one of them: an organisation that contracts with independent practices on one side and with plans on the other, so that a plan can reach a large number of small practices through one agreement and the practices can reach plans without negotiating individually.

Nothing about that is improper, and it is why the phrase turns up in searches at all — a name appears on a card, a letter or a referral, and it belongs to none of the parties the patient thought were involved. Our guide to two meanings of network in one search box covers the adjacent confusion.

The federal vocabulary for the layer

Medicare Advantage regulation names these arrangements precisely, and the definitions at 42 CFR 422.2 are the ones to know.

A first tier entity is any party that enters into a written arrangement, acceptable to CMS, with an MA organization or applicant to provide administrative services or health care services. That is where an IPA contracting directly with a plan normally sits.

A downstream entity is any party that enters into a written arrangement, acceptable to CMS, with persons or entities involved with the MA benefit, below the level of the arrangement between an MA organization and a first tier entity. A practice contracting with the IPA rather than with the plan is downstream.

A related entity is any entity that is related to the MA organization by common ownership or control that performs management functions, furnishes services, or leases property or sells materials exceeding $2,500 during a contract period.

Read together, those three describe a chain rather than a pair. The regulation assumes several layers and writes rules that reach down all of them.

Two features of the definitions are worth noticing because they are unusually inclusive. First tier status turns on entering a written arrangement to provide administrative services or health care services — so a claims processor and a physician group can occupy the same category, which is why the term surprises people who expect it to mean clinicians. And the related entity test is triggered by ownership or control plus ordinary commercial dealing above a low dollar threshold, which means the chain captures arrangements that look purely operational from outside. The drafting is deliberately broad because the purpose is to prevent a plan's obligations from disappearing into a contracting structure.

Delegation is not transfer

This is the sentence worth carrying away from the whole subject.

Under 42 CFR 422.504(i)(1), the MA organization maintains ultimate responsibility for adhering to and otherwise fully complying with all terms of its contract with CMS — regardless of any relationship with a first tier, downstream or related entity.

An IPA can be handed work. It cannot be handed the plan's accountability. When an authorisation is refused by an entity you have no contract with, the answer to who is responsible for this is not complicated: the plan is.

That principle resolves a specific and common frustration. A patient calls the plan and is told the matter sits with a delegated group; the group says it operates under the plan's rules. Both statements can be true and neither is an answer. The regulation settles the question by locating ultimate responsibility in one place and leaving it there no matter how the work was distributed, which means the plan cannot correctly treat a delegate's decision as somebody else's problem.

What the contract must contain

The delegation is not informal either. 42 CFR 422.504(i)(3) requires all contracts or written arrangements between MA organizations and first tier, downstream and related entities to contain specified provisions, and (i)(3)(ii) requires accountability provisions indicating that the MA organization may only delegate activities or functions consistent with the requirements that follow.

Two of those requirements are the ones a patient can reason from. Under (i)(4)(ii), each contract must either provide for revocation of the delegation activities and reporting requirements or specify alternative remedies where performance is unsatisfactory. Under (i)(4)(iii), each contract must specify that the performance of the parties is monitored by the MA organization on an ongoing basis.

So a plan that says a delegated entity handled your case is describing an arrangement it is required to monitor and empowered to revoke. That is a different posture from a plan that has simply passed you along.

What does not move

Contracting layers do not dilute the plan's access obligations, which sit at 42 CFR 422.112.

An MA organization must maintain and monitor a network of appropriate providers that is supported by written agreements, sufficient for the population served. Where in-network options are unavailable, it must arrange for and cover any medically necessary covered benefit outside the plan provider network, but at in-network cost sharing.

The regulation also fixes appointment standards — emergency and urgent care immediately, medical attention within 7 business days, routine and preventive within 30 business days — requires hours of operation convenient to the population served, and requires plan services to be available 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, when medically necessary.

On continuity, an initial assessment of each enrollee's health care needs is expected within 90 days of enrolment, and enrollees new to a plan and in active treatment get a minimum 90-day transition period.

What to do when an unfamiliar name appears

Ask the plan three questions in writing. Is this entity a first tier, downstream or related entity under 42 CFR 422.2? Which functions has the plan delegated to it? And who made the decision in my case — the plan or the delegated entity?

Then route the appeal through the plan. Naming the entity is useful, because the plan is required to monitor its performance on an ongoing basis and to hold a contract that provides for revocation or alternative remedies. But the appeal deadlines and the review machinery belong to the plan, and our guides to which regulator is yours and what comes after the call set those out.

What this does not settle

It does not tell you whether a particular IPA is well run; that is not a question federal contracting categories answer. It does not tell you how the IPA is paid, which may be capitation or fee-for-service and which the plan is not obliged to publish to enrollees.

And it does not apply identically outside Medicare Advantage. The definitions quoted here come from Part 422, so a commercial plan's delegation arrangements are governed by its own contracts and by state insurance regulation rather than by these provisions.

What it does settle is the question people actually arrive with. An unfamiliar organisation in the middle of your care is not a dead end and not a separate insurer. It is a delegate, and the entity that delegated to it is the one that answers for the result.

Questions people actually ask

What is an IPA?

An independent practice association is an organisation that contracts with independent physicians and practices on one side and with health plans on the other. It is a contracting layer rather than a clinic or an insurer. In Medicare Advantage terms it usually sits as a first tier entity, which 42 CFR 422.2 defines as any party that enters into a written arrangement, acceptable to CMS, with an MA organization or applicant to provide administrative services or health care services.

Is an IPA my insurer?

No. Your coverage contract is with the plan. The IPA is an entity the plan has contracted with, and possibly delegated functions to. That distinction decides where a complaint or appeal goes, because the plan remains the party accountable to CMS regardless of what it has delegated.

Can a plan hand its responsibilities to an IPA?

It can delegate activities, but not accountability. Under 42 CFR 422.504(i)(1) the MA organization maintains ultimate responsibility for adhering to and otherwise fully complying with all terms of its contract with CMS, regardless of any relationship with a first tier, downstream or related entity.

What must be in the contract between a plan and an IPA?

Specified provisions. 42 CFR 422.504(i)(3) requires all contracts or written arrangements between MA organizations and first tier, downstream and related entities to contain them, including accountability provisions at (i)(3)(ii) limiting what may be delegated. Under (i)(4)(ii) each contract must provide for revocation of the delegated activities or specify alternative remedies where performance is unsatisfactory, and under (i)(4)(iii) it must specify that performance is monitored by the MA organization on an ongoing basis.

What are downstream and related entities?

A downstream entity is defined at 42 CFR 422.2 as any party that enters into a written arrangement, acceptable to CMS, with persons or entities involved with the MA benefit, below the level of the arrangement between an MA organization and a first tier entity. A related entity is an entity related to the MA organization by common ownership or control that performs management functions, furnishes services, or leases property or sells materials exceeding $2,500 during a contract period.

Does using an IPA change my access rights?

The plan's access obligations do not move. Under 42 CFR 422.112 an MA organization must maintain and monitor a network of appropriate providers supported by written agreements, and must arrange for and cover any medically necessary covered benefit outside the network at in-network cost sharing where in-network options are unavailable. Those duties belong to the plan whatever contracting layers sit beneath it.

Where do I complain about an IPA decision?

Start with the plan, because the plan holds ultimate responsibility under 42 CFR 422.504(i)(1) and holds the contract that must specify monitoring and revocation. Naming the IPA in the complaint is useful, since the plan is required to monitor that entity's performance on an ongoing basis, but the appeal route runs through the plan rather than around it.
