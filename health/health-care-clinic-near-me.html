Health Care Clinic Near Me: The Estimate You Can Demand

Every result for this search is a clinic selling proximity. None of them leads with price, because price is the thing the format is worst at.

There is a federal answer to that. If you are uninsured, or insured and choosing not to use your coverage, a provider must give you a written good faith estimate of expected charges before a scheduled service — and must give you one on request even if you schedule nothing. If the bill later comes in at least $400 above it, a dispute process opens. This page sets out the deadlines, the required contents, and how to ask.

What the results are selling

The first page for this term is clinic groups and booking platforms. Their offer is real: proximity, availability, and a short wait. It is also the whole of what the format communicates.

Which door you pick has consequences beyond convenience — a federally qualified health centre owes you a sliding fee discount, an emergency department owes you a screening examination, an independent urgent care owes you neither. Our guide to the four legal forms of the word clinic covers those distinctions, and which door and what it must do compares the routes. This page takes the narrower question the results never answer: what will it cost, and can you find out first.

The document you can ask for

45 CFR 149.610 requires providers and facilities to furnish a good faith estimate of expected charges to uninsured (or self-pay) individuals.

Two things in that phrase do more work than people expect. Uninsured is obvious. Self-pay covers someone who has coverage but does not want a claim submitted for that item or service — a choice, not only a status. And the obligation is not limited to scheduled care: an estimate must also be produced on request.

The three deadlines

The timing rules at 149.610(b)(1)(vi) run off two triggers, and both are business days.

Where the item or service is scheduled at least 3 business days in advance, the estimate is due not later than 1 business day after the date of scheduling. Where it is scheduled at least 10 business days in advance, it is due not later than 3 business days after the date of scheduling. And on request, without scheduling, 149.610(b)(1)(vi)(C) gives not later than 3 business days after the date of the request.

Notice the shape: the further ahead you book, the longer the provider has, because the estimate is harder to assemble for complex care. The request route is the one most people never use, and it is the one that lets you compare two clinics before committing to either.

What must be in it

149.610(c)(1) lists the required contents, and the list is more specific than most people expect from an estimate.

It must carry the patient's name and date of birth; a clear description of the primary item or service; an itemised list grouped by provider or facility; diagnosis and service codes with expected charges; provider identifiers and locations; a list of items or services that require separate scheduling; disclaimers including your dispute resolution rights; and a statement that the estimate is not a binding contract.

The codes are the part worth insisting on. A single figure is hard to check and harder to compare; a coded, itemised list can be read against another clinic's list line by line, and it tells you which parts of the visit are being billed separately.

The separate-scheduling line deserves the same attention. A visit that looks like one appointment is often several billable encounters — the consultation, the laboratory work, the imaging, the reading of that imaging — and the requirement to list items that need separate scheduling is what forces those out into the open before rather than after. A quoted price that omits them is not wrong so much as incomplete, and the incompleteness is exactly what the itemisation rule is written to prevent.

They must tell you it exists

The obligation is not purely reactive. Under 149.610(b)(1)(iii), providers must inform uninsured or self-pay individuals of the availability of a good faith estimate through their website, office signage and oral notification when scheduling, in accessible formats and in the languages spoken by the individuals they serve.

That converts a silent clinic into evidence. If nothing on the site, nothing on the wall and nothing on the phone has ever mentioned an estimate, the notification requirement is not being met, and asking directly is entirely reasonable.

The $400 line

An estimate with no consequence would be decoration. The consequence sits in 45 CFR 149.620.

The regulation defines substantially in excess, at 149.620(a)(2)(ii), as total billed charges by a provider or facility that are at least $400 more than the total expected charges listed on the good faith estimate for that provider or facility.

Where that threshold is crossed, 149.620(c)(1) allows the individual to initiate patient-provider dispute resolution by submitting notification postmarked within 120 calendar days of receiving the initial bill. A selected dispute resolution entity then makes a determination regarding the amount to be paid, under 149.620(f)(2)(ii). An administrative fee applies, set by the Secretary through guidance under 149.620(g)(2).

The 120 days runs from receipt of the bill, not from the date of service. Keep the envelope.

Self-pay is a decision

The most under-used part of this framework is that self-pay status can be chosen. Someone with coverage who decides not to have a claim submitted for a particular item or service falls inside the definition and can ask for the estimate.

That is not automatically the right choice: amounts you pay yourself generally will not count toward a deductible or out-of-pocket maximum, so the calculation depends on where you are in the plan year. Our guide to the dial nobody caps sets out how those figures interact. The point here is narrower — the option exists, and with it the right to a written number in advance.

When a different door has a better price rule

An estimate tells you what a clinic intends to charge. It does not lower the charge. Two other doors have rules that do.

A federally qualified health centre must maintain a schedule of fees together with a corresponding schedule of discounts adjusted on the basis of the patient's ability to pay, under 42 U.S.C. §254b(k)(3)(G). That is a discount, not an estimate, and it is means-tested rather than universal. Our guide to what the federal health centre test is explains how to tell whether the place near you qualifies.

For anyone whose real question is which type of place to use rather than what one place charges, the federal facility list sets out the categories.

What the estimate does not do

It does not bind anyone; the regulation requires the document to say so. It does not cover care you did not know you would need, which is precisely the care that produces surprising bills. It does not apply when you are using your insurance, where the relevant protections are the balance billing rules instead.

What it does is convert an unknowable into a written, coded, dated document with a dispute threshold attached. For a routine visit at a clinic you found by searching, that is the difference between hoping and checking — and it costs one phone call placed three business days early.

Questions people actually ask

Can I get a price before I go to a clinic?

If you are uninsured, or insured but choosing not to use your coverage for the visit, yes. 45 CFR 149.610 requires providers and facilities to give uninsured or self-pay individuals a written good faith estimate of expected charges for scheduled items and services, and to give one on request even without scheduling anything.

How quickly must the estimate arrive?

Under 45 CFR 149.610(b)(1)(vi), not later than 1 business day after the date of scheduling when the service is scheduled at least 3 business days ahead, and not later than 3 business days after the date of scheduling when it is scheduled at least 10 business days ahead. If you simply ask for one without scheduling, it must arrive not later than 3 business days after the date of the request.

What has to be in a good faith estimate?

Under 45 CFR 149.610(c)(1) it must include the patient's name and date of birth, a clear description of the primary item or service, an itemised list grouped by provider or facility, diagnosis and service codes with expected charges, provider identifiers and locations, a list of items that require separate scheduling, disclaimers including your dispute resolution rights, and a statement that the estimate is not a binding contract.

Does the clinic have to tell me the estimate exists?

Yes. 45 CFR 149.610(b)(1)(iii) requires providers to inform uninsured or self-pay individuals of the availability of a good faith estimate through their website, office signage and oral notification when scheduling, in accessible formats and in the languages spoken by the individuals they serve. A clinic that has never mentioned it is not meeting that requirement.

What if the bill comes in higher than the estimate?

There is a formal dispute route once the gap is large enough. 45 CFR 149.620(a)(2)(ii) defines substantially in excess as at least $400 more than the total expected charges on the good faith estimate for that provider or facility. Under 149.620(c)(1) the individual must submit notification postmarked within 120 calendar days of receiving the initial bill, and a selected dispute resolution entity then determines the amount to be paid.

Is the estimate binding?

No, and it says so on its face: one of the required contents is a statement that the estimate is not a binding contract. Its force is indirect. Because a bill at least $400 above it opens a dispute process, the estimate sets the reference point against which the eventual charge is measured, which is a different and more useful thing than a promise.

Does this apply if I am using insurance?

The good faith estimate provisions in this subpart apply to uninsured or self-pay individuals, and self-pay includes someone who has coverage but does not want a claim submitted for the item or service. If you are using your coverage, the relevant protections are different ones, including the balance billing rules and your plan's own appeal process.
