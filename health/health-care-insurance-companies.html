Health Care Insurance Companies: Who Pays Your Claim

There is no single kind of health care insurance company. The phrase covers four structurally different entities, and the one whose name appears on your card is frequently not the one whose money pays your claims.

In a self-insured plan the employer pays the claims and the named company is a contracted administrator. AHRQ's employer survey put 57.7 per cent of private-sector enrollees in that arrangement in 2020, and 76.9 per cent at firms of 1,000 or more. Which of the four you hold decides who can overturn a denial, which regulator will take a complaint, and what happens if the money runs out.

Four entities, one phrase

Search this term and the results are dominated by the companies themselves and by comparison pages ranking them. Neither format has any reason to explain the distinction that matters most, because it is the same distinction that determines who you can hold responsible.

The same brand name can occupy all four rows simultaneously in different lines of business. That is why "who is my insurance company" is a question with no useful answer until you have established which arrangement you are actually in.

The issuer: the only one that is literally insuring you

An issuer is the licensed entity that accepts the financial risk. It collects a premium, promises to pay covered claims, and holds reserves against that promise. State insurance departments license it, examine its solvency, and take consumer complaints about it.

Two consumer protections attach to this structure and to no other. The first is the Affordable Care Act's medical loss ratio rule: an issuer must spend at least 80 or 85 per cent of premium dollars on medical care and quality improvement, depending on the market segment, and since 2012 an issuer that misses the applicable standard has to rebate the shortfall to its customers.

The second is insolvency protection. Every state, along with the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, maintains a guaranty association that pays covered claims when an insurer fails, subject to limits written into each state's statute. Both protections follow the policy, not the brand. Neither reaches the self-funded plan described next.

When the employer is the insurer and the company is a contractor

HealthCare.gov defines a self-insured plan as one where "the employer itself collects premiums from enrollees and takes on the responsibility of paying employees' and dependents' medical claims", noting that such employers commonly contract with third-party administrators for enrollment and claims processing.

Nothing on the membership card announces this. The card carries the administrator's logo, the administrator's network, the administrator's member services number. The administrator adjudicates the claim. But the money is the employer's, the benefit rules are the employer's, and the final say on an internal appeal is usually the employer's too.

This is not a niche arrangement. Two independent federal datasets, built on different methods, put it near or above half of the employer market.

The firm-size gradient is the practical finding. Between the smallest and largest employer categories the gap is 62.5 percentage points. If you work for a large employer, the working assumption should be that your employer is your insurer and the familiar name on the card is a vendor.

Two consequences follow. Complaints about a self-funded plan go to the US Department of Labor under ERISA, not to the state insurance department, which is the single most common misrouting in this area. And state insurance mandates — the rules requiring coverage of particular treatments — generally do not bind a self-funded plan, because it is not an insurance policy issued in the state.

When the company is running a public programme

The third structure is a private company operating a government benefit under contract: Medicare Advantage, Medicare Part D drug coverage, and Medicaid managed care, where a state Medicaid agency pays a company a fixed monthly amount per member to deliver benefits.

Here the company is neither the risk-bearer in the ordinary sense nor a pure administrator. It operates inside a contract and a rulebook it did not write, and its appeals process is prescribed by federal programme rules rather than by state insurance law. The regulator is CMS, and for Medicaid plans the state Medicaid agency as well.

The scale is easy to underestimate because the spending is classified as public even when a private company handles it.

Companies appear inside the Medicare and Medicaid rows too, as contractors, and their obligations there come from a different rulebook.

The fourth kind, which cannot pay anything

Brokers, agents, comparison sites and lead-generation pages all present themselves in the vocabulary of insurance companies. They carry no risk and decide nothing about a claim. Licensed agents and brokers are regulated by state insurance departments; unlicensed lead generators that merely collect and sell contact details are a different category again.

One structural fact is worth memorising because impersonators exploit it: the federal Marketplace call centre cannot accept questions or documents by email or fax. Any message claiming to be an official marketplace channel and asking you to email documents is not one. The official contact routes, and how to check a caller against them, are set out in our guide to the official HealthCare.gov contact lines.

Working out which one you have, from documents you already hold

Four checks, in order of speed.

The card. Wording such as "administered by" or "claims administered by" next to the company name points to a self-funded plan. A card naming the employer's plan rather than a product name points the same way.

The plan document. An employer plan issues a summary plan description and a statement of ERISA rights. A fully insured product issues a policy or certificate of coverage. The two are different legal instruments, and which one you were given is the cleanest single test available to an employee.

The appeal letter. A denial that routes the final internal appeal to a plan administrator or benefits committee is describing a self-funded plan. A denial that offers external review under state law is describing an insured one.

The regulator named in the fine print. Notices that point you to the Department of Labor indicate ERISA. Notices that point you to a state insurance department indicate an issuer.

Where the premium goes before anyone pays a claim

For employer coverage, the sticker price and the visible price differ sharply. AHRQ's employer survey put the 2024 average total premium at $8,486 for single coverage, of which the employee contributed $1,789 — a 21.1 per cent share. Family coverage averaged $24,540, with an employee contribution of $7,216, or 29.4 per cent.

The remaining $6,697 and $17,324 respectively are paid by the employer, which is why the self-funded distinction has teeth: in that structure the employer is not buying insurance with that money, it is holding the risk directly and buying administration. What you are comparing when you compare "insurance companies" at open enrolment is, in most large-employer cases, a network and a service operation rather than a risk transfer.

How these pieces sit inside the wider system — providers, payers and the public programmes — is covered in our overview of how US health care actually works, and the vocabulary vendors use when selling into it is unpicked in what "health care solutions" actually means.

Questions people actually ask

Is the company on my insurance card the one paying my claims?

Often not. In a self-insured plan the employer collects the contributions and pays the medical claims itself, and the insurance company named on the card is a contracted administrator. AHRQ's Medical Expenditure Panel Survey found 57.7 per cent of private-sector enrollees were in self-insured plans in 2020, rising to 76.9 per cent at firms of 1,000 or more employees.

What is a health insurance issuer?

An issuer is the licensed entity that takes on the financial risk of a policy: it collects the premium, promises to pay covered claims, and holds reserves against that promise. Issuers are licensed and examined by state insurance departments. Only when an issuer is involved does the arrangement meet the ordinary meaning of insurance.

How many Americans have private health insurance?

The Census Bureau reported that 66.1 per cent of people had private coverage at some point during 2024 and 35.5 per cent had public coverage; the two overlap because people can hold both in one year. Employment-based coverage accounted for 53.8 per cent and direct-purchase coverage for 10.7 per cent.

Who regulates health insurance companies?

It depends on the structure. Fully insured products are regulated by state insurance departments, with federal marketplace rules layered on. Self-funded employer plans are governed by ERISA and overseen by the US Department of Labor, not the state insurance department. Medicare Advantage, Part D and Medicaid managed care plans answer to CMS and, for Medicaid, the state Medicaid agency.

How much of my premium must an insurer spend on medical care?

Under the Affordable Care Act's medical loss ratio rule, issuers must spend at least 80 or 85 per cent of premium dollars on medical care and quality improvement depending on market segment. An issuer that misses the applicable standard in a given year has been required to rebate the difference to its customers since 2012.

What happens if a health insurance company goes insolvent?

Every state, plus the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, maintains a guaranty association to pay covered claims arising from insurer insolvency, subject to statutory limits set by each state. That mechanism attaches to insurance policies. A self-funded employer plan is not an insurance policy, so the protection works differently and depends on the employer's own solvency and any stop-loss arrangement.

How much do health insurance companies handle in total?

CMS put private health insurance spending at $1,644.6 billion in 2024, about 31 per cent of the $5.3 trillion in total national health expenditure. Medicare accounted for $1,118.0 billion and Medicaid $931.7 billion, much of it administered by private companies under contract rather than paid out by them as insurers.
