Health Information Professionals: Roles, Pay and Credentials

Last verified
2 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Wage data
BLS, May 2024
Sources
7 cited
Reading time
10 min

A health information professional manages the accuracy, security and accessibility of patient records. In practice that means translating diagnoses and procedures into standardised codes, auditing clinical documentation for completeness, controlling who may lawfully release a record, and maintaining the systems that hold all of it.

The phrase covers at least three occupations that the US government counts separately, and that report median wages more than $67,000 apart. Which one a job belongs to matters more than the job title on the posting.

What the work actually is

Every clinical encounter in an American hospital or clinic produces a record. That record has to be complete enough to justify the care, precise enough to bill, protected enough to satisfy federal privacy law, and structured enough that someone can later count it. Health information professionals are the people responsible for all four properties.

The work sits between clinical care and administration, and it is largely invisible when it goes well. A physician writes a note; a coder converts it into ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes and CPT or ICD-10-PCS procedure codes; a documentation specialist queries the physician when the note does not support the severity of the case; a privacy officer decides whether a subpoena is sufficient to release the chart; an analyst pulls the coded data to report readmission rates to a regulator. Each of those steps is a different job, and only some of them are the same occupation.

Two things follow from this. First, the field rewards precision over speed in a way that most administrative work does not, because a mis-sequenced code is a compliance issue rather than a typo. Second, the ceiling is much higher than the entry salary suggests, because the same underlying skill — knowing what a record means — scales into analytics, compliance and management.

The three occupations the government counts separately

This is the single most useful thing to understand before comparing job postings, and it is the thing salary aggregators most often blur. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics does not have an occupation called "health information professional". It has three, and they are not interchangeable.

Read that table twice. The entry occupation pays roughly the national median for all work. The second pays about a third more. The third pays more than double. The distance between them is not seniority within one job — it is a move into a different occupation, usually gated by a degree and a credential.

The narrowest category, health information technologists and medical registrars, is also the smallest: about 41,900 jobs in 2024, with roughly 3,200 openings a year. It pays well and grows fast, but there are far fewer chairs than in coding. Plan the route accordingly.

The jobs inside the field

Underneath those three statistical buckets sit the job titles you will actually see advertised.

Medical coder

Converts documented diagnoses and procedures into ICD-10-CM, ICD-10-PCS, CPT and HCPCS codes. Inpatient coding is generally harder and better paid than outpatient or professional-fee coding, because inpatient cases involve sequencing decisions, present-on-admission indicators and diagnosis-related group assignment. This is the largest entry point into the field.

Clinical documentation integrity (CDI) specialist

Reviews records while the patient is still admitted and queries clinicians when the documentation does not reflect the clinical picture. Often staffed by nurses who move sideways, or by experienced inpatient coders who move up. Sits at the point where clinical judgement and reimbursement meet, which is why it pays above straight coding.

Release of information (ROI) specialist

Decides what may lawfully leave the organisation, to whom, and on what authority — patient requests, attorney subpoenas, insurer audits, public health reporting. Heavily governed by HIPAA and by state law that is frequently stricter. Lower-paid than coding on average, but the fastest route into privacy and compliance work.

Cancer or trauma registrar

Abstracts and maintains disease registries used for research, accreditation and public health surveillance. Registrars are counted inside the health information technologist occupation, which is part of why that occupation's median sits so much higher.

Health data analyst

Turns coded and abstracted data into the reports that leadership and regulators see. The differentiator over a general analyst is knowing what the data actually means — that a diagnosis code reflects a documentation decision, not a clinical truth.

HIM manager or director

Runs the department: staffing, coding accuracy audits, backlog, vendor contracts, compliance readiness. This is where the field crosses into the medical and health services manager occupation and its much higher wage band.

What the pay actually looks like

Three qualifications belong on every salary figure in this field.

The median is not the range. For medical and health services managers — the management tier — BLS reported that the lowest ten per cent earned under $69,680 and the highest ten per cent earned more than $219,080 in May 2024. A single median hides a spread of $150,000.

Setting moves the number. The same credential pays differently in a large hospital system, a physician practice, a payer, a consultancy and a government agency. Payer-side and consulting roles typically pay above provider-side roles for the same nominal title.

Wage data lags. The figures above are the May 2024 estimates, which are the most recent BLS occupational wage estimates published. Treat them as a structural comparison between occupations rather than as this month's market rate.

Credentials, and what each one actually requires

Credentials in this field are gatekeeping in a specific and useful way: the requirement is usually the education pathway, not the exam. You cannot study your way into the RHIT or RHIA from outside an accredited programme.

Two details catch people out. AHIMA credentials are maintained on a two-year recertification cycle requiring continuing education units and a fee, so the cost is ongoing rather than one-off. And AHIMA requires candidates to bring the current year's code books to the testing centre for coding exams — from 1 May 2026, exams require the 2026 editions, and candidates who arrive with the wrong books are turned away and forfeit the fee.

Check CAHIIM accreditation before enrolling, not after. An unaccredited HIM programme will not make you eligible for the RHIT or RHIA no matter how good the teaching is, and that is the most expensive mistake available in this field.

How people actually get in

Three routes account for most entrants.

Coding first. A coding certificate programme, then the CCA, then a production coding job, then the CCS once the experience requirement is met. This is the fastest and cheapest route to a paycheck, and it is the route with the lowest ceiling unless a degree follows later.

Associate degree first. A CAHIIM-accredited associate programme, then the RHIT. Slower and more expensive, but it opens technician and analyst postings that filter on the credential, and it transfers cleanly into a bachelor's later.

Sideways from clinical work. Nurses and therapists move into CDI and utilisation review, bringing clinical credibility that coders have to build. They usually skip the entry tier entirely.

The honest constraint is that entry-level coding jobs are competitive precisely because the credential barrier is low. Employers filter on production experience, and the first job is the hardest one to get. Apprenticeship programmes, hospital internal transfers from registration or scheduling, and outpatient practices are the usual ways through.

Where the work is

Hospitals remain the largest single employer, but the field has spread. Physician practices, insurers, consulting firms that audit coding accuracy on contract, registry organisations, government agencies and health IT vendors all employ health information staff. Payer-side and vendor-side roles are frequently invisible to people who search only for hospital jobs, and they often pay better.

Geography matters less than it used to for coding and CDI, and still matters a great deal for release of information, registry and management roles that involve physical records or on-site staff supervision.

What is changing

Computer-assisted coding has been in production for years and has not eliminated coders; it has shifted the job from assigning codes to validating suggested ones and handling the cases the software cannot. The pattern in the BLS projections is consistent with that: the coding-heavy occupation is projected to grow more slowly (7 per cent) than the systems-and-data occupation (15 per cent) or the management occupation (23 per cent). Automation is compressing the bottom of the field and expanding the top.

The practical reading for anyone entering now is to treat production coding as an entry point rather than a destination, and to plan the move into auditing, documentation integrity, analytics or management as an explicit step with its own credential — not as something that happens with tenure.

Questions people actually ask

What does a health information professional do?

They manage the accuracy, security and accessibility of patient records. In practice that means translating diagnoses and procedures into standardised codes, auditing clinical documentation for completeness, controlling who may release a record, and maintaining the systems that hold all of it.

How much do health information professionals earn?

It depends on which of three occupations the job falls under. In May 2024 the US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a median annual wage of $50,250 for medical records specialists, $67,310 for health information technologists and medical registrars, and $117,960 for medical and health services managers.

What qualification do you need to work in health information?

Entry coding roles can be reached with a high school diploma plus AHIMA's CCA credential. Technician roles generally expect an associate degree from a CAHIIM-accredited programme and the RHIT credential. Management roles generally expect a bachelor's degree from a CAHIIM-accredited programme and the RHIA credential.

Is health information a growing field?

Yes, on the government's own projections. Between 2024 and 2034 the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 7 per cent growth for medical records specialists, 15 per cent for health information technologists and medical registrars, and 23 per cent for medical and health services managers. All three are above the average for all occupations.

Can you work in health information remotely?

Coding and clinical documentation integrity roles are among the most commonly remote jobs in healthcare, because the work is done inside the electronic health record rather than at the bedside. Release of information and departmental management roles are more often on-site.

Is health information management the same as health informatics?

No, and the distinction affects eligibility. Health information management is the governance of the record itself. Health informatics is the design and application of the systems and data. They overlap in practice, but AHIMA requires a CAHIIM-accredited HIM programme for RHIT eligibility — an informatics programme does not substitute.

Which credential should you start with?

If the goal is a job quickly and cost is the constraint, the CCA. If the goal is a management career and time allows, go directly for the accredited degree that makes you eligible for the RHIT or RHIA, because that pathway cannot be retrofitted from exam study.
