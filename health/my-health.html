My Health: Patient Portals, Your Record and Your Right of Access

Last verified
2 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Legal basis
HIPAA · Cures Act
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

"My health" almost always means one of two things: the online portal where you read your results and message your clinician, or the underlying medical record that the portal shows a filtered view of. They are not the same thing, and the difference matters when something is missing.

You have a legal right of access to the record itself — not just to the portal — and the provider has a deadline.

What a patient portal actually is

A patient portal is a website or app run by a health care provider that gives you a read-only window into part of their electronic health record, plus a few transactional features: messaging, appointment booking, prescription refill requests and bill payment.

The critical point is ownership. The portal belongs to the provider, not to you. The brand name on the login screen is usually the software vendor that the hospital licensed, which is why the same interface appears at unrelated health systems and why your login at one does not work at another.

That single fact explains most portal frustration. If you have seen clinicians at three organisations, you have three records and probably three portals. There is no national account, no master profile, and no authority that merges them.

How to find yours

Start from the provider rather than from a search engine, because searching a portal brand name usually lands you on the wrong organisation's login page.

  • Check the after-visit summary. The printed or emailed summary from a visit almost always names the portal and its web address.
  • Check a billing statement. Statements normally carry the portal address because that is where payment is taken.
  • Go to the provider's own website and look for "patient portal" in the top navigation. Confirm the organisation name on the page matches the one that treated you.
  • Call the medical records or health information department. They can issue an enrolment token, which is normally what an activation code on a visit summary is.

If you are trying to reach a portal you used years ago and the organisation has since merged, the account may have been migrated to the acquiring system's portal. Ask the acquiring organisation's health information department — the record itself has to go somewhere even when the website disappears.

What is actually in your record

The portal shows a subset. The legal record — what HIPAA calls the designated record set — is broader and is what you are entitled to request.

When people say the portal is "missing" something, it is usually in the right-hand column rather than genuinely absent. Requesting the designated record set is a different action from scrolling the portal.

Your legal right of access

The HIPAA Privacy Rule gives you a right to inspect and obtain a copy of the protected health information a covered entity holds about you. Three details are worth knowing precisely.

The deadline is 30 calendar days. A covered entity must act on your request within 30 calendar days of receiving it. One extension of up to 30 additional days is permitted, and only if the entity tells you in writing why and gives you the date it will complete the request. Federal regulators treat that as an outer limit, not a target.

The fee is capped by its nature, not by a number. You can be charged a reasonable, cost-based fee — labour for making the copy, supplies, postage, and preparing a summary if you agreed to one in advance. You cannot be charged for the cost of searching for or retrieving the record.

The format is largely your choice. If you ask for an electronic copy and the entity can readily produce one, it should. You can also direct the copy to a third party in writing.

Enforcement is real rather than theoretical. The HHS Office for Civil Rights runs a dedicated right-of-access enforcement initiative and has settled numerous cases against providers who failed to produce records on time.

Why results appear before your doctor calls

This changed for almost everyone and the reason is a specific law. The 21st Century Cures Act and the information blocking regulations that implement it made prompt electronic sharing the default. A provider generally may not delay releasing electronic health information to you simply so that a clinician can review it first — doing so can itself be information blocking.

The practical effect is that a laboratory or pathology result can appear in the portal before anyone has called you. This is disorienting the first time it happens, and it was contested when the rules took effect. The evidence since has been fairly one-sided: in a study of about 8,000 patients who had received results through a portal, more than 95 per cent wanted to keep receiving them immediately — and that figure held at 95 per cent among patients whose results were not normal.

A result in the portal is data, not an interpretation. A value flagged outside the reference range is common and frequently unimportant; a normal-looking value can still matter in context. The clinical meaning is a conversation with your clinician, not something the portal is claiming.

If something in the record is wrong

You have the right to request an amendment. Send it in writing to the organisation that created the entry, identify the specific item, and state what it should say and why. The provider can deny the request — for example if it did not create the entry, or if it considers the record accurate and complete — but it must tell you why.

If it is denied, you can file a statement of disagreement, which is retained with the record and must be included when that portion of the record is disclosed. In practice a clear, specific, dated statement of disagreement is more useful than an argument, because it travels with the file.

Note the distinction between correcting a clinical entry and correcting a coded entry on a bill. The latter is usually a coding review handled by the health information or billing department, and it is often the faster route when the problem is a claim rather than a chart.

Third-party apps and the API route

There is a second way into your own data that most people never use. Certified electronic health record systems are required to expose a standards-based application programming interface that lets you authorise an app of your choosing to pull your record directly, without the provider acting as intermediary each time.

In practice this is what health apps on phones are doing when they offer to connect to a hospital: you log in with your existing portal credentials, approve the scope, and the app receives structured data rather than a PDF. The advantage over the portal is that data from several organisations can sit in one place. The limitations are worth knowing.

  • Coverage varies. Not every organisation exposes everything, and older records predating the requirement are often absent.
  • The privacy rules change hands. Once your data reaches a consumer app, HIPAA generally no longer governs it — the app's own privacy policy does. That is a genuine downgrade in protection, not a technicality.
  • It is a copy, not the record. If you need something for a legal or insurance purpose, the provider's certified copy is what counts.

Before connecting a health app, read the part of its privacy policy that says what it does with data when you delete your account. That single paragraph tells you more about the company than the marketing does.

Who is on the other side of the request

Requests for records are handled by release of information staff inside the health information management department, working under HIPAA and under state law that is frequently stricter than HIPAA. They are the people who decide what may lawfully leave the organisation, to whom, and on what authority.

Knowing that changes how you ask. A request addressed to "medical records" or "health information management", naming the dates of service and the specific documents you want, moves faster than a general request to a clinic front desk. If you want to understand who these people are and what the rest of the department does, see our guide to health information professionals, and for the wider context of who pays for the care in the first place, how the US health care system works.

Questions people actually ask

How do I find my patient portal?

Portals belong to the provider, not to you, so start from whoever treated you. Check the after-visit summary or billing statement for a portal name and web address, or search the provider's own website for the word portal. The portal brand you see is usually the software vendor rather than a service you signed up for separately.

Why do I have several patient portals?

Because each health system, clinic and laboratory keeps its own record and runs its own portal. There is no single national account. Some portals let you link accounts across organisations that use the same software, but that linking is a convenience feature, not a merged record.

How long does a provider have to give me my medical records?

Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule a covered entity must act on a request for access within 30 calendar days, with one permitted extension of up to 30 additional days if it notifies you in writing of the reason and the new date. State law may be stricter.

Why did my test result appear before my doctor called me?

Because the 21st Century Cures Act information blocking rules made prompt electronic sharing the default. Providers generally may not delay releasing electronic health information to you simply to allow a clinician to review it first.

Can I be charged for a copy of my medical records?

Yes, but only a reasonable cost-based fee covering things such as labour for copying, supplies and postage. Under HIPAA you cannot be charged for the cost of searching for or retrieving the record.

What do I do if my medical record is wrong?

Request an amendment in writing to the provider that created the entry. HIPAA gives you the right to request one; if the provider denies it, you have the right to file a statement of disagreement that is kept with the record.

Can I get my records after the clinic closed?

Usually yes. Records have retention obligations that survive a closure, and they are typically transferred to a successor practice, a custodian named by the clinician, or a records storage company. Your state medical board is the usual place to find out who holds them.
