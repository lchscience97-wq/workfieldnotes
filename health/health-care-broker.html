Health Care Broker: How They Are Paid, and What That Forbids

Last verified
25 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources

A broker is paid by the plan, and in Medicare that payment is capped by regulation. The fair market value benchmarks written into 42 CFR 422.2274 were $539 nationally as of 1 January 2021, with higher figures for some states, adjusted annually by CMS.

Two features of the structure matter more than the number. Renewal years pay 50 percent of that, so keeping you and moving you are worth different amounts. And a plan may not write any contract term whose direct or indirect effect would inhibit an objective recommendation.

Who pays, restated

Our guide to who may recommend a plan set out the three kinds of helper and what each is permitted to do. This page takes the next question: how the one that may recommend actually earns, and what federal rules do about the conflict that creates.

The short version is that you do not pay a broker. Issuers do. That is not a secret and not improper, and it is the reason a service that looks free is available at all. The interesting part is that Medicare does not leave the amount to the market.

What counts as compensation

42 CFR 422.2274(a) defines compensation broadly: monetary or non-monetary remuneration of any kind relating to the sale, renewal, or services related to a plan.

From 2025 that definition expressly reaches items that used to sit outside it — fees for state appointment compliance, training, testing, mileage reimbursement, and venue costs associated with beneficiary appointments.

Widening a definition is how a cap is defended. A ceiling on commission alone can be worked around by paying for other things; a ceiling on remuneration of any kind cannot.

The items named in the 2025 expansion are worth reading closely, because none of them looks like a payment for selling anything. Reimbursing a state appointment fee, paying for training, covering mileage or hiring a room for an appointment are all ordinary business costs, and each can be met by a plan without a dollar of commission changing hands. Bringing them inside the definition is an acknowledgement that the economics of a relationship are not confined to the line labelled commission, and that a broker whose costs are met by one issuer is in a different position from one whose costs are not.

The cap, and how it moves

The regulation sets geographic fair market value benchmarks. As of 1 January 2021 those were $539 nationally, $607 for Connecticut, Pennsylvania and the District of Columbia, $672 for California and New Jersey, and $370 for Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands.

Those are the figures written into the rule as a baseline, not current-year amounts. CMS adjusts the fair market value annually, calculating the current year figure and the product of that figure and the Medicare Advantage growth percentage for aged and disabled beneficiaries published in the rate announcement. From 2025 a one-time $100 increase was added to account for administrative payments being brought inside the cap.

The geographic spread is worth noting: the highest benchmark sits about 25 percent above the national figure and the lowest about 31 percent below it. Broker economics are not uniform across the country, and neither is the intensity of the sales effort you are likely to encounter.

Initial versus renewal

This is the structural fact with the clearest consequence for a consumer.

An initial enrolment year is paid at fair market value. A renewal year is paid at 50 percent of fair market value under 42 CFR 422.2274(d)(3).

Take the national baseline as an illustration and the gap is $539 against $269.50. A move is worth roughly twice a stay. That does not make any particular recommendation to switch wrong — switching is often right — but it is a reason to ask directly whether the enrolment being proposed would be an initial one for the broker, and to weigh the answer.

The administrative payment route, and its closing

Caps invite adjacent payments, and the regulation has been tightened accordingly.

Through 2024, administrative payments were governed by 42 CFR 422.2274(e)(1), which required that they not exceed the value of those services in the marketplace — a reasonableness test rather than a ceiling. Beginning in 2025, (e)(2) provides that administrative payments are included in the calculation of enrollment-based compensation.

That is the difference between a second pot and one pot. Under the earlier rule a plan could pay compensation up to the cap and administrative amounts alongside it; under the current rule the administrative amounts count against the same ceiling.

The anti-steering clause

The provision worth quoting to anyone who assumes commission rules are only about totals is 42 CFR 422.2274(c)(13).

An organisation must ensure that no provision of a contract has a direct or indirect effect of creating an incentive that would reasonably be expected to inhibit an agent or broker's ability to objectively assess and recommend which plan best fits the beneficiary's needs.

Read the phrase direct or indirect effect. The test is not whether a contract says steer people here; it is whether the arrangement would reasonably be expected to have that result. That is a substantially harder standard to satisfy, and it is the clause a complaint about steering should cite.

The Marketplace does it differently

Individual market brokers are not under the Medicare compensation caps. The Exchange rules attack the same problem from the display side instead.

For web-brokers, 45 CFR 155.220(c)(3)(i) requires display of all qualified health plan data provided by the Exchange, prohibits financial incentives, such as rebates or giveaways, requires that plan placement not be based on compensation the agent, broker, or web-broker receives from QHP issuers, and requires a clear explanation of the rationale for QHP recommendations to be prominently displayed.

Medicare caps the money; the Marketplace regulates the ordering and the disclosure. Neither approach removes the conflict, and neither pretends to.

The difference in method reflects a difference in what each market makes visible. Medicare enrolment is overwhelmingly a conversation, often in a home or on a telephone, where no third party sees the comparison being made; regulating the money is the only lever that reaches it. Marketplace enrolment frequently runs through a screen, where the order of results is itself the recommendation and can be inspected. Each regime attacks the point at which its own market actually applies pressure.

Three questions worth asking

Which issuers are you appointed with, and which are you not? Would this enrolment be an initial or a renewal year for you? And do you receive anything from the plan beyond enrolment compensation?

All three have short factual answers. The first bounds the menu you are being shown, the second exposes the two-to-one economics above, and the third tests the boundary the 2025 change was written to police. A broker operating properly can answer all three without difficulty.

A fourth is worth adding when the conversation is about switching: what specifically changes for me, and what do I lose? A recommendation that survives that question is a recommendation about your coverage. One that returns only general advantages is a recommendation about the enrolment.

What the rules do not do

They do not make a broker impartial. Impartiality is the navigator's obligation, not the broker's — a navigator may not receive any consideration directly or indirectly from any health insurance issuer under 45 CFR 155.210(d), which is a different instrument for a different purpose.

They do not oblige a broker to represent every issuer, so the menu remains a subset. They do not set the premium, the network or the formulary. And the Medicare caps do not reach commercial group coverage, where compensation is a matter for contract and state law.

What they do is bound the size of the conflict and forbid the contract terms most likely to weaponise it. That is worth knowing precisely, because it tells you which questions have regulatory backing behind them. Questions people actually ask

How is a health care broker paid?

By the plan, not by you. In Medicare Advantage the payment is regulated: 42 CFR 422.2274(a) defines compensation as monetary or non-monetary remuneration of any kind relating to the sale, renewal, or services related to a plan, and from 2025 that definition expressly reaches items such as state appointment fees, training, testing, mileage reimbursement and venue costs for beneficiary appointments.

Is there a limit on what a broker can earn per enrolment?

Yes, a fair market value cap. The regulation sets geographic benchmarks that, as of 1 January 2021, were $539 nationally, $607 for Connecticut, Pennsylvania and the District of Columbia, $672 for California and New Jersey, and $370 for Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands. CMS adjusts the figure annually using the Medicare Advantage growth percentage published in the rate announcement.

Does the broker earn the same amount every year?

No. Renewal years are paid at 50 percent of fair market value under 42 CFR 422.2274(d)(3), against the full amount in an initial enrolment year. That structure is why a broker's economics differ sharply between keeping you where you are and moving you somewhere new, and it is worth knowing before a recommendation to switch.

What about administrative payments?

They were the pressure point and the rule changed. Through 2024, administrative payments had to not exceed the value of those services in the marketplace under 42 CFR 422.2274(e)(1). Beginning in 2025, administrative payments are included in the calculation of enrollment-based compensation under (e)(2), which folds them inside the fair market value ceiling rather than alongside it.

Can a plan pay a broker to steer me?

No. Under 42 CFR 422.2274(c)(13) an organisation must ensure that no provision of a contract has a direct or indirect effect of creating an incentive that would reasonably be expected to inhibit an agent or broker's ability to objectively assess and recommend which plan best fits the beneficiary's needs. The prohibition reaches indirect effects, not only explicit bonuses.

Do the same rules apply on the Marketplace?

No, a different instrument does similar work. For web-brokers, 45 CFR 155.220(c)(3)(i) requires display of all qualified health plan data provided by the Exchange, prohibits financial incentives such as rebates or giveaways, requires that plan placement not be based on compensation received from issuers, and requires a clear explanation of the rationale for recommendations to be prominently displayed.

What should I ask a broker about their pay?

Which issuers you are appointed with, whether this would be an initial or a renewal enrolment for you, and whether you receive anything from the plan beyond enrolment compensation. None of those questions is impolite, and all three have short factual answers that materially change how a recommendation should be weighed.
