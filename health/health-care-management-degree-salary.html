Health Care Management Degree Salary: Nobody Forecasts Pay

Two numbers get quoted together and neither means what the pairing implies. The wage is a dated snapshot: $117,960, measured in May 2024. The growth figure is a projection of employment, and BLS does not project wages at all.

The projection also carries assumptions almost never quoted with it: a full employment economy in the target year, no recessions, and a labour market in equilibrium. Anyone weighing a two-year programme is applying a 2024 measurement to a 2028 market, across a gap no federal dataset bridges.

The pairing that misleads

Almost every page on this subject puts a wage and a growth rate side by side, and the arrangement invites a reading neither number supports: that pay is rising because demand is rising.

They are different measurements from different programmes. One is a wage survey of what employers paid at a point in time. The other is a projection of how many positions will exist a decade later. Nothing joins them, and no federal series interpolates between them.

Our companion guide to why no federal source publishes a salary by degree deals with the credential half of this problem. This page deals with the time half.

BLS does not project wages

The Employment Projections programme is the source of every ten-year growth figure in American career writing. Its scope is employment.

The projections cover occupational employment growth, industry employment trends, and labour force size and composition. They do not cover wages or earnings, and BLS publishes no forecast of either.

That absence is the single most useful fact in this area, because it identifies exactly what a projected salary figure is: an estimate produced by whoever published it, using a method they chose. It may be reasonable. It is not federal data.

The omission is deliberate rather than a gap waiting to be filled. Projecting employment requires modelling how many positions an economy will support, which is hard but tractable from industry and demographic structure. Projecting wages would require modelling bargaining, inflation, productivity and policy a decade out, on an occupation-by-occupation basis. A statistical agency that publishes what it can defend does not publish that, and the restraint is a feature of the series rather than a shortcoming of it.

What the growth figure assumes

The projection is not a prediction of the world, and BLS says so in terms.

First: the analysis underlying the BLS employment projections focuses on long-term structural change and growth and assumes a full employment economy in the target year. The target year is assumed healthy by construction.

Second: BLS does not project recessions, though structural changes arising from them may show up indirectly.

Third: the projections assume a labor market in equilibrium, i.e., one where overall labor supply meets labor demand except for some degree of frictional unemployment.

Read those three together and the growth rate becomes legible. It answers: under a healthy, recession-free, balanced labour market, how much larger would this occupation be? That is a genuinely useful question. It is not the question will I earn more.

Every figure has a vintage

Projections run on a ten-year horizon and are updated annually. The 2024–34 set was released on 28 August 2025, and the 2025–35 set is scheduled for 27 August 2026.

Wage data has its own clock. The medians in circulation now are May 2024 figures.

Stack the two clocks against a decision. A prospective student reading in 2026 is reading a wage measured in 2024. If the programme runs two years, they enter the market in 2028. The number guiding the decision is four years older than the market it is being used to describe, and by then two further projection rounds will have been published.

None of that makes the figure wrong. It makes it a measurement of a past state, which is what a wage survey is, and which is the only thing it claims to be.

The practical correction is not to inflate the number by a guess. It is to re-check it at the point of decision rather than at the point of research, and again on entry to the market. Both series are republished on schedules you can put in a calendar, which makes staleness an avoidable error rather than an inherent one.

What the occupation pays now

For completeness, the current figures. Medical and health services managers had a median annual wage of $117,960 in May 2024, with 616,200 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 23 percent over 2024–34 and about 62,100 openings a year. BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry level and notes that prospective managers typically need prior work experience in an administrative or clinical role.

The openings figure repays decomposition. Applying 23 percent to 2024 employment gives roughly 141,700 additional positions over the decade, about 14,200 a year. Against about 62,100 total openings a year, that means roughly 77 percent of vacancies arise from people leaving the occupation rather than from growth.

That ratio matters more than the growth headline for anyone entering. Most of the hiring in this occupation is refilling established posts, which tend to be advertised at the experience level of the person who left.

It also cuts against a common inference from a high growth rate. Twenty-three percent is genuinely large for a management occupation, and it is easy to read as a wide-open door. But growth contributes under a quarter of annual vacancies here, and the replacement three quarters are, by definition, positions somebody already held. An entrant is competing for a refill far more often than for a newly created post, which is precisely the situation in which the prior work experience BLS describes as typical does the most work.

Reading across a decision horizon

Three habits make these numbers usable rather than misleading.

Date every figure you quote to yourself. A median without a month is not a fact you can reason with, because you cannot tell how far it sits from the market you will enter.

Separate the two questions. What does this pay is answered by a dated wage survey. Will there be jobs is answered by a projection with stated assumptions. Answering the second with the first, or the reverse, is where most career writing goes wrong.

Treat the assumptions as part of the number. A 23 percent projection under an assumed full-employment, recession-free equilibrium is a different object from a 23 percent forecast of the actual future, and only the first exists.

Where the degree fits

None of the above is degree-specific, and that is not an omission. The federal wage survey does not record education, so no figure here is attributable to a management degree rather than an administration degree, or to a master's rather than a bachelor's.

What can be said is what BLS says about entry: a bachelor's degree is typical, some employers hire candidates with an associate's degree, others prefer a master's, and work experience sometimes substitutes for education. Our guides to two words, one code and five paths behind one title work through what the degree names do and do not distinguish, and the full spread by setting covers where the variation actually lives.

What no source will give you

There is no federal projection of what this occupation will pay in 2028, 2030 or 2034. There is no federal measurement of what a specific degree adds to a wage. And there is no federal series that connects the two numbers most people quote together.

What exists is better than a forecast for most purposes, provided it is read as what it is: a dated measurement of what the work pays, a stated-assumption estimate of how many positions there will be, and an honest silence where a prediction would be. A decision built on those three is built on something real. A decision built on a projected salary is built on a number that nobody official publishes and that no dataset stands behind.

Questions people actually ask

Does BLS forecast future salaries?

No. The Employment Projections programme projects employment and labour force, not wages or earnings. Its published material covers occupational employment growth, industry employment trends and labour force composition. There is no federal wage forecast, which means any number describing what a job will pay in several years' time is not coming from a federal source.

What assumptions sit behind the growth figure?

Three that are rarely quoted with it. BLS states that the analysis underlying its employment projections focuses on long-term structural change and growth and assumes a full employment economy in the target year. It states that BLS does not project recessions. And it assumes a labour market in equilibrium, where overall labour supply meets labour demand except for some degree of frictional unemployment.

How old is the wage figure I am reading?

Older than it looks. The current occupational medians are May 2024 figures. Someone reading them in 2026 and starting a two-year programme would enter the market in 2028, applying a snapshot taken four years earlier. That is not a criticism of the data; it is a description of what a decision horizon does to it.

How often are the projections updated?

Annually, on a ten-year horizon. The 2024 to 2034 projections were released on 28 August 2025, and the 2025 to 2035 projections are scheduled for release on 27 August 2026. Any figure quoted without its vintage may already have been superseded by a newer set.

What does the occupation actually pay now?

Medical and health services managers had a median annual wage of $117,960 in May 2024, with 616,200 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 23 percent over 2024–34 and about 62,100 openings a year. BLS lists the typical entry-level education as a bachelor's degree and notes that prospective managers typically need prior work experience in an administrative or clinical role.

Are most of those openings new jobs?

No. Applying the 23 percent projection to 2024 employment gives roughly 141,700 additional positions across the decade, about 14,200 a year. Against about 62,100 total openings a year, that means roughly 77 percent of vacancies come from people leaving the occupation rather than from growth. Replacement demand is the larger half of the hiring picture by some distance.

So how should I use these numbers?

As a description of the present, not a forecast of your future. The wage tells you what the occupation paid at a dated point. The projection tells you how many positions BLS expects under stated assumptions. Neither predicts your pay, and no federal dataset attempts to, so a decision resting on a projected salary is resting on something nobody publishes.
