Health Center 6 is at 301 W. Girard Ave., Philadelphia, PA 19123, with published numbers (215) 685-3803 and (215) 685-3816, run by the Philadelphia Department of Public Health.

The part nobody looks up is who governs it. In the federal Health Center Program a governing board must have a patient majority — and that board is not advisory. It approves the budget, hires and fires the chief executive, and sets which services run at which sites during which hours. A city satisfies this through a co-applicant board. This page explains the machinery.

Health Center 6, in one place

The City of Philadelphia lists Health Center 6 at 301 W. Girard Ave., Philadelphia, PA 19123, with two published telephone numbers: (215) 685-3803 and (215) 685-3816.

It sits among the centres the City describes as providing a full range of primary medical and support services to registered patients, and the City's own instruction applies here as everywhere on its roster: call ahead to make sure the service you need is available when you plan to visit. Our guides to the full City roster and why services vary by building cover those two questions.

The governance nobody mentions

Ask who runs a hospital and the honest answer is a board of trustees drawn from business, medicine and philanthropy. Ask who runs a federally qualified health centre and the answer is structurally different, and it is the most unusual feature of the whole programme.

42 U.S.C. §254b(k)(3)(H) requires a governing board composed of individuals, a majority of whom are being served by the center, meeting at least once a month, selecting services and approving budgets. The people receiving the service hold formal control of the entity providing it.

That is not a consultative arrangement bolted onto a normal organisation. It is a condition of the funding, audited like any other.

Who counts as a patient member

HRSA does not leave the term loose. A patient, for board purposes, is an individual who has received at least one service in the past 24 months that generated a health center visit, where both the service and the site where the service was received are within the HRSA-approved scope of project.

Three conditions are doing work in that sentence. There must be a service that generated a visit, not merely contact. It must fall within 24 months, so the majority is composed of current rather than historical patients. And both the service and the site must be inside the approved scope — the same scope machinery covered in our guide to why services vary by building.

The rest of the seats

The board must consist of at least 9 and no more than 25 members, with the majority — at least 51 percent — being patients.

The remaining seats are constrained too. Of the non-patient members, no more than one-half may derive more than 10 percent of their annual income from the health care industry. And non-patient members must be representative of the community served by the health center and must be selected for their expertise in relevant subject areas.

Read the income clause carefully, because it is the quiet one. It caps how far a board can be filled with people whose livelihood depends on the sector, even in the minority seats. The effect is a board that cannot become a professional body by drift.

What the board actually decides

The authority list is where the requirement becomes consequential rather than decorative.

The board must review and approve the annual Health Center Program project budget, and approve the selection and termination/dismissal of the health center's Project Director/Chief Executive Officer. It must adopt policies addressing scope and availability of services, service site location(s) and hours of operation of service sites.

It must also adopt policies for financial management practices and a system to ensure accountability for center resources, and establish general personnel policies covering selection, dismissal, compensation, grievances and equal opportunity. And it must assess the achievement of project objectives through evaluation of health center activities, including service utilization patterns, productivity of the center, and patient satisfaction.

Finally, it must hold monthly meetings and record attendance, key actions, and decisions.

How a city satisfies the requirement

A municipal health department cannot hand budget and hiring authority to an outside board without colliding with its own governance. The programme anticipates this.

HRSA states that for public agencies that elect to have a co-applicant, these board composition requirements apply to the co-applicant board, and that the health center has a co-applicant agreement that delegates the required authorities and functions to the co-applicant board — while the public agency may retain authority over financial management and accounting systems and personnel policies.

So in a city arrangement the patient-majority board is a distinct body with delegated powers, and the agreement between it and the department is the document that says which powers went where. That agreement is the single most informative artefact about how a city health centre is actually run, and almost nobody outside the programme has heard of it.

Why it matters to you

Look again at three items on that list: services, site locations, hours of operation. Those are precisely the things a patient runs into — whether dentistry is offered here, whether this site is open on a Saturday, whether behavioural health moved to another building.

In an ordinary clinic those are management decisions taken privately and announced afterwards. In a health centre they are board policies, adopted by a body with a patient majority, meeting monthly, with attendance and decisions recorded. The difference is not that the answers are better; it is that there is a route to the people who set them, and that route is a condition of the funding rather than a courtesy.

Checking it yourself

Ask the centre three questions. Does the organisation have a co-applicant board, and how do I contact it? When does it meet, and are the minutes available? And is there a seat process for patients — because a board that must be 51 percent patients is permanently recruiting them.

That last point is the practical one. The composition requirement creates a standing demand for patient board members that most patients never learn exists. If you use the centre and want the hours changed, the formal route is not a complaint form; it is a seat.

What board control does not mean

It does not mean the board runs the clinic day to day; it sets policy and hires the executive who does. It does not override the federal conditions the centre operates under, including the obligation to serve all residents of the area and to maintain a sliding fee discount schedule under 42 U.S.C. §254b(k)(3)(G) — a board cannot vote those away, and our guide to what the federal test actually is sets them out.

And in a public agency arrangement it does not mean the city has stepped back entirely, since financial management and personnel policies may be retained. What it does mean is that a specific, identifiable group of patients holds specific, written powers over the centre in front of you. That is unusual enough in American health care to be worth knowing about the building on Girard Avenue, or any other on the roster.

Questions people actually ask

Where is Health Center 6?

The City of Philadelphia lists Health Center 6 at 301 W. Girard Ave., Philadelphia, PA 19123, with published telephone numbers (215) 685-3803 and (215) 685-3816. It is one of the City health centers operated by the Philadelphia Department of Public Health, and the City advises calling ahead to confirm the service you need is available when you plan to visit.

Who sits on a health center board?

HRSA requires a governing board of at least 9 and no more than 25 members, of whom the majority, at least 51 percent, must be patients served by the health center. Of the non-patient members, no more than one-half may derive more than 10 percent of their annual income from the health care industry, and non-patient members must be representative of the community served and selected for their expertise in relevant subject areas.

What counts as a patient for board purposes?

HRSA defines a patient as an individual who has received at least one service in the past 24 months that generated a health center visit, where both the service and the site where the service was received are within the HRSA-approved scope of project. It is a documented status tied to the approved scope, not a self-description.

What powers does the board actually hold?

Substantial ones. The board must review and approve the annual Health Center Program project budget, approve the selection and termination or dismissal of the health center's Project Director or Chief Executive Officer, adopt policies on the scope and availability of services, service site locations and hours of operation, adopt financial management and personnel policies, and assess achievement of project objectives. It must hold monthly meetings and record attendance, key actions and decisions.

How can a city health department meet a patient-majority rule?

Through a co-applicant. HRSA states that for public agencies that elect to have a co-applicant, the board composition requirements apply to the co-applicant board, and that the health center has a co-applicant agreement delegating the required authorities and functions to that board. The public agency may retain authority over financial management and accounting systems and personnel policies.

Does the statute require monthly meetings?

Yes. 42 U.S.C. §254b(k)(3)(H) requires a governing board composed of individuals, a majority of whom are being served by the centre, and requires that board to meet at least once a month, select services and approve budgets. The monthly cadence is statutory rather than a matter of internal practice.

Why does any of this matter to a patient?

Because the decisions that most affect a visit — which services are offered, at which sites, during which hours — are among the policies the board must adopt. In an ordinary clinic those are management decisions taken privately. In a health center they belong to a body with a patient majority that meets monthly and records its decisions.
