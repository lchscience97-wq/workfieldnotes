Health Care Consultant: The Job BLS Does Not Measure

Last verified
4 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Wage data
BLS, May 2024; SOC 2018
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
9 min

There is no federal occupation called health care consultant. The 2018 Standard Occupational Classification contains 867 detailed occupations and the title is not among them. BLS sorts workers by job duties, not job titles, so the same business card lands in different codes depending on what the person does all day.

That is why quoted salaries scatter. The two nearest codes have medians of $101,190 and $117,960 — a $16,770 gap before experience is considered — and the federal wage survey excludes the self-employed entirely, removing much of the independent end of the field from the estimate.

The occupation with no code

Pages ranking for this term fall into three groups: consultancy marketing, university programme pages, and job boards. All three have a reason to treat "health care consultant" as a settled job with a knowable salary. The federal statistical system does not.

The 2018 SOC organises work into 23 major groups, 459 broad occupations and 867 detailed occupations. The Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics programme produces estimates for roughly 830 of them. Neither list contains this title, and BLS is explicit about why that is not an oversight: workers are classified into occupations based on their job duties, not their job titles, and workers that perform essentially the same tasks are in the same occupation, whether or not they are in the same industry.

Read that second clause carefully. It means health care is not, in the statistics, a separate profession. It is an industry that people from several occupations work inside.

The last row is the one that most distorts salary pages. A great many people titled "consultant" in health care are employed by a vendor to implement that vendor's product. That is a delivery role sold under an advisory word, and its pay follows software rather than advisory economics. The distinction is checkable in a single question asked before any engagement: is the deliverable a recommendation the buyer is free to reject, or the installation of something already purchased? Advisory economics apply to the first and delivery economics to the second, and the two are priced differently in every other industry as well. Two readings matter. The $16,770 gap between the first two medians is larger than most of the increments a career page will attribute to a certificate or a master's degree — so the code your role is classified under moves your benchmark more than the credential does. And the spreads are enormous: management analysts run $114,420 from the tenth to the ninetieth percentile, which is more than the median itself. A single quoted figure inside a distribution that wide carries almost no information.

The management analyst median works out to $48.65 an hour. That conversion is worth holding onto when comparing an employed offer against day rates for the same work.

Who is missing from the numbers entirely

This is the structural point that no salary page mentions, and it is decisive for anyone thinking of going independent.

The OEWS survey covers all part-time and full-time workers who are paid a wage or salary. It explicitly excludes the self-employed, owners and partners in unincorporated firms, household workers, or unpaid family workers.

Independent consultants are, definitionally, in the excluded group. So are partners in unincorporated advisory partnerships. The published wage estimates therefore describe employed analysts and managers, not the sole practitioner charging a day rate — which is exactly the model most people picture when they imagine the job. There is no federal wage estimate for that population, and any page presenting one is presenting something else.

The practical consequence is a comparison problem. An employed analyst at the $101,190 median works alongside an employer carrying the other half of payroll taxes, paid leave, benefits and unbilled time. A day rate is gross revenue out of which all of those come from the same figure. Setting the two against each other without adjusting for that difference is the commonest error in this decision, and there is no federal series that will settle it for you.

Three growth rates, three different stories

Projected growth is where the choice of code stops being academic. BLS projects 9 per cent growth for management analysts between 2024 and 2034, 23 per cent for medical and health services managers, and 4 per cent for lawyers.

Annual openings tell a related story. Management analysts are projected at about 98,100 openings a year against 1,075,100 jobs — roughly 9.1 per cent of the occupation turning over annually. Medical and health services managers are at about 62,100 against 616,200, or roughly 10.1 per cent. Lawyers are at about 31,500 against 864,800, or roughly 3.6 per cent.

So the same career page can honestly describe this field as growing at 4, 9 or 23 per cent depending on which code it silently picks. When you see a growth figure quoted for "health care consulting", the useful question is not whether it is true but which occupation it belongs to. The 23 per cent row and what it actually covers is set out in our guide to what a health care administrator does, with the full wage distribution in health care administration salary.

What the entry requirement really is

For management analysts, BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry-level education. Medical and health services managers enter with a bachelor's as well. Neither occupation is licensed.

That absence of licensure is the structural fact behind everything on this page. A lawyer must be admitted to the bar by their state's highest court before practising; a nursing home administrator needs a state licence. Nobody has to qualify to call themselves a health care consultant. The title is therefore a marketing choice rather than a credential, and it carries exactly as much information as the person using it decides to put into it.

Certification: not required, occasionally decisive

BLS is direct on this point: management analysts are not required to get certification, but having a credential may give jobseekers a competitive advantage.

The honest reading is that certification functions as a screening signal in competitive hiring rather than as a gate. Because no licence exists, a buyer has no statutory way to verify competence, so credentials, references and demonstrated scope do that job instead. For anyone selling, that is an argument for arriving able to state budget size, headcount and number of sites from previous engagements, which is what compensation structures on the employer side actually key on.

There is a second-order effect worth naming. Where a title is unlicensed and the official wage series omits the independent segment entirely, the price signal reaches the market from buyers rather than from any published benchmark. That is why demonstrated scope travels further than credentials here: it is the only evidence both sides of the table can check.

Reading a salary page critically

Three questions resolve most of the confusion, in this order.

Which occupation code is this figure attached to? If the page does not say, it is quoting one of several possible answers without telling you which. The difference between the two nearest codes is $16,770 at the median.

What month and year is the estimate? BLS revises annually and publishes the reference period explicitly. A figure with no stated period cannot be compared with anything.

Median or average? These distributions are right-skewed — the distance from the median up to the ninetieth percentile is far greater than the distance down to the tenth — so the average sits above the median and describes fewer people than it appears to. And whichever it is, remember that the population behind it excludes every self-employed consultant in the country. Questions people actually ask

Is health care consultant an official occupation?

No. The 2018 Standard Occupational Classification contains 867 detailed occupations and none of them carries that title. BLS classifies workers by job duties rather than job titles, so the same business card is sorted into management analysts, medical and health services managers or another code depending on what the person actually does.

What does a health care consultant earn?

There is no single figure, because there is no single code. The two closest are management analysts, with a median annual wage of $101,190 in May 2024, and medical and health services managers at $117,960. The gap between those medians is $16,770, which is larger than most of the differences a salary page will attribute to experience or certification.

Why do quoted salaries for this job vary so much?

Three reasons compound. Different sources map the title to different occupation codes. The percentile spread inside each code is wide, running from $59,720 to $174,140 for management analysts. And the federal wage survey excludes the self-employed and owners and partners in unincorporated firms altogether, which removes a large share of independent consultants from the estimate.

Is the field growing?

It depends entirely on which code you look at, and the three point in different directions. BLS projects management analysts to grow 9 per cent from 2024 to 2034, medical and health services managers 23 per cent, and lawyers 4 per cent. Quoting one of these as the growth rate for health care consulting is a choice, not a measurement.

What qualification do you actually need?

For management analysts BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry-level education, and states that certification is not required but that having a credential may give jobseekers a competitive advantage. Medical and health services managers also enter with a bachelor's. Neither occupation is licensed, which is the structural reason the title is used so loosely.

Which industry pays management analysts the most?

In May 2024 professional, scientific and technical services led at a median of $107,790, followed by management of companies and enterprises at $101,560, finance and insurance at $98,710, and government excluding education and hospitals at $94,080. The spread between the highest and lowest of those four is $13,710.

How do I read a health care consultant salary page critically?

Ask three questions. Which occupation code is the figure attached to, since the answer changes it by thousands. What month and year is the estimate, because BLS revises annually. And is the figure a median or an average, because these distributions are right-skewed and the average sits above the median.
