Health Care Bill: Two Meanings, and Where They Meet

Two unrelated things share this phrase. One is a measure before Congress. The other is the invoice that arrives after a visit. The results lean legislative, because the congressional record is a government site and invoices are private documents.

This page handles both, and then the place they meet: the No Surprises Act is a health care bill that governs your health care bill. Its provider rules say that in defined situations a provider must not bill you above your cost sharing at all — and name the services for which that protection cannot be signed away.

One phrase, two documents

A legislative bill is a proposal that may become law. A medical bill is a demand for payment for care already given. They share four letters and nothing else — different authors, different audiences, different remedies.

The search results tilt toward the first because congressional material is published on government domains that rank well, while the second is a piece of paper on somebody's kitchen table. That asymmetry is worth naming, because it means most people arrive at pages answering the question they did not ask.

The legislative meaning, precisely

Congress works in four forms, and the House sets them out plainly.

A bill is the form used for most legislation, whether permanent or temporary, general or special, public or private, and it requires presidential approval to become law. Bills originating in the House carry the H.R. designation.

A joint resolution may start in either chamber and follows the same procedure, with one exception: a resolution proposing a constitutional amendment bypasses the President entirely and goes, after two-thirds approval in both chambers, to the states for ratification.

A concurrent resolution handles matters affecting the operations of both chambers. It is not presented to the President and does not become law. A simple resolution concerns one chamber alone, is likewise not presented, and does not have the force of law.

So a headline about a health care resolution passing may describe something with no legal effect whatsoever. The form is the first thing to check.

Reading the right version

The second thing to check is which version you are reading, because a bill exists in many and they differ.

GovInfo describes the introduced version as the bill as formally presented by a member of Congress to a clerk, and the reported version as the bill as reported by the committee, including changes, if any, made in committee.

The engrossed version is the official copy of the bill or joint resolution as passed, including the text as amended by floor action. The enrolled version is the final official copy of the bill or joint resolution which both the House and the Senate have passed in identical form, sent to the President for signature.

Quoting an introduced version as though it were law is the commonest error in this area, and it is entirely avoidable: the version label is printed on the document.

Where the two meanings meet

The bridge is straightforward once stated. Whether an invoice is lawful is decided by statutes and the regulations under them. A medical bill is not a negotiation with a hospital; it is a claim measured against rules Congress wrote.

The clearest current example is the No Surprises Act, whose provider and facility requirements sit at 45 CFR Part 149 Subpart E. It is a health care bill in the legislative sense that directly determines what may appear on a health care bill in the invoice sense.

The emergency rule

Under 45 CFR 149.410, a nonparticipating emergency facility (at (a)(1)) and a nonparticipating provider (at (a)(2)) must not bill, and must not hold liable, a participant, beneficiary or enrollee for a payment amount that exceeds the cost-sharing requirement for those services.

That is a prohibition rather than a discount. In the situations it covers, the out-of-network status of the provider is not your problem to pay for.

149.410(b) permits a narrow notice-and-consent exception for certain post-stabilisation items and services, conditional on the attending physician determining that the patient can travel to a participating provider, proper notice and consent under 149.420, the patient being able to receive and give informed consent, and state law being satisfied. And 149.410(c) is categorical: the exception will always be inapplicable to items or services furnished as a result of unforeseen, urgent medical needs that arise at the time an item or service is furnished.

The rule for planned care at an in-network facility

The second scenario is the one people find most surprising: an in-network hospital, an out-of-network doctor inside it.

45 CFR 149.420(a) provides that a nonparticipating provider must not bill, and must not hold liable, a participant, beneficiary or enrollee for amounts exceeding the applicable cost-sharing requirement for non-emergency services at a participating facility, unless notice and consent requirements are satisfied.

Where consent is permitted at all, 149.420(c)(1)(iii) fixes the timing: written notice not later than 72 hours prior to the date on which the individual is furnished such items or services where the appointment was made at least 72 hours ahead, or on the date of scheduling where it is sooner, and in any event no later than 3 hours prior to the items or services being furnished.

The services that cannot be consented away

149.420(b)(1) is the provision worth memorising, because it removes the consent route entirely for the specialties most likely to produce an unexpected out-of-network bill.

It covers items and services related to emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology and neonatology; services by assistant surgeons, hospitalists and intensivists; diagnostic services, including radiology and laboratory services; and services where no participating provider can furnish them at the facility.

For those categories there is no form to sign, because none may be offered. A consent document presented for an anaesthetist at an in-network facility is not a document the rules contemplate.

The logic of the list is worth seeing, because it explains why these specialties and not others. Each is a service you do not choose and often never meet: the anaesthetist is assigned, the pathologist reads a slide in a room you never enter, the radiologist interprets an image hours later. Consent presupposes a choice, and where there is no realistic choice a signature would be a formality dressed as a decision. The fourth limb makes the same point structurally — if no participating provider can furnish the service at that facility, then agreeing to an out-of-network one is not an election either.

If your question was the invoice

Three moves in order. Ask for an itemised statement, because a summary total cannot be checked against anything. Establish whether the care was emergency, or non-emergency at a participating facility, because that determines which of the two rules above applies. And check whether any consent document you were given falls inside the timing rules, or inside the ancillary categories where consent is not available at all.

If you were uninsured or self-pay, a different and earlier protection applies, covered in our guide to the estimate you can demand. If your plan refused to pay, the route is your plan's internal appeal and then external review, set out in which regulator is yours and what comes after the call.

What neither meaning settles

A legislative bill tells you what someone proposed, not what the law is; only the enacted text does that, and only in the version labelled accordingly. A medical bill tells you what was charged, not what is owed; that depends on coverage, cost sharing and the prohibitions above.

The habit worth taking from both halves is the same. Find the document that governs, read the label on it, and measure the number against the rule rather than against expectation. For the wider map of which statute reaches which actor, our guide to ten statutes and who they bind sets it out.

Questions people actually ask

What is the difference between a bill and a resolution?

The House explains that a bill is the form used for most legislation, whether permanent or temporary, general or special, public or private, and that it requires presidential approval to become law. A joint resolution follows the same procedure with one exception for constitutional amendments, which go to the states rather than the President. Concurrent resolutions address the operations of both chambers and are not presented to the President. Simple resolutions concern one chamber alone and do not have the force of law.

What do the prefixes H.R. and S. mean?

They record the chamber of origin. Bills originating in the House carry the H.R. designation; House joint resolutions are H.J.Res., concurrent resolutions H.Con.Res. and simple resolutions H.Res. The Senate uses the S. family in the same way. The prefix tells you where a measure started, not whether it passed.

What does engrossed or enrolled mean?

They are stage labels. GovInfo describes the engrossed version as the official copy of the bill or joint resolution as passed, including the text as amended by floor action. The enrolled version is the final official copy which both the House and the Senate have passed in identical form, sent to the President for signature. Reading the wrong version is the commonest error in following legislation.

Which health care bill governs surprise bills?

The No Surprises Act, whose provider requirements sit at 45 CFR Part 149 Subpart E. Under 45 CFR 149.410 a nonparticipating emergency facility and a nonparticipating provider must not bill, and must not hold liable, a participant, beneficiary or enrollee for a payment amount that exceeds the cost-sharing requirement for those services.

Can a provider ask me to waive that protection?

Only in limited cases, through notice and consent. Under 45 CFR 149.420(c)(1)(iii) written notice must be given not later than 72 hours before the service if it was scheduled at least 72 hours ahead, or on the date of scheduling if the appointment is sooner, and in any case no later than 3 hours before the items or services are furnished. And 45 CFR 149.410(c) states the exception never applies to items or services furnished as a result of unforeseen, urgent medical needs arising at the time.

Which services can never be consented away?

Ancillary ones. 45 CFR 149.420(b)(1) excludes items and services related to emergency medicine, anesthesiology, pathology, radiology and neonatology; services by assistant surgeons, hospitalists and intensivists; diagnostic services including radiology and laboratory services; and services where no participating provider can furnish them at the facility. For those, notice and consent cannot be used at all.

Where do the two meanings actually meet?

In the ordinary case, a statute passed by Congress is what determines whether an invoice is lawful. The protections above did not arise from negotiation with a hospital; they are regulations implementing an Act. Reading a medical bill without knowing which statute governs it is reading a number with no rule attached.
