Health Care 360: What a Complete View Would Actually Require

Every result for this term is a company with 360 in its name. That is the whole finding about the phrase: it is a suffix meaning comprehensive, not a category anyone defines or certifies.

The claim underneath it can still be checked. For a platform promising a complete view of a patient, two federal instruments apply: USCDI, which defines the data classes that are supposed to move, and the information blocking rule, which says what may not be withheld and names nine exceptions for when it may. This page turns the adjective into four questions.

A suffix, not a category

No federal agency defines a 360 product, certifies one, or maintains a list. The number is doing what solutions, platform and ecosystem do elsewhere: signalling breadth without committing to a boundary. Our guide to what vendors actually mean covers the same manoeuvre in a different word.

That is not an accusation. Breadth is a real thing to sell. It simply means the word cannot be the unit of comparison, because two products carrying it may have nothing in common.

Two product families

In practice the suffix attaches to two quite different things.

The first is employer wellness and population health: screenings, biometric checks, coaching and incentives arranged for a workforce. The customer is an employer or a plan sponsor.

The second is clinical data aggregation: assembling records from multiple sources into a single patient view for clinicians or for a health system. The customer is a provider organisation.

They share an adjective and no rulebook. The first runs into wellness programme regulation; the second runs into interoperability regulation. Establishing which one you are looking at is the first move, and it is usually settled by asking who pays.

The confusion is not accidental, because the two families make overlapping promises about the same material. Both say they will assemble health data about a person that is currently scattered. What differs is the purpose and therefore the law: an employer platform assembles it to design incentives and manage a population's costs, while a clinical platform assembles it so a clinician can see one patient whole. Data that is unremarkable in the second use becomes sensitive in the first, which is why the rules diverge rather than converge.

The employer side, and the rule that caps it

Wellness programmes attached to a group health plan are regulated, and the shape of the regulation is worth knowing before evaluating a vendor's incentive design.

45 CFR 146.121(f) separates participatory programmes, where no reward depends on a health factor, from health-contingent programmes, where obtaining a reward requires satisfying a health-related standard.

For health-contingent programmes the reward must not exceed the applicable percentage of the total cost of employee-only coverage, which 146.121(f)(5) sets at up to 30 percent, or 50 percent for tobacco-related programmes. The programme must offer a reasonable alternative standard for individuals unable to satisfy the primary requirement for medical reasons, with accommodation of a personal physician's recommendations. And it must give eligible individuals the opportunity to qualify for the reward at least once per year.

A design that offers no alternative route, or that runs a single qualification window, is not meeting those conditions regardless of how comprehensive the platform is.

Two features of that structure are worth holding onto. The percentage is measured against the total cost of coverage rather than the employee's share, so the ceiling is larger in absolute terms than it first appears. And the reasonable alternative standard is not a courtesy extended to people who ask nicely: it is a condition of the programme being permitted at all, which means its absence is a defect in the programme rather than a limitation on the individual.

The federal yardstick for "all your data"

The second family's claim is testable against a published standard.

USCDI — the United States Core Data for Interoperability — is described by ASTP as a standardized set of health data classes and constituent data elements for nationwide, interoperable health information exchange. A data class is an aggregation of data elements by a common theme or use case; a data element is a piece of data defined in USCDI for access, exchange or use of electronic health information.

Versions advance through the Standards Version Advancement Process described in the Cures rule, which allows developers to update to newer versions on a defined footing rather than all at once. New elements are proposed through a public submission system.

The practical consequence: complete is not a property of a product, it is a property of a version. Two platforms supporting different USCDI versions are complete to different depths, and the version number is a document rather than a claim. What may not be withheld

The other half of a complete view is whether anyone is allowed to stop it.

Under 45 CFR 171.103, information blocking is a practice that interferes with access, exchange or use of electronic health information, except where required by law or covered by an exception. The knowledge standard differs by actor: for health IT developers, health information networks and health information exchanges, the actor knows, or should know the practice will interfere; for a health care provider, the provider knows that such practice is unreasonable and will interfere.

45 CFR 171.102 defines an actor as one of four things: a health care provider, a health IT developer of certified health IT, a health information network, or a health information exchange. That definition is a gate. A platform that is neither certified health IT nor a network or exchange may not be an actor at all, and the rule then does not reach it.

The two groups answer different questions. Subpart B is about declining. Subpart C is about conditions — the manner exception at 171.301 governs when limiting how a request is fulfilled is acceptable, and fees and licensing govern what may be charged and required. A refusal and a restrictive condition are both practices, and both need an exception.

Four questions for a vendor

Which USCDI version does the platform support, and where is that stated in writing? Is the product certified health IT? Is the company an actor under 45 CFR 171.102, and if so in which of the four capacities? And which of the nine exceptions does it expect to rely on when it declines or conditions a request — fees and licensing being the two that most often surface in a contract?

Those four convert an adjective into documents. A vendor genuinely selling breadth will have answers, because each answer is something they already had to determine internally.

The last of the four is the most revealing and the least often asked. Fees and licensing are the two exceptions that surface in commercial terms rather than in clinical judgement, and a contract that attaches a charge or a licence condition to data access is relying on one of them whether or not it says so. Asking which exception a term sits under moves the conversation from what the platform can do to what it is permitted to charge for doing, and those are different questions with different answers.

What 360 cannot mean

It cannot mean every record everywhere. Data that was never digitised, never coded to a USCDI element, or held by an organisation that is not an actor is outside the machinery regardless of the platform.

It cannot mean unconditional either. Nine exceptions exist precisely because some withholding is lawful — preventing harm and privacy among them — and a product that promised to override those would be promising something the rules do not permit.

What it can honestly mean is: a defined version of a defined standard, assembled from a defined set of sources, by a party with a defined status under the rule. That is narrower than the marketing and far more useful, because every clause in it can be checked before signing. Questions people actually ask

Is 360 a recognised category in health care?

No. It is a marketing suffix meaning comprehensive, attached to at least two unrelated product families: employer wellness and population health platforms, and clinical data platforms promising a complete view of a patient. Neither is a federal category, and no agency certifies the term. What can be checked is the underlying claim, against a published data standard and a published set of rules on withholding data.

What is USCDI?

The United States Core Data for Interoperability, described by ASTP as a standardized set of health data classes and constituent data elements for nationwide, interoperable health information exchange. A data class is an aggregation of data elements by a common theme or use case; a data element is a piece of data defined in USCDI for access, exchange or use of electronic health information. Versions advance through the Standards Version Advancement Process described in the Cures rule.

What counts as information blocking?

Under 45 CFR 171.103 it is a practice that interferes with access, exchange or use of electronic health information, except where required by law or covered by an exception. The knowledge standard differs by actor: for health IT developers, health information networks and exchanges the actor knows or should know the practice will interfere, while for a health care provider the provider knows the practice is unreasonable and will interfere.

Who is bound by the information blocking rule?

45 CFR 171.102 defines an actor as one of four things: a health care provider, a health IT developer of certified health IT, a health information network, or a health information exchange. A vendor selling a platform that is not certified health IT and does not operate as a network or exchange may sit outside the definition entirely, which is worth establishing before relying on the rule.

How many exceptions are there?

Nine, in two groups. Subpart B covers not fulfilling requests: preventing harm at 171.201, privacy at 171.202, security at 171.203, infeasibility at 171.204, health IT performance at 171.205 and protecting care access at 171.206. Subpart C covers procedures for fulfilling requests: manner at 171.301, fees at 171.302 and licensing at 171.303.

Is there a limit on wellness programme rewards?

For health-contingent programmes, yes. 45 CFR 146.121(f) sets the reward at no more than the applicable percentage of the total cost of employee-only coverage, which 146.121(f)(5) puts at up to 30 percent, or 50 percent for tobacco-related programmes. The programme must also offer a reasonable alternative standard and must give eligible individuals the opportunity to qualify at least once per year.

What should I ask a vendor selling a 360 view?

Which USCDI version the platform supports, whether the product is certified health IT, whether the company is an actor under 45 CFR 171.102, and which of the nine exceptions it expects to invoke when declining a request. Those four questions convert an adjective into claims that can be checked against published documents.
