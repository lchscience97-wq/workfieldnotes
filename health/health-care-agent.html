Health Care Agent: The Role Federal Law Only Asks About

Last verified
2 Sep 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal; appointment governed by State law
Data sources
42 U.S.C. 1395cc(f); 42 CFR 489.100, 489.102; 42 CFR 482.13; 45 CFR 164.502(g)
Sources
5 cited
Reading time
10 min

No federal statute creates a health care agent. The appointment is made under state law, and federal regulation says so expressly rather than filling the gap.

What federal law guarantees is narrower and still valuable: that a participating provider gives you written information about your state-law rights, records in a prominent part of your chart whether you have a directive, does not condition care on the answer, and complies with state law. Separately, your agent is treated as you for records purposes. Five duties, one exception, and one document that has to be made somewhere else.

What the word names

A health care agent is the person who decides for you when you cannot decide for yourself. Different states call the role different things — agent, proxy, surrogate, attorney-in-fact for health care — and the instrument that appoints them carries different names too.

Federal regulation sidesteps the naming problem by referring to the document. 42 CFR 489.100 defines an advance directive as a written instruction, such as a living will or durable power of attorney for health care, recognized under State law (whether statutory or as recognized by the courts of the State), relating to the provision of health care when the individual is incapacitated. The statute uses effectively the same words at 42 U.S.C. 1395cc(f)(3).

Read that definition closely and the architecture is already visible. Every operative term — what counts as valid, who may be appointed, what they may decide — is handed to state law. The federal layer is deliberately thin.

The appointment is state law, and only state law

This is the single most useful thing to understand, because it explains why general advice about agents is so often wrong for the reader. Witnessing requirements, notarisation, whether the appointment survives a move across state lines, whether the agent may decline artificial nutrition, whether a spouse is automatically substituted — all state questions.

The federal rules do not merely stay silent on these. 42 CFR 489.102(a)(4) affirmatively requires a provider to ensure compliance with requirements of State law regarding advance directives. The regulation points outward on purpose.

The practical consequence is that a template downloaded without regard to jurisdiction is a gamble, and the question to research is always your own state’s statute. Where a document turns into a dispute, the sorting of which professional is needed is in which kind you actually need.

What federal law actually guarantees

42 U.S.C. 1395cc(f) and 42 CFR 489.102 impose five duties on participating providers, and each is worth reading as a right you hold.

First, information. The provider must provide written information about your rights under state law including the right to accept or refuse medical or surgical treatment and to formulate advance directives, together with the provider’s own written policies on implementing those rights — including a clear and precise statement of limitation if the provider cannot implement an advance directive on the basis of conscience.

Second, documentation. The provider must document in a prominent part of the individual’s current medical record whether or not the individual has executed an advance directive. Third, non-discrimination. Fourth, state-law compliance. Fifth, education of staff and of the community.

Why the documentation duty is the operative one

A directive that exists and cannot be found does nothing. The requirement is not merely that the record mention it, but that it be documented in a prominent part of the individual’s current medical record — language chosen because the failure mode is burial, not omission.

That makes this a records-management duty as much as a rights duty, which is why it lands on the people described in the duties attached to the chair. When a patient transfers between organisations, the same problem recurs at the boundary, which is the subject of who moves your record.

The practical inference for a patient is simple. Giving a copy to a hospital once is not a filing system. Confirm it is in the current record at each admission, and give your agent a copy directly, because the fastest route to a document is often the person holding it.

The clause that cannot be traded away

42 CFR 489.102(a)(3) requires the provider to not condition the provision of care or otherwise discriminate against an individual based on whether or not the individual has executed an advance directive.

It runs in both directions. Care cannot be withheld because you have not made one, and it cannot be adjusted because you have. Anything presented as a condition of admission on this point is contrary to the rule, and the written policy the provider is separately obliged to hand you is the document to read against it.

The agent and your records

Decision authority and record access are different powers, joined by one provision. Under 45 CFR 164.502(g)(1) a covered entity must, except as provided in paragraphs (g)(3) and (g)(5)…, treat a personal representative as the individual for purposes of this subchapter.

And (g)(2) supplies the link: where under applicable law a person has authority to act on behalf of an individual who is an adult or an emancipated minor in making decisions related to health care, the entity must treat that person as a personal representative. State law supplies the authority; the privacy rule follows it without adding a separate appointment.

The exception at (g)(5) is narrow and deliberate. An entity may decline recognition where it reasonably believes the individual has been or may be subjected to domestic violence, abuse or neglect by that person, or that recognition could endanger the individual, and a professional decides in the exercise of professional judgment that declining is in the individual’s best interests. The patient-side view of the same records right is in your record and your right of access.

What the hospital owes on top

Hospitals carry an additional condition of participation. 42 CFR 482.13(b)(3) provides that the patient has the right to formulate advance directives and to have hospital staff and practitioners who provide care in the hospital comply with these directives.

Around it sit two related rights: at (b)(1) the patient has the right to participate in the development and implementation of his or her plan of care, and at (b)(2) the patient or representative has the right to make informed decisions regarding his or her care, including being informed of health status, being involved in care planning and treatment, and being able to request or refuse treatment.

Read together with the personal representative rule, that is the agent’s working authority inside a hospital: participate in the plan, receive the information, decide, and have the directive complied with. What binds each type of facility more generally is mapped in the federal list, and what binds each.

What to actually do with this

Work outward from the state instrument. Find your own state’s form or statutory requirements first, because that is the only step no federal rule performs for you, and the formalities are the part that fails.

Then use the federal duties as a checklist at the point of care. Ask for the provider’s written policy on advance directives, which you are entitled to receive. Ask whether the directive is documented in the current record, in the words the regulation uses. If a conscience-based limitation applies, the provider owes you a clear and precise statement of it, and that is the moment to learn it rather than during a crisis.

Finally, tell the agent. The appointment is a legal act, but the job is an informational one, and an agent who learns of the role from a nurse is starting at a disadvantage the paperwork cannot fix.

What an agent is not

Not a guardian, which is a court appointment with a different scope. Not a payer, and not a party to your bills by virtue of the role. Not a free statutory advocate either — the programmes that supply those, and the conflict rules that define them, are covered in what the free ones may not be.

And not a substitute for a capable patient. Every rule above operates on incapacity; while you can decide, you decide. The agent is an instruction for a moment that may never arrive, which is exactly why it is worth writing before it does.

Questions people actually ask

What is a health care agent?

The person you appoint to make health care decisions for you when you cannot make them yourself. The appointment is created by state law. Federal regulation refers to the instrument rather than the person: 42 CFR 489.100 defines an advance directive as a written instruction, such as a living will or durable power of attorney for health care, recognized under State law, whether statutory or as recognized by the courts of the State, relating to the provision of health care when the individual is incapacitated.

Does federal law create the role?

No. Federal law requires that you be asked about it and that the answer be handled properly. Under 42 U.S.C. 1395cc(f) and 42 CFR 489.102 a participating provider must give written information about your rights under State law to make decisions concerning medical care and to formulate advance directives, document in a prominent part of your current medical record whether or not you have executed one, not condition care or discriminate based on whether you have, ensure compliance with State law, and educate staff and the community.

Can a hospital refuse to treat me because I have no advance directive?

No. 42 CFR 489.102(a)(3) requires a provider not to condition the provision of care or otherwise discriminate against an individual based on whether or not the individual has executed an advance directive. The same protection runs the other way: having one cannot be used against you either.

Can my agent see my medical records?

Generally yes, through the personal representative rule. Under 45 CFR 164.502(g)(1) a covered entity must treat a personal representative as the individual, and (g)(2) provides that where under applicable law a person has authority to act on behalf of an adult in making decisions related to health care, the covered entity must treat that person as a personal representative. The authority comes from state law; HIPAA follows it.

Are there situations where the hospital can refuse to recognise my agent?

Yes, narrowly. Under 45 CFR 164.502(g)(5) a covered entity may decline to treat a person as a personal representative where it reasonably believes the individual has been or may be subjected to domestic violence, abuse or neglect by that person, or that treating them as the representative could endanger the individual, and a professional in the exercise of professional judgment decides that non-recognition is in the individual's best interests.

What must a hospital do about my directive once it has it?

42 CFR 482.13(b)(3) states that the patient has the right to formulate advance directives and to have hospital staff and practitioners who provide care in the hospital comply with these directives. The provider must also disclose its own written policies on implementing those rights, including a clear and precise statement of limitation if it cannot implement a directive on the basis of conscience.

Where does the appointment actually get made?

Under state law, through the instrument your state recognises, which varies in name, in witnessing and notarisation requirements, and in what the agent may decide. Because federal regulation defers to State law expressly, the operative document is a state one and its formalities are the ones that determine whether the appointment works when it is needed.
