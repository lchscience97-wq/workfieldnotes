Three of these four terms are used interchangeably and none of them mean the same thing. HIT is a category. HIE is an activity inside it. An EHR is a product type. Health informatics is an academic field. Only one of the four has a definition you can test a product against.

That one is certification. The ONC Health IT Certification Program, established in 2010 under the HITECH Act, tests products against published criteria through accredited laboratories. And the adoption question it was built to solve is finished: by 2024, over 99 per cent of non-federal acute care hospitals had a certified EHR.

Who is answering this, and what they are answering

The results for this term split cleanly in two. Federal sites publish policy and programme material written for institutions. University pages sell degrees. Neither group has a reason to define the vocabulary, because each is speaking to an audience that already shares it.

The gap is a plain glossary with the regulatory status of each term attached — which of these words is a legal category, which is a product description, and which is marketing. That is what this page is.

The third column is the useful one. When a vendor, a job posting or a programme page uses one of these words, the question that resolves most ambiguity is whether anything about the claim is externally checkable — and for exactly one row, it is.

The one place the term becomes testable

The ONC Health IT Certification Program is described as a third-party conformity assessment program for health IT based on the principles within the ISO and IEC framework, established in 2010, enabling voluntary certification of health information technology products.

Its authority is statutory. The programme operates under the authority granted by section 3001(c)(5) of the Public Health Service Act (PHSA) and as defined in the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act. That is why the criteria are set in federal regulation rather than by a trade association — and why they change on a rulemaking cycle rather than a product cycle.

The word doing the most work in that description is voluntary. Certification is not a licence to sell. A product can be uncertified and perfectly functional; what it cannot do is claim conformance with the criteria.

What certification asserts, and what it does not

Certification demonstrates that health IT meets specific functional requirements called certification criteria, established by ONC through federal regulation, with developers showing conformance using approved testing methods.

Two bodies do the work, and the split is worth knowing because vendors sometimes blur it. ONC-ATLs are NVLAP-accredited testing laboratories that have been authorized by ONC to perform health IT testing to determine conformance with ONC's standards and certification criteria. ONC-ACBs certify health IT based on information gathered and provided by ONC-ATLs and maintain the public listing.

Testing and certifying are therefore separate functions performed by separate organisations, which is the structural feature that makes the result meaningful rather than self-declared. The output is public: the Certified Health IT Product List (CHPL) provides an authoritative, comprehensive listing of Health IT Modules that have been tested and certified, updated at minimum weekly.

What certification does not assert is that a product is good, that it suits your organisation, or that it will interoperate in practice with any particular counterparty. It asserts conformance with named criteria, and nothing wider.

HIE: two directions, not one thing

ONC defines the activity as follows: Electronic health information exchange (HIE) allows doctors, nurses, public health professionals, pharmacists, other healthcare providers, and patients to appropriately access and securely share a patient's vital medical information electronically—improving the speed, quality, safety, and cost of patient care.

It comes in two forms, and confusing them is the commonest error in this vocabulary.

Directed exchange — push. Used by providers to easily and securely send patient information—such as laboratory orders and results, patient referrals, or discharge summaries—directly to another health care professional, over encrypted connections among trusted professionals. The sender knows the recipient.

Query-based exchange — pull. Used by providers to search and discover accessible clinical sources on a patient, the case ONC illustrates with an emergency clinician retrieving medications and recent imaging.

The distinction is not academic. Push has an identified recipient and a purpose known at the moment of sending; pull involves searching sources the requester did not select in advance. They therefore raise different questions about who authorised what, which connects directly to the individual's right of access set out in your record and your right of access.

EHR: adoption is finished, so the problem moved

The programme was built during a period when the open question was whether records were electronic at all. That question has closed. As of 2024, more than 99 per cent of non-federal acute care hospitals and 91 per cent of office-based physicians had adopted a certified electronic health record.

Two readings follow. The first is that hospital adoption is effectively universal, so any argument or programme premised on getting hospitals onto electronic records is describing a finished project. The second is the 8 percentage point gap between the two figures: office-based practice trails hospitals, and the residual is concentrated in the small-practice end where the cost and support burden falls on the fewest people.

With adoption complete, the live questions are what happens between systems rather than inside them — which is why exchange, data quality and usability now occupy the space adoption used to.

The occupation the acronym leads to

Degree pages for this term point at a specific federal occupation, and it is worth seeing its actual dimensions. BLS records health information technologists and medical registrars at a May 2024 median annual wage of $67,310, or $32.36 an hour, with 41,900 jobs in 2024, 15 per cent projected growth to 2034 and about 3,200 annual openings. Typical entry-level education is an associate degree, and employers may prefer or require certification, with credentials available from professional organisations.

Three things stand out when those numbers are handled together.

The wage is above the national median. Against the $49,500 median for all occupations, $67,310 is $17,810 higher, about 36.0 per cent.

The occupation is small. 41,900 jobs is a fraction of adjacent healthcare occupations, and 3,200 annual openings is a modest absolute number however healthy the percentage looks.

Turnover is about 7.6 per cent a year. That is 3,200 openings against 41,900 jobs — a reasonable rate of door-opening within a small population.

What the programme pages leave out

A 15 per cent growth rate is accurate and also easy to misread. Fifteen per cent of a small base is a small number of jobs, and a prospectus quoting the percentage without the base is telling the truth in a way that overstates the opportunity.

The second omission is the ceiling. The associate-degree route leads into an occupation with a $67,310 median; the management occupation adjacent to it has a median of $117,960, a gap of $50,650, and enters at bachelor's level with prior healthcare experience expected. The technical credential does not by itself cross that gap. What does, and what the various degrees actually buy, is examined in five paths, one title.

The wider structure of the sector's labour market — and the licensing boundary that organises its pay — is in two tiers, $45,910 apart, with the records and coding specialisms adjacent to this occupation covered in roles, pay and credentials.

Reading a posting or a prospectus

Is the claim about a certified module? If so it is checkable on the Certified Health IT Product List. If it is about "HIT capability" generally, there is nothing to check it against.

Push or pull? A role or product described as doing exchange is doing one of two quite different things, and the governance around each differs.

What is the base behind the growth rate? Ask for the number of jobs, not the percentage.

Which occupation does this credential actually enter? The answer decides the wage benchmark, and it is frequently not the one the page's imagery implies. Where the technology sits inside the wider system is set out in how the system actually works.

Questions people actually ask

What does HIT stand for, and is it a defined term?

Health information technology. As a general phrase it is not defined anywhere binding. The one place it becomes testable is the ONC Health IT Certification Program, established in 2010, which is described as a third-party conformity assessment program for health IT based on the principles within the ISO and IEC framework. Outside that programme, HIT is a category label rather than a specification.

What does certified health IT actually mean?

That a product has been tested against specific functional requirements called certification criteria, established by ONC through federal regulation. Testing is done by ONC-ATLs, which are NVLAP-accredited laboratories authorised to determine conformance, and certification is issued by ONC-ACBs. Certified products appear on the Certified Health IT Product List, updated at minimum weekly.

What is the difference between HIT and HIE?

HIT is the technology category; HIE is a specific activity within it. ONC defines electronic health information exchange as allowing clinicians and patients to appropriately access and securely share a patient's vital medical information electronically. It takes two forms: directed exchange, which pushes information to a known recipient, and query-based exchange, which pulls information by searching for accessible clinical sources.

Where does the certification programme get its authority?

The programme operates under the authority granted by section 3001(c)(5) of the Public Health Service Act and as defined in the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act, known as HITECH. That statutory grounding is why certification criteria are set by regulation rather than by an industry body.

Is EHR adoption still the problem?

Not by the adoption measure. As of 2024, more than 99 per cent of non-federal acute care hospitals and 91 per cent of office-based physicians had adopted a certified electronic health record. With hospital adoption effectively complete, the remaining questions concern exchange, usability and data quality rather than whether systems exist.

What job does a health information technology qualification lead to?

Most directly, health information technologists and medical registrars. BLS records a May 2024 median annual wage of $67,310, 41,900 jobs in 2024, 15 per cent projected growth to 2034 and about 3,200 annual openings, with an associate degree as typical entry-level education and certification often preferred by employers.

Is 41,900 jobs a small occupation?

Yes, and that is the fact degree pages tend not to foreground. About 3,200 projected openings a year against 41,900 jobs is roughly 7.6 per cent annual turnover, which is healthy, but the absolute number of openings is small compared with adjacent healthcare occupations. A programme's growth-rate claim can be accurate while the number of doors stays modest.
