Health Care Options: Six Routes and Their Clocks

Last verified
10 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources

The hard part of this question is not which option is best. It is which clock is running. Marketplace enrolment closes on 15 January. Medicare gives a seven-month window around your 65th birthday. COBRA has a deadline and a price — up to 102 per cent of what the plan costs.

Only two routes have no deadline at all: Medicaid and CHIP, which accept applications any time of year, and federally funded health centres, which must serve you regardless of ability to pay. Establish your deadline first; compare plans second.

Why the results feel incomplete

The pages ranking for this phrase are state exchange sites, federal portals and one large insurer. Each is accurate and each describes the route it operates. None of them sets the routes side by side, because none of them administers more than one.

So a person who does not already know which category they fall into reads three pages that each assume the answer. This page is the missing comparison. It does not name or rate any plan or insurer.

Read the third column first. Four of the six can be missed. Two cannot.

That asymmetry is the practical core of this page. A person weighing options usually starts by comparing what each covers and what each costs, which is reasonable and also the wrong order. Coverage and cost are properties you can evaluate at leisure; a deadline is a property that removes options while you evaluate. Someone who spends three weeks comparing Marketplace plans in early January and someone who spends the same three weeks in February have done the same work to entirely different effect.

The two routes with no clock are also the two that most people assume they do not qualify for, which compounds the error. Medicaid eligibility is set state by state on several factors at once, and health centre fees scale with income rather than switching off above a line. Both are worth testing precisely because testing them costs nothing and forecloses nothing.

The Marketplace window, and the date inside it

HealthCare.gov sets out the annual cycle: November 1: Open Enrollment starts and January 15: Open Enrollment ends — last day to enroll in or change Marketplace health plans for the year.

There is a second date inside that window which matters more than most people realise. Coverage can start as soon as January 1 for those enrolling by 15 December. For anyone who enrols or changes plans between 16 December and 15 January and pays the first premium, coverage starts on 1 February.

So the practical deadline is not one date but two, and the gap between them is a month of coverage. Deciding in the first half of December and deciding in the first half of January produce the same plan with a different start date.

Outside that window, a Special Enrollment Period is a period of time outside of Open Enrollment when you can enroll in or change Marketplace plans due to a life event, like moving, losing other coverage, getting married, or having a baby.

The route with no deadline

HealthCare.gov states it directly: You can apply for Medicaid and CHIP any time of year.

Eligibility is not a single national number. Each state has coverage options that consider income, household size, family status (like pregnancy or caring for young children), disability, age, and other factors, and some states have expanded their Medicaid programs to cover all people with household incomes below a certain level.

Two consequences follow. First, an income figure that disqualified you in one state may not in another, and a figure that disqualified you last year may not this year. Second — and this is the part worth acting on — because there is no deadline, applying costs nothing but time, and the answer is authoritative in a way that a self-assessment is not.

Seven months, then eight

Medicare's Initial Enrollment Period runs starting 3 months before you turn 65 and ending 3 months after the month you turn 65 — a seven-month window with your birthday month in the middle.

For people still working there is a separate route. Medicare notes that with job-based insurance a Special Enrollment Period runs within 8 months after you or your spouse stop working, and that this period begins when you stop working, even if you choose COBRA or other coverage.

That last clause is the trap. Electing COBRA does not pause the eight-month Medicare window. Someone who leaves work at 66, takes COBRA for its full run and then turns to Medicare can find the special window has closed while they were covered. The two clocks run at the same time, and only one of them is visible on a monthly invoice.

What COBRA is, and what it costs

The Department of Labor describes COBRA as giving workers and their families who lose their health benefits the right to choose to continue group health benefits provided by their group health plan for limited periods of time under certain circumstances.

It applies to a defined set of employers: the law generally requires that group health plans sponsored by employers with 20 or more employees in the prior year offer employees and their families the opportunity for a temporary extension of health coverage.

The price is the part that catches people. Qualified individuals may be required to pay the entire premium for coverage up to 102% of the cost to the plan.

Read entire premium carefully. While employed, most people see only their payroll deduction; the employer paid a share that never appeared on a payslip. COBRA can charge both halves, plus 2 per cent for administration. The monthly figure is therefore frequently several times the number someone was used to, and it is not an error.

The route that does not require coverage

The last row of the table is the one that is not insurance at all. HRSA requires funded health centres to serve everyone, even if they cannot pay and to adjust their fees based on income and family size.

Because that is a grant condition rather than a discount policy, it does not depend on enrolment, deadlines or a card in your wallet. And the income band reaches further than most people assume: HRSA reports that about 90 per cent of health centre patients have incomes at or below 200 per cent of the federal poverty level, which against the 2026 guidelines for the 48 contiguous states is $31,920 for one person and $66,000 for a household of four.

A working order of operations

First, find your deadline. If it is Medicaid or a health centre, there isn't one. If it is Marketplace, Medicare or COBRA, write the date down before anything else.

Second, apply where applying is free. Medicaid and CHIP cost nothing to test and the determination is authoritative.

Third, compare like with like. A COBRA quote at 102 per cent of plan cost and a Marketplace premium after savings are not the same kind of number, and neither is a sliding-scale fee.

Fourth, check what any plan must cover before comparing prices. The ten categories every Marketplace plan must include are listed in what the law requires covered, and why a covered service can still be billed at out-of-network rates is in two meanings, one search box. Where to go once you have coverage — and which door owes you what — is in which door, and what it must do.

Questions people actually ask

When does Marketplace open enrollment run?

HealthCare.gov states that Open Enrollment starts 1 November and ends 15 January, the last day to enroll in or change Marketplace health plans for the year. Coverage can start as soon as 1 January for those who enroll by 15 December; those who enroll or change plans between 16 December and 15 January and pay the first premium start on 1 February.

Can I apply for Medicaid outside open enrollment?

Yes. HealthCare.gov states plainly that you can apply for Medicaid and CHIP any time of year. Eligibility is set state by state and considers income, household size, family status such as pregnancy or caring for young children, disability and age, so applying is worthwhile even if the income figure looks too high.

How long is the Medicare initial enrollment period?

Seven months. Medicare describes it as starting 3 months before you turn 65 and ending 3 months after the month you turn 65. If you miss it you may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period; for people with job-based insurance that window runs within 8 months after you or your spouse stop working, and it begins when the work stops even if you take COBRA.

What does COBRA actually cost?

Up to the full price of the coverage plus an administrative margin. The Department of Labor states that qualified individuals may be required to pay the entire premium for coverage up to 102 per cent of the cost to the plan. That is the employer share and the employee share together, plus 2 per cent, which is why the figure surprises people who only ever saw a payroll deduction.

Which employers does COBRA apply to?

The Department of Labor states that COBRA generally requires group health plans sponsored by employers with 20 or more employees in the prior year to offer a temporary extension of coverage. Below that threshold the federal rule does not apply, though some states operate their own continuation requirements.

Is there an option that does not depend on insurance at all?

Yes. HRSA-funded health centres must serve everyone even if they cannot pay and must adjust fees based on income and family size. That obligation is a condition of the grant rather than a discount policy, and it applies regardless of whether you hold any coverage.

What should I check first?

Which clock is running. Medicaid and CHIP accept applications year-round, so there is no deadline to miss. Marketplace, Medicare and COBRA each have a defined window, and missing one usually means waiting for the next annual period. Establish the deadline before comparing plans, because a comparison you finish too late buys nothing.
