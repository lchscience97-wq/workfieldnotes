Health Care Administration Degree Salary: No Federal Source Says That

Last verified
20 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources
BLS OEWS; BLS OOH May 2024; U.S. Dept of Education
Sources

The federal wage survey does not know what degree anyone holds. BLS says so directly: the OEWS survey does not gather demographic information. Education is demographic information.

So a figure presented as the salary for this degree is not coming from the federal wage data, whatever it is coming from. What federal data does support is a wage for an occupation — $117,960 median for medical and health services managers in May 2024 — and, separately, earnings for graduates of a named programme at a named school. This page explains why those are different questions and which one you actually have.

What the search is asking for

The phrase joins two things that federal statistics keep apart. A degree is an educational credential. A salary is a property of a job. The query assumes a table exists mapping one to the other.

The results are salary aggregators and education marketing sites, both of which will supply a number. The number is not from the source people assume it is from, and knowing that changes how much weight it should carry.

Why BLS cannot answer it

The Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics programme is the source behind almost every wage figure quoted in American career writing. Its own documentation states the limitation plainly: the OEWS survey does not gather demographic information.

Education and degrees held fall inside that exclusion. OEWS asks establishments what they pay for particular occupations; it does not ask who holds what qualification. The programme produces employment and wage estimates for approximately 830 occupations, and that is the unit it works in.

There is a second boundary worth knowing while you are here. OEWS states that its wage estimates represent wages and salaries only, and do not include nonproduction bonuses or employer costs of nonwage benefits, such as health insurance or employer contributions to retirement plans. For a management role where bonus can be a meaningful share of compensation, the published median is therefore a floor on total package rather than a measure of it.

For demographic breakdowns, BLS points users elsewhere: the Current Population Survey provides information on employment, unemployment, and weekly earnings by a variety of demographic characteristics. That is a household survey with a different design and much broader categories — not a source for programme-level outcomes either.

What BLS does publish

The occupation this degree name points at is medical and health services managers. For May 2024 BLS reports a median annual wage of $117,960, with 616,200 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 23 percent over 2024–34 and about 62,100 openings a year.

On entry it lists a bachelor's degree as typical, while noting that some employers hire candidates with an associate's degree; others prefer to hire those with a master's, and that work experience sometimes may substitute for education. It also states that prospective managers typically need work experience in an administrative or clinical role first.

Notice what that last sentence does to the question. If experience is part of entry, then a wage figure attached to a credential alone is describing a person who does not exist in the data. The one federal dataset that ties a credential to earnings

There is a federal source that connects a programme to money, and it is not BLS.

The Department of Education's College Scorecard publishes, in its own words, earnings of students one-year after graduation by field of study and cumulative loan debt of borrowers by field of study.

That is a genuinely useful instrument and a narrow one. It measures graduates of a particular programme at a particular institution, at a particular point after completion. It is not a forecast for a career, and it is not comparable to an occupational median, because the populations differ: everyone in an occupation versus everyone who finished one course.

Used correctly, it answers the question how did this school's graduates do rather than what does this degree pay. Those look similar in a headline and behave very differently in a decision.

Programme earnings and occupation wages are not the same number

Three differences matter.

Population. An occupational median covers everyone working in the occupation, whatever their route in. A programme earnings figure covers people who completed one programme, wherever they ended up working — including outside the field entirely.

Timing. An occupational median is a snapshot across all career stages. A one-year-after figure is a snapshot at the earliest and lowest point of a career, which is exactly when a management-track graduate has not yet reached management.

Selection. Neither figure controls for who chose what. People who enter a programme differ from people who do not, and the difference in outcomes is not automatically caused by the programme.

What the aggregator numbers are

Salary aggregators are not fabricating figures. They are reporting what they have: job postings, employer submissions, or user-reported salaries, in proportions and samples that are usually not published.

Three consequences follow. Posting-based figures reflect what employers advertise, which skews toward vacancies rather than incumbents. Self-report figures reflect who chose to submit, which is not a random sample. And neither is anchored to a standard occupational definition, so two sites can label the same title differently and both be internally consistent.

The usable move is to treat an aggregator figure as a range indicator and to check whether its stated method is published at all. Where it is not, the number has no error bar you can reason about.

Using the sources together

Start from the occupation you intend to enter, because that is what federal wage data measures, and read the spread by setting rather than the single median. Then use programme-level earnings as a check on a specific school you are considering, not as a forecast for yourself. Then treat aggregator figures as a sanity check on the local market, not as evidence.

And keep the credential question separate from the wage question. Our guides to five paths behind one title and the associate as a transfer credential cover what the degrees actually buy, which is access to a competition rather than a price.

What no dataset will tell you

None of these sources will tell you what you will earn, because none of them is measuring you. They measure occupations, cohorts and postings.

None will isolate the effect of the degree either, because that would require comparing people who took it with otherwise identical people who did not, and no federal dataset is built that way.

What they will do, read in the right order, is bound the question honestly: this is what the job pays across the country, this is what one school's graduates earned a year out, and this is what the market is currently advertising. Three real numbers answering three real questions is a better basis than one number answering a question nobody actually measured.

Questions people actually ask

Does BLS publish a salary for this degree?

No. The Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics programme states plainly that the OEWS survey does not gather demographic information, which places education and degrees held outside its scope. OEWS produces employment and wage estimates for approximately 830 occupations. It measures what a job pays, not what a graduate of a named programme earns.

What does BLS actually measure?

Wages and salaries for occupations, and only those. OEWS states that its wage estimates represent wages and salaries only, and do not include nonproduction bonuses or employer costs of nonwage benefits such as health insurance or employer contributions to retirement plans. For demographic breakdowns BLS directs users to the Current Population Survey instead.

What does the target occupation pay?

Medical and health services managers had a median annual wage of $117,960 in May 2024, with 616,200 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 23 percent over 2024–34 and about 62,100 openings a year. BLS lists the typical entry-level education as a bachelor's degree and notes that prospective managers typically need prior work experience in an administrative or clinical role.

Is there any federal earnings data tied to a degree?

Yes, but it is a different instrument. The Department of Education's College Scorecard publishes earnings of students one year after graduation by field of study, alongside cumulative loan debt by field of study. That measures graduates of a specific programme at a specific institution rather than everyone working in an occupation, so it answers a narrower question than the one most people are asking.

Why do aggregator figures differ so much?

Because they are measuring different populations by different methods. Job-board and salary-aggregator figures typically come from postings or self-reported submissions, with sample composition that is not published and not designed to be representative. Two sites can both be reporting honestly and still differ by tens of thousands of dollars, because neither is drawing from the same frame.

So how should I estimate what I would earn?

Anchor on the occupation you intend to enter rather than the degree you intend to buy, because that is what federal wage data measures. Then adjust for setting and geography using the occupational data, and treat programme-level earnings as a check on a specific school rather than a forecast for you. The degree is an input to the occupation, not a separate wage category.

Does a higher degree raise the figure?

BLS does not publish a wage split by degree held, so no federal figure supports a specific premium. What the handbook does say is that some employers hire candidates with an associate's degree while others prefer a master's, and that work experience sometimes may substitute for education. That is a description of employer variation, not a measured return on a credential.
