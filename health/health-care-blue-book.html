The phrase names a commercial product, and the useful question is what it is built on. Since the hospital price transparency rule took effect, every hospital must publish a machine-readable file of all standard charges for all items and services, plus at least 300 shoppable services in a consumer-friendly format.

That data is free. It is also harder to read than a product, which is the honest case for paying for one. This page sets out what is published, in what five forms, and what a hospital risks by not publishing it — up to $5,500 a day.

What the phrase names

Search this term and you get a branded price-comparison product and a set of organisations that use one. The underlying idea — a reference book of fair prices, by analogy to used cars — is a good one and the reason the name works.

The generalisable question is where such a product gets its numbers. Part of the answer is now federal, public and free, and knowing what is in it tells you what a paid tool is adding: organisation, comparability and interpretation, rather than access.

Five kinds of price, not one

45 CFR 180.20 defines a standard charge as the regular rate established by the hospital for an item or service provided to a specific group of paying patients. The definition is plural by design, and the rule requires five distinct figures.

The gross charge is the chargemaster amount without discounts. The discounted cash price is the charge for patients paying in cash or a cash equivalent. The payer-specific negotiated charge is the amount agreed with an individual third-party payer. The de-identified minimum and maximum negotiated charges are the lowest and highest negotiated rates across all payers.

The de-identified minimum and maximum deserve a note of their own. They exist so a hospital can publish the range without revealing whose contract sits at either end, which is what made requiring negotiated figures possible at all. For a patient they are the honest summary: the spread between the best and the worst deal a hospital has struck for the same service, with no way to tell which payer got which.

That structure resolves a confusion people meet constantly. There is no such thing as the price of a hospital procedure. There are at least five, and a quoted figure is meaningless until you know which one it is. The one most people need — the discounted cash price — is required to be published, not merely available on request.

The machine-readable file

Under 45 CFR 180.50 a hospital must publish a file containing all standard charges for all items and services. The rule is unusually prescriptive about form, which is what makes the data usable at all.

Format must comply with CMS templates as of 1 July 2024, under 180.50(c)(2). The file must be named to CMS specification — <ein>_<hospital-name>_standardcharges with a json or csv extension — under 180.50(d)(5). It must carry hospital identifiers, payer names, coding information and setting type, inpatient or outpatient. From 1 January 2026, hospitals must attest to accuracy and include percentile-based allowed amounts under 180.50(a)(3)(iii). The file must be updated at least annually under 180.50(e).

The naming rule is the quietly important one. A predictable filename means the files can be found and compared across hospitals without asking anyone's permission, which is the difference between disclosure and transparency.

The 300 shoppable services

A file of every charge is not a consumer product, so the rule requires a second, friendlier disclosure.

Under 45 CFR 180.60(a)(1) a hospital must display standard charges for at least 300 shoppable services — those that can be scheduled in advance — combining CMS-specified services and hospital-selected ones. Under (a)(2) a hospital may instead maintain an internet-based price estimator tool providing estimates for at least 300 services.

The display must include plain-language descriptions, payer-specific negotiated charges, discounted cash prices and the de-identified ranges, and must be updated at least annually with clear dates under 180.60(e).

Note the alternative at (a)(2). A hospital offering an estimator has satisfied the requirement without publishing a browsable list, which is why the experience of looking up a price varies so much between hospitals that are both fully compliant.

What non-compliance costs

The rule has escalating enforcement, and the amounts are worth knowing because they explain why compliance improved.

Under 180.70(b), CMS may issue a written warning notice, then request a corrective action plan where the violation is material, then impose civil monetary penalties for failure to comply with that plan.

The penalty structure at 180.90(c)(2) is scaled by size: $300 a day for hospitals with 30 or fewer beds; beds multiplied by $10 a day for 31 to 550 beds; and $5,500 a day above 550 beds, adjusted annually by the OMB multiplier.

The scaling is smoother than the three tiers make it look. At 31 beds the daily amount is $310, barely above the $300 floor, and it climbs by $10 for each additional bed until 550 beds, where beds multiplied by $10 reaches $5,500 exactly. Above that it stops growing. A 600-bed hospital and a 1,200-bed hospital face the same daily figure, so the deterrent weakens in real terms as an institution gets larger.

Annualised, the floor is about $109,500 a year and the ceiling about $2 million a year. A 35 percent reduction is available under 180.90(c)(4) where a hospital waives appeal rights within 30 days, payment is due within 60 calendar days after notice under 180.90(d), and appeals run under 45 CFR Part 150 Subpart D.

The other half of the picture

Hospital data tells you what a facility charges. It does not tell you what your plan has agreed to pay, which is a different number held by a different party.

A parallel regime covers that side. The definitions at 45 CFR 147.210 establish the terms — machine-readable file as a digital representation of data or information in a file that can be imported or read by a computer system, along with negotiated rate, out-of-network allowed amount and cost-sharing liability — with the operative requirements at 45 CFR 147.211 and 147.212.

For most individual questions, though, neither file is the fastest route. If you are uninsured or self-pay, the written good faith estimate at 45 CFR 149.610 is a document addressed to you personally, covered in our guide to the estimate you can demand.

Using the free sources

Decide which of the five prices you need first. Uninsured or paying cash: the discounted cash price. Insured and in network: the payer-specific negotiated charge for your payer. Comparing hospitals in general: the de-identified minimum and maximum, which bound the range without naming payers.

Then pick the artefact. The shoppable services display or estimator is the consumer-facing route and covers at least 300 services. The machine-readable file covers everything and is built for software rather than for reading, which is precisely the gap commercial tools exist to fill.

One habit is worth building. Whichever artefact you open, note the hospital's EIN next to the figure. The filename convention ties the file to that identifier, so a price recorded six months ago can be rechecked against the current file without hunting for the hospital again, and two versions of the same file can be compared directly to see what moved.

And check the date. Both artefacts are required to be updated at least annually and to carry clear dates, so a figure without a date is a figure to distrust regardless of where it came from.

What a price list cannot tell you

It cannot tell you what you will owe, because that depends on your deductible, coinsurance, network status and out-of-pocket maximum — the dials covered in our guide to the one nobody caps. It cannot tell you how many separately billed items a single episode will generate, which is what makes real bills exceed quoted prices.

It cannot tell you quality, which is measured elsewhere and covered in the federal data behind the rankings. And it does not bind the hospital to a figure in the way a good faith estimate creates a dispute threshold.

What it does is remove the excuse for not knowing. Five named prices, published on a schedule, in a named file, with a daily penalty for absence, is a more solid foundation than any consumer of American health care had a decade ago — whether or not you pay someone to make it readable.

Questions people actually ask

Are hospital prices public?

Yes, by federal requirement. Under 45 CFR Part 180 a hospital must publish a machine-readable file containing all standard charges for all items and services, and must separately display standard charges for shoppable services in a consumer-friendly manner. Both must be updated at least annually, and both are free to access.

What counts as a standard charge?

45 CFR 180.20 defines it as the regular rate established by the hospital for an item or service provided to a specific group of paying patients. There are five types: the gross charge, the discounted cash price, the payer-specific negotiated charge, and the de-identified minimum and maximum negotiated charges. A single number for a procedure is therefore always one of five, and rarely the one you would pay.

What is the discounted cash price?

One of the five required standard charges: the charge that applies to patients paying in cash or a cash equivalent. It is the figure most relevant to someone not using insurance, and it must be published alongside the others rather than quoted only on request.

How many services must be shown in a friendly format?

At least 300. Under 45 CFR 180.60(a)(1) a hospital must display standard charges for at least 300 shoppable services, combining CMS-specified services and hospital-selected ones. Under (a)(2) a hospital may instead maintain an internet-based price estimator tool providing estimates for at least 300 services.

What happens if a hospital does not comply?

Enforcement escalates. CMS may issue a written warning notice, then request a corrective action plan for a material violation, then impose civil monetary penalties for failure to comply with that plan. Penalties are scaled by bed count: $300 a day for hospitals with 30 or fewer beds, beds multiplied by $10 a day for 31 to 550 beds, and $5,500 a day above 550 beds, adjusted annually.

Is there a discount for accepting a penalty?

Yes, for waiving appeal. Under 45 CFR 180.90(c)(4) a 35 percent reduction is available if the hospital waives its appeal rights within 30 days. Payment is otherwise due within 60 calendar days after notice under 180.90(d), and appeals run under 45 CFR Part 150 Subpart D.

Do health plans have to publish prices too?

A parallel regime applies to plans and issuers. The definitions at 45 CFR 147.210 establish terms including machine-readable file, negotiated rate, out-of-network allowed amount and cost-sharing liability, with the operative requirements at 45 CFR 147.211 and 147.212. Hospital data tells you what a facility charges; plan data tells you what your coverage has agreed to pay.
