Health Care Recruiter: Who Is Paying Them

Three different jobs share this title, and the results for it are dominated by job boards and staffing firms — that is, by the parties selling the service rather than describing it.

One question separates all three: who pays the recruiter. And one of the three — physician recruitment by hospitals — is the only branch with its own federal rules, sitting under the physician self-referral law and the anti-kickback statute. If you are a physician being offered a package, there are clauses that must be in the agreement.

Three jobs, one title

The first is the in-house recruiter: a salaried employee of a hospital, health system or group practice, filling that organisation's own vacancies. Their employer is the same organisation you would be joining.

The second is the agency recruiter: an employee of a staffing or search firm, paid by client organisations. This covers contingency search, retained search, travel nursing, per-diem staffing and locum tenens. The candidate is the product, not the customer.

The third is the physician recruiter, which sounds like a subset of the first two but is legally its own animal. When a hospital pays money to bring a physician into its area, it has created a financial relationship with someone who will refer patients to it — and federal law has a great deal to say about that.

The question that resolves it

In the standard model, the candidate never pays. In-house recruiters are salaried. Agency recruiters earn a placement fee or a margin on a bill rate charged to the client. Nothing in the ordinary operation of this market involves a jobseeker writing a cheque.

That gives you a clean test. If someone presenting themselves as a health care recruiter asks you for money — for placement, for a training bundle, for credentialing help, for priority access — they are selling something other than recruitment. The request itself is the finding; you do not need to evaluate the offer.

The subtler point applies to legitimate agency recruiters. Their fee is earned when a placement happens, not when the right placement happens. That is a structure rather than a character flaw, and the correct response is to treat them as a well-informed counterparty: useful for market intelligence, not a substitute for reading the contract.

What the statistics actually count

There is no BLS occupation called health care recruiter. Recruiters are counted inside human resources specialists, which BLS reports at a median annual wage of $72,910 in May 2024, with 944,300 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 6 percent over 2024–34 adding 58,400 positions, and about 81,800 openings a year.

BLS describes recruitment specialists — also called recruiters or talent acquisition specialists — as finding, screening and interviewing applicants, and notes that some organisations contract recruitment and placement work to outside firms in the employment services industry. Employment services firms employ 14 percent of all human resources specialists, which is roughly where the agency side of this market sits within the data.

For scale on the other side of the transaction: BLS projects about 1.9 million openings a year across healthcare occupations over 2024–34, against median annual wages of $83,090 for healthcare practitioners and technical occupations and $37,180 for healthcare support occupations. Our guide to the two tiers of health care jobs works through that split.

The one branch federal law regulates

The physician self-referral law, 42 U.S.C. §1395nn, prohibits a physician with a financial relationship with an entity from referring patients to it for designated health services unless an exception applies. A hospital paying a physician's relocation costs, income guarantee or signing incentive creates exactly such a relationship.

HHS OIG draws the distinction that matters here: the anti-kickback statute is a criminal law requiring knowing and willful conduct, so intent is an essential element, while the Stark law is a strict liability statute, which means proof of specific intent to violate the law is not required. In recruitment that asymmetry is the whole practical story. A defective recruitment agreement can be a problem on the Stark side even where nobody intended anything improper — which is why hospitals paper these arrangements carefully and why a physician should read what is in front of them.

What must be in a physician recruitment agreement

The recruitment exception lives at 42 CFR 411.357(e), and its conditions are specific enough to check against a draft.

Under (e)(1) the arrangement must be set out in writing and signed by both parties and must not be conditioned on the physician's referral of patients to the hospital. Under (e)(1)(iii) the remuneration may not be determined in any manner that takes into account the volume or value of actual or anticipated referrals.

The clause most worth looking for is (e)(1)(iv): the physician is allowed to establish staff privileges at any other hospital(s) and to refer business to any other entities. That is a condition of the exception, not a courtesy. An agreement that restricts where you may hold privileges or send patients is not merely unattractive — it is outside the exception the hospital is relying on.

Where a hospital recruits through an existing practice, (e)(4) requires that the remuneration be passed directly through to or remain with the recruited physician, with an exception only for actual costs the practice incurs in recruiting. If a group is retaining part of a hospital recruitment payment, that is a question worth asking before signing.

Why the offers point at shortage areas

Two separate mechanics push recruitment money toward particular places.

The first is geographic. Under 411.357(e)(2)(iv) the relocation requirement is met if the physician moves the practice at least 25 miles and into the hospital's geographic service area, or moves into that area and derives at least 75 percent of revenues from professional services to patients not seen or treated at the prior practice site during the preceding three years. The service area itself is defined at (e)(2)(i) as the lowest number of contiguous ZIP codes from which the hospital draws at least 75 percent of its inpatients.

The second is the anti-kickback safe harbor at 42 CFR 1001.952(n), which is written around relocation into a HPSA — a health professional shortage area — for the practitioner's specialty. Its conditions include a written agreement signed by the parties at (n)(1), benefits provided for a period not in excess of 3 years at (n)(3), at least 75 percent of the new practice's revenues generated from patients residing in a HPSA or a medically underserved area at (n)(8), and an agreement at (n)(7) to treat patients receiving benefits under any federal health care programme in a nondiscriminatory manner.

Read together, the 25-mile rule and the HPSA condition explain a pattern that otherwise looks arbitrary: the largest recruitment packages appear where the federal government has designated a shortage, and they usually require you to move.

Agency, travel and locum recruiting

The staffing branch operates on a bill rate: the client pays the firm an hourly rate, the firm pays the worker less than that, and the difference covers the firm's costs and margin. Nothing in that is hidden, but it is rarely stated, and it explains why the same assignment can be quoted at different pay by different firms.

The practical consequences are contractual rather than regulatory. Assignment length, cancellation terms, guaranteed hours, housing and stipend arrangements, and who holds responsibility for licensure and credentialing all sit in the written agreement rather than in the conversation. Verbal figures in this branch of the market are quotes, not terms.

What to check before you engage

Ask which of the three you are dealing with, in those words. Ask who pays their fee and on what trigger. Ask whether the role is with the organisation directly or through a staffing contract, because that determines who your employer would actually be.

If you are a physician receiving a recruitment package, check the three Stark clauses above by citation: the writing and signature, the absence of any referral condition, and the freedom to hold privileges and refer elsewhere at (e)(1)(iv). If the package runs through a practice rather than directly, check the pass-through at (e)(4). None of this is legal advice and none of it substitutes for counsel — our guide to which kind of health care lawyer you actually need covers who handles this — but knowing which clauses to look for changes what you can ask.

What a recruiter cannot do for you

A recruiter cannot change a licensure requirement, shorten a credentialing timeline that belongs to a medical staff office, or make a state board recognise a qualification it does not recognise. They cannot alter the employer's pay bands, and in the staffing branch they generally cannot alter the bill rate the client has agreed.

What they can do is real: tell you which employers are hiring now, what a role actually involves versus what the posting says, and where in a process an application has stalled. That is worth having. It is simply not the same as representation, and the difference is visible in the answer to the only question that matters here — who is paying them.

Questions people actually ask

Should a health care recruiter ever charge me a fee?

In the standard model, no. Both in-house recruiters and agency recruiters are paid by the employer, either as salaried staff or through a placement fee or bill-rate margin charged to the hiring organisation. A party asking a candidate for money is selling something other than recruitment, and the request itself is the signal worth acting on.

What does BLS pay data say about recruiters?

BLS does not publish a separate health care recruiter occupation. Recruiters are counted within human resources specialists, which had a median annual wage of $72,910 in May 2024, 944,300 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 6 percent over 2024–34 adding 58,400 positions, and about 81,800 openings a year. Employment services firms employ 14 percent of all human resources specialists, which is where agency recruiters sit.

Is physician recruitment regulated differently?

Yes, and it is the only branch of health care recruiting with its own federal rules. A hospital paying a physician to join its area creates a financial relationship, which the physician self-referral law reaches. To be lawful, the arrangement must fit the recruitment exception at 42 CFR 411.357(e), and organisations usually try to fit the anti-kickback safe harbor at 42 CFR 1001.952(n) as well.

What must a physician recruitment agreement contain?

Under 42 CFR 411.357(e)(1) the arrangement must be set out in writing and signed by both parties and must not be conditioned on the physician's referral of patients to the hospital. The remuneration cannot be determined in any manner that takes into account the volume or value of actual or anticipated referrals. Paragraph (e)(1)(iv) requires that the physician be allowed to establish staff privileges at any other hospitals and to refer business to any other entities.

Why do recruitment packages require relocating?

Because the exception is written around relocation. Under 42 CFR 411.357(e)(2)(iv) the requirement is met if the physician moves the practice at least 25 miles and into the hospital's geographic service area, or moves into that area and derives at least 75 percent of revenues from professional services to patients not seen or treated at the prior practice site during the preceding three years. The hospital's service area is defined at (e)(2)(i) as the lowest number of contiguous ZIP codes from which it draws at least 75 percent of its inpatients.

What is the difference between the Stark law and the anti-kickback statute here?

OIG states that the anti-kickback statute is a criminal law requiring knowing and willful conduct, so intent is an essential element, while the Stark law is a strict liability statute, meaning proof of specific intent to violate the law is not required. In recruitment that difference matters: a technically defective Stark arrangement can be a problem even where nobody intended anything improper.

Do agency recruiters work for me or for the hospital?

For the organisation paying them. An agency recruiter's fee is earned on a placement, so their interest is aligned with a placement happening rather than with any particular placement being right for you. That is not a criticism; it is a structure. The practical response is to treat the recruiter as a well-informed counterparty rather than an adviser, and to verify pay, schedule and duration against the written offer.
