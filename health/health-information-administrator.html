Health Information Administrator: The Duties Attached to the Chair

United States, federal

The title names a credential and a programme code, not a federal occupation. What actually defines the job is a set of duties that federal text attaches to somebody in the organisation, whatever that person is called.

There are four of them, and they are precise: define the designated record set, meet the access and accounting clocks, hold the named privacy roles and the training and policy obligations, and — in a hospital — run a medical record service that meets a condition of participation. Two retention periods sit underneath, and they do not match.

What the title actually names

The phrase points at three different things. It is a credential held by people who passed a professional examination. It is a programme classification — the federal code 51.0706, whose definition and its junior counterpart are set out in our guide to the code underneath the name. And it is a job title employers use with a good deal of local variation.

None of those tells you what the work is. The reliable description comes from the other direction: certain duties must be discharged by someone, and in most organisations they land on this chair. Read them and the job becomes concrete, portable between employers, and checkable against any job advert.

Duty one: deciding what the record set is

Almost everything else depends on this. 45 CFR 164.501 defines the designated record set as a group of records maintained by or for a covered entity that is (i) The medical records and billing records about individuals maintained by or for a covered health care provider; (ii) The enrollment, payment, claims adjudication, and case or medical management record systems maintained by or for a health plan; or (iii) Used, in whole or in part, by or for the covered entity to make decisions about individuals.

The same definition adds that record means any item, collection, or grouping of information that includes protected health information and is maintained, collected, used, or disseminated by or for a covered entity. Not a file. Not a system. Any grouping.

Limb (iii) is where the judgement lives. It turns on use — whether something informs decisions about individuals — not on which department stores it or what software holds it. Care management notes, scanned correspondence and a spreadsheet a team relies on can all qualify. Drawing that boundary is the first real act of the role, and every deadline below is measured against it.

Duty two: the access clock

Under 45 CFR 164.524(b)(2)(i) a covered entity must act on a request for access no later than 30 days after receipt of the request. Paragraph (b)(2)(ii) allows a single extension of up to 30 additional days where the individual is notified in writing of the reasons for the delay and the date by which the request will be completed. One extension, not a rolling one.

Format is a duty too, not a courtesy. Under (c)(2)(i) the entity must provide the individual with access to the protected health information in the form and format requested by the individual, if it is readily producible in such form and format, and otherwise in readable hard copy or another agreed form.

That clause is what turns a records office into an operational function: someone has to know what the systems can readily produce, which is an infrastructure question answered long before any request arrives. The patient side of the same right is covered in your record and your right of access.

Duty three: the six-year ledger

45 CFR 164.528 gives an individual the right to receive an accounting of disclosures of protected health information made by a covered entity in the six years prior to the date on which the accounting is requested. Each entry must carry the date, the recipient, a description of what was disclosed and the purpose.

The clock is different from the access clock: the entity must act no later than 60 days after receipt of such a request, with one 30-day extension available on written notice. The first accounting in any twelve-month period is free; a reasonable cost-based fee is permitted for later ones in the same period if notice is given in advance.

The operational weight is not in the response window. It is in the six-year look-back, which can only be answered if disclosures were logged as they happened. This is a duty that is either being discharged continuously or cannot be discharged at all — and it is the reason the role sits close to who moves your record.

Duty four: two named officials, training and policies

45 CFR 164.530(a)(1) requires two designations. A covered entity must designate a privacy official who is responsible for the development and implementation of its policies and procedures, and must designate a contact person or office who is responsible for receiving complaints and providing information. They may be the same individual, but both designations must exist and be documented.

Two continuing obligations sit alongside. Under (b)(1) a covered entity must train all members of its workforce on the policies and procedures with respect to protected health information, and under (i)(1) it must implement policies and procedures with respect to protected health information that are designed to comply with the applicable standards.

Note the shape of this. The rule does not prescribe a curriculum or a policy library; it names a person and requires that the thing exist and work. That is why the role is genuinely managerial rather than clerical: the content is delegated to the officeholder’s judgement, and so is the failure.

The two retention periods that do not match

This is the most common error in the field, and it is worth stating flatly. The Privacy Rule sets no retention period for medical records.

What it sets is a period for the entity’s own compliance documentation. Under 45 CFR 164.530(j)(2) a covered entity must retain the documentation required by paragraph (j)(1) of this section for six years from the date of its creation — the policies, the notices, the designations, the records of training and complaints.

Medical record retention comes from somewhere else entirely. For hospitals, 42 CFR 482.24(b)(1) requires that medical records must be retained in their original or legally reproduced form for a period of at least 5 years. Six years for compliance paperwork, at least five for the clinical record, and state law frequently requires longer than either. Three separate periods attaching to three different objects, and a policy that quotes one number for everything is wrong in at least two directions.

What the hospital rule adds

42 CFR 482.24 is a condition of participation, so it is enforced through the hospital’s Medicare agreement rather than through privacy enforcement. Its standard for the function is deliberately elastic: the organization of the medical record service must be appropriate to the scope and complexity of the services performed.

The substance is specific enough. Records must be accurately written, promptly completed, properly filed and retained, and accessible. The hospital must have a system of coding and indexing medical records that must allow for timely retrieval by diagnosis and procedure, in order to support medical care evaluation studies. Entries must be legible, complete, dated, timed, and authenticated. And the record must contain information to justify admission and continued hospitalization, support the diagnosis, and describe the patient’s progress, with the final diagnosis with completion of medical records within 30 days following discharge.

Read together, that is a job description written by a regulator: a coding and indexing system, a completion deadline enforced against clinicians the postholder does not manage, and an accessibility standard. The wider set of facility obligations is mapped in the federal list, and what binds each.

Reading a job advert against this

Adverts for the role tend to list systems and software. The duties above are a better interview script, because they are the same everywhere and the answers are diagnostic.

Ask who currently holds the privacy official designation and whether it is the same person as the complaints contact. Ask how the designated record set is documented and when it was last reviewed, which is the fastest way to find out whether limb (iii) has ever been thought about. Ask what the current turnaround is on access requests against the 30-day standard, and whether extensions are routine.

Then ask how disclosures are logged, because that is the duty that cannot be retrofitted. And in a hospital, ask what proportion of records are completed within 30 days of discharge. Five questions, and you will know more about the post than the advert contains.

What the chair does not carry

It does not carry clinical responsibility, and it does not carry authority over the clinicians whose documentation the completion standard measures — a structural tension the regulation creates and does not resolve. It does not make the postholder a lawyer; where the question is a subpoena, a contract or a state variation, the sorting is in ten statutes and who they bind.

Nor does the credential itself confer the duties. They attach to the covered entity, which assigns them. The credential is evidence of preparation, and the routes to it, including the exclusion that traps people whose programme has a similar name, are covered in the name binds.

What the chair does carry is a boundary decision, four clocks, two designations, two retention periods and a standard of accessibility. It is one of the few administrative posts in health care whose job description was written by a regulator rather than an employer, which is exactly what makes it portable. The wider field around it is mapped in roles, pay and credentials.

Questions people actually ask

What does a health information administrator do?

The work is best described by the obligations that attach to the post rather than by a task list. Someone must define the designated record set, meet the access and accounting deadlines, hold the named privacy roles, train the workforce, keep policies and documentation, and in a hospital run a medical record service appropriate to the scope and complexity of the services performed. Those duties are written in 45 CFR Part 164 and 42 CFR 482.24.

What is the designated record set?

45 CFR 164.501 defines it as a group of records maintained by or for a covered entity that is the medical records and billing records about individuals maintained by or for a covered health care provider; the enrollment, payment, claims adjudication, and case or medical management record systems maintained by or for a health plan; or used, in whole or in part, by or for the covered entity to make decisions about individuals. The third limb is the broad one, because it turns on use rather than on where a record is filed.

How long is there to answer a request for access?

Under 45 CFR 164.524(b)(2)(i) a covered entity must act on a request for access no later than 30 days after receipt of the request. One extension of up to 30 additional days is permitted under (b)(2)(ii) if the individual is notified in writing of the reasons for delay and the date of completion. The outer limit is therefore 60 days, and only one extension is allowed per request.

How long is there to produce an accounting of disclosures?

Under 45 CFR 164.528 an individual has a right to an accounting of disclosures made in the six years prior to the request. The covered entity must act no later than 60 days after receipt, with one 30-day extension available on written notice, giving an outer limit of 90 days. The first accounting in any twelve-month period must be provided at no cost.

Does HIPAA say how long to keep medical records?

No, and this is the most common confusion in the role. The six-year period at 45 CFR 164.530(j)(2) applies to the covered entity's own compliance documentation, retained for six years from the date of its creation. Medical record retention comes from elsewhere: for hospitals, 42 CFR 482.24(b)(1) requires records to be retained in their original or legally reproduced form for a period of at least 5 years, and state law commonly requires longer.

Which officials must be designated?

Two, under 45 CFR 164.530(a)(1). A covered entity must designate a privacy official responsible for the development and implementation of its policies and procedures, and must designate a contact person or office responsible for receiving complaints and providing information. They may be the same person, but both designations must exist.

What does the hospital condition of participation add?

42 CFR 482.24 requires that the organization of the medical record service be appropriate to the scope and complexity of the services performed, that records be accurately written, promptly completed, properly filed and retained, and accessible, and that the hospital have a system of coding and indexing allowing timely retrieval by diagnosis and procedure. Entries must be legible, complete, dated, timed, and authenticated, and the final diagnosis with completion of medical records is required within 30 days following discharge.
