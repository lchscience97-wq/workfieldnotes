Health Care Gov Customer Service: What Comes After the Call

A call centre cannot overturn an eligibility decision. It can explain one, correct a data entry error, and record information — but the power to reverse a determination sits in a formal appeal, and that appeal has a clock.

The clock is 90 days from the date of the notice. Coverage generally continues at the previously determined level while the appeal runs, the decision can be backdated at your option, and an expedited route exists for medical urgency. This page sets out the machinery the phone line sits in front of. For which number to ring in the first place, see our guide to the official lines by task.

What the call actually is

Marketplace customer service is a front door onto an administrative process with published rules. That framing matters because it changes what a call is for.

A call is the right instrument for understanding a notice, fixing something clerical, confirming what the record says, and being told which process applies. It is the wrong instrument for changing a decision someone has already made about your eligibility, because that authority is not held by the person answering.

Recognising which of those you need, early, is the difference between an hour on hold and a filing that starts a clock in your favour.

The two things no call can fix

The first is an eligibility determination you disagree with. That is appealable, and the appeal is a written process with its own timeline, not an escalation within the call.

The second is a decision by your insurer about whether to pay for a service. The Exchange did not make that decision and cannot review it. It belongs to a different regime with different deadlines, covered below and in our guide to which regulator is yours.

The eligibility appeal, and its 90-day clock

45 CFR 155.505 sets out what may be appealed. An applicant or enrollee may appeal an initial eligibility determination — including the amount of advance payments of the premium tax credit and the level of cost-sharing reductions — a redetermination of eligibility, an exemption eligibility determination, a failure by the Exchange to provide timely notice of an eligibility determination, a denial of a request to vacate a dismissal, and a State Exchange appeals entity decision.

That third item is worth pausing on. A failure to send a notice is itself appealable, so being left without an answer is not a dead end.

The deadline is at 155.520: an appeal request must be submitted within 90 days of the date of the notice of eligibility determination, or within a timeframe consistent with state Medicaid requirements that is no less than 30 days. An appeal of a State Exchange appeals entity decision goes to HHS within 30 days of that entity's notice.

State Exchange appeals entities may offer an informal resolution process first. Where they do, the appellant's right to a hearing is preserved in any case where they remain dissatisfied with the informal outcome, so accepting informal resolution does not forfeit the hearing.

Coverage while you wait

45 CFR 155.525 is the provision most worth knowing before you file. On receipt of a valid appeal request, the Exchange or the Medicaid or CHIP agency must continue to consider the appellant eligible at the previously determined level while the appeal is pending.

The practical consequence is that filing quickly does two jobs at once: it preserves the argument and it preserves the status quo. An appeal filed on day 89 has the same merits as one filed on day 3 and a much worse practical position, because the intervening months have already been lived at the new determination.

When you cannot wait

45 CFR 155.540 requires appeals entities to establish an expedited process where there is an immediate need for health services because a standard appeal could jeopardize the appellant's life, health, or ability to attain, maintain, or regain maximum function.

Read that test carefully, because it is a medical test rather than an administrative one. Urgency in the sense of a deadline approaching does not meet it; a clinical consequence of delay does. If it applies, saying so explicitly and in those terms at the point of filing is what triggers the route.

The third limb of the test is the one most often left out of a request. It is not only life and health: it also covers the ability to attain, maintain, or regain maximum function. That reaches rehabilitation after an injury, therapy that preserves an existing level of function, and treatment whose delay costs ground that cannot be recovered later. A request that describes function rather than only diagnosis is addressing the standard as written.

What a hearing actually is

Under 45 CFR 155.535 the hearing is an evidentiary hearing conducted by impartial officials who have not been directly involved in the prior determination. The appellant may review the documents in the file, present evidence, bring witnesses, and confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses.

Those are the features of an administrative tribunal, not a customer service escalation. They also imply a preparation task: the documents in the file are reviewable, which means asking for them is part of preparing rather than a separate favour.

Prospective or backdated

45 CFR 155.545 governs what a win is worth. An appeal decision takes effect either prospectively, on the first day of the month following the decision, or retroactively to the coverage effective date the appellant did receive or would have received had the correct determination been made — at the appellant's option.

Retroactive is not automatically better. Backdating coverage can mean backdated premiums, so the choice is a calculation rather than a formality. The Exchange must also redetermine the eligibility of household members affected by the decision, which means a successful appeal can move more than one person's position.

The two appeals people confuse

An eligibility appeal asks whether you qualify, and for how much financial help. It runs through the Exchange on the 90-day clock above.

A claim appeal asks whether your plan must pay for something. It runs through the plan first, with at least 180 days following receipt of an adverse benefit determination to appeal under 29 CFR 2560.503-1, and then to external review within four months under 45 CFR 147.136, where a reversal obliges the plan to provide coverage or payment immediately.

Different decision-maker, different clock, different remedy. Calling the Marketplace about a claim denial loses time that the plan's clock is already spending.

If someone else touched your application

One scenario deserves its own note, because the paperwork rights are unusually strong and almost unknown.

Where an agent or broker assisted with enrollment, 45 CFR 155.220(j)(2)(iii) requires documented consent obtained prior to assisting with or facilitating enrollment, specifying its scope, purpose and duration. And (j)(2)(ii) requires documentation that the eligibility application information was reviewed by and confirmed to be accurate by the consumer, maintained for ten years.

If an application was submitted or altered without your confirmation, those records should exist and can be asked for. Our guide to who may recommend a plan sets out the wider rules on assisters.

What to have in front of you

The notice itself, with its date, because every deadline on this page runs from a dated document. The application identifier. A short written statement of what you think is wrong and what the correct answer is. Any documents supporting income, household size or immigration status if those are what is disputed. And a note of whether medical urgency applies, in the terms 155.540 uses.

Then call, and ask one question first: is this something the call can resolve, or does it need an appeal? The answer determines everything that follows, and it is the one question the person on the line is genuinely well placed to answer.

Questions people actually ask

How long do I have to appeal an eligibility decision?

Ninety days. Under 45 CFR 155.520 an appeal request must be submitted within 90 days of the date of the notice of eligibility determination, or within a timeframe consistent with state Medicaid requirements that is no less than 30 days. If you are appealing a State Exchange appeals entity decision to HHS, that must be submitted within 30 days of the entity's notice.

What can actually be appealed?

Under 45 CFR 155.505 an applicant or enrollee may appeal an initial eligibility determination, including the amount of advance premium tax credit and the level of cost-sharing reductions, a redetermination of eligibility, an exemption eligibility determination, a failure by the Exchange to provide timely notice of an eligibility determination, a denial of a request to vacate a dismissal, and a State Exchange appeals entity decision.

Do I lose coverage while the appeal runs?

Not automatically. Under 45 CFR 155.525, on receipt of a valid appeal request, the Exchange or the Medicaid or CHIP agency must continue to consider the appellant eligible at the previously determined level while the appeal is pending. That provision is why filing promptly matters as much as the argument you file.

Can an appeal be expedited?

Yes. 45 CFR 155.540 requires appeals entities to establish an expedited process where there is an immediate need for health services because a standard appeal could jeopardise the appellant's life, health, or ability to attain, maintain, or regain maximum function. That test is about medical urgency rather than administrative inconvenience, and it should be stated explicitly in the request.

Is the appeal decision retroactive?

It can be, at your option. Under 45 CFR 155.545 an appeal decision takes effect either prospectively, on the first day of the month following the decision, or retroactively to the coverage effective date the appellant did receive or would have received had the correct decision been made. The Exchange must also redetermine the eligibility of household members affected by the decision.

What kind of hearing is it?

An evidentiary one. Under 45 CFR 155.535 the hearing is conducted by impartial officials who have not been directly involved in the prior determination, and the appellant may review the documents in the file, present evidence, bring witnesses, and confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses. It is closer to a small administrative tribunal than to a phone conversation.

Is a denied claim the same as an eligibility appeal?

No, and mixing them costs people their deadlines. An eligibility appeal concerns whether you qualify and for how much help, and runs through the Exchange on a 90-day clock. A denied claim concerns whether your plan will pay for a service, and runs through the plan's internal appeal with at least 180 days to file under 29 CFR 2560.503-1, then external review within four months under 45 CFR 147.136.
