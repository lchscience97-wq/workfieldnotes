Two people can hold plans with identical benefits and identical networks and still have different rights. The results for this phrase are written by insurers comparing products. Product comparison is not the question that decides what happens when a claim is refused.

That question is who regulates the plan. It turns on one structural fact — whether the plan is insured or self-funded — and it determines whether state insurance law reaches you, where you appeal, and on what clock. This page sets out the test, the deadlines, and how to work out which one you have.

One phrase, several legal animals

Under this single phrase sit employer group coverage, individual market coverage bought on an exchange, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, and a set of short-duration products that are defined precisely so as not to be any of those things.

They are not variations on a theme. They answer to different bodies, carry different mandatory protections, and offer different routes when a claim is denied. A comparison chart of deductibles and networks tells you nothing about any of that, which is why the standard results for this term are unhelpful for the moment you actually need help.

Our guides to six routes and their clocks and what the law requires covered deal with choosing and with benefits. This page deals with authority: who is in charge of your plan.

Insured or self-funded

An insured plan means the employer buys a policy and an insurance company bears the risk of claims. A self-funded plan means the employer pays claims from its own funds and usually hires a third party administrator to process them; the administrator's name on your card can look identical to an insurer's, because it often is the same company acting in a different capacity.

To the member the two are frequently indistinguishable. Legally they are not close. The insured plan sits inside state insurance regulation, subject to state mandated benefits, state market conduct rules and the state insurance department. The self-funded plan largely does not.

Three clauses, one result

The mechanism is three subsections of 29 U.S.C. §1144, and they are worth reading in order because each modifies the last.

Subsection (a) is the preemption clause: the provisions of the subchapter shall supersede any and all State laws insofar as they may now or hereafter relate to any employee benefit plan described in section 1003(a) and not exempt under 1003(b).

Subsection (b)(2)(A) is the savings clause, which pulls some of that back: nothing in this subchapter shall be construed to exempt or relieve any person from any law of any State which regulates insurance, banking, or securities. On its own, this would let state insurance law reach employer plans.

Subsection (b)(2)(B) is the deemer clause, which closes the loop: an employee benefit plan, or a trust established under one, shall not be deemed to be an insurance company or other insurer … or to be engaged in the business of insurance or banking for purposes of any law of any State purporting to regulate insurance companies, insurance contracts, banks, trust companies, or investment companies.

Put together: state insurance law survives preemption, but a self-funded plan is not insurance for the purposes of that law, so the saved state law has nothing to attach to. An insured plan, by contrast, contains an actual insurance policy the state can regulate. The same benefit design, funded two ways, lands in two different regulatory worlds.

The internal clock

Federal claims procedure rules at 29 CFR 2560.503-1 apply to group health plans regardless of funding, and they are specific.

An urgent care claim must be decided as soon as possible, taking into account the medical exigencies, but not later than 72 hours after receipt under §2560.503-1(f)(2)(i). A preservice claim must be decided within a reasonable period of time appropriate to the medical circumstances, but not later than 15 days after receipt. A postservice claim must be decided within 30 days after receipt.

On appeal, claimants must have at least 180 days following receipt of an adverse benefit determination to appeal it, under §2560.503-1(h)(1) and (h)(3)(i). Plans must then decide within 15 days for preservice claims and 30 days for postservice claims at each level of review.

Those numbers are the reason a denial letter is a dated document rather than an opinion. The 180 days runs from receipt, and it is a floor rather than a target.

External review, and who runs it

After internal appeals, a further step exists that is not a further internal opinion.

Under 45 CFR 147.136(d)(2)(i) a request for external review must be filed within four months after the date of receipt of a notice of an adverse benefit determination or final internal adverse benefit determination. Preliminary review occurs within five business days following the date of receipt of the external review request. The independent review organisation must decide within 45 days after the IRO receives the request, or for an expedited review in no event more than 72 hours.

The consequential clause is the last one: upon a final external review decision reversing the determination, the plan or issuer immediately must provide coverage or payment.

Who administers that process varies. CMS states that plans and issuers must comply with the state's external review process or the federal external review process. States that meet or exceed the protections in the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' Uniform External Review Model Act run their own; where HHS determines a state has neither implemented the required consumer protections nor a process meeting federal standards, issuers must either participate in the HHS-administered external review process or contract with accredited independent review organisations.

The category built to sit outside

Short-term limited-duration insurance is defined by duration precisely because duration is what keeps it outside the individual market rules.

Under the federal rule applying to coverage periods beginning on or after 1 September 2024, the maximum initial contract term is three months and the maximum total duration including any renewals or extensions is four months. Issuers must give a consumer notice in concise and easy-to-understand language, displayed prominently on the first page of policy documents and in marketing, application and enrolment materials.

CMS is direct about what is being traded away: individuals who enrol in it are not guaranteed these key consumer protections under federal law — among them prohibitions on discrimination based on health status, pre-existing condition exclusion protections, and limits on lifetime and annual dollar caps on essential health benefits.

How to tell which one you have

The insurance card will not tell you, because a self-funded plan's administrator is often a household insurance brand. The plan document will.

An insured plan is issued under a policy or certificate of insurance and identifies an insurance company as the entity assuming risk. A self-funded plan identifies the employer or plan sponsor as the payer and names a third party administrator whose function is to process claims. If the paperwork describes an administrator rather than an insurer, that is the single strongest indicator.

Two other tells are useful. Employers with self-funded plans must furnish a summary plan description on request, and denial letters on self-funded plans generally point to federal rather than state appeal rights. If a denial letter names your state insurance department, that is informative in itself.

When a claim is denied

Date the letter and count. Note whether the claim was urgent, preservice or postservice, because the applicable deadline follows from that. Confirm your appeal window — at least 180 days from receipt — and calendar the four-month external review deadline separately, because it runs from a different notice.

Ask the plan in writing for the specific provision relied on and for the documents considered. Then decide the route: state insurance department for an insured plan, the U.S. Department of Labor for a self-funded one, and external review in either case once internal appeals are exhausted. Our guide to who pays your claim covers the entities involved, and which kind of health care lawyer you actually need covers when this stops being a paperwork problem.

What this does not settle

Knowing your regulator does not tell you whether a denial was right. It tells you who has authority to review it and by when — which is the part people most often lose by default, through a missed deadline rather than a lost argument.

It also does not settle price. Regulatory category and cost are independent: a self-funded plan may be more generous than an insured one, and a state with strong mandates may still leave a given service uncovered. And it does not settle networks, which are contractual rather than regulatory. The narrow claim this page makes is the useful one: before you argue about a plan, find out who is in charge of it.

Questions people actually ask

Why does it matter whether my plan is self-funded?

Because it decides which body regulates it. ERISA at 29 U.S.C. §1144(a) supersedes state laws insofar as they relate to an employee benefit plan. The savings clause at 1144(b)(2)(A) preserves state laws that regulate insurance, but the deemer clause at 1144(b)(2)(B) says a plan may not be deemed to be an insurance company or engaged in the business of insurance for purposes of any state law purporting to regulate insurance. The combined effect is that a self-funded employer plan is generally outside state insurance regulation.

How long does a plan have to decide my claim?

Under 29 CFR 2560.503-1 a group health plan must decide an urgent care claim as soon as possible taking into account the medical exigencies but not later than 72 hours after receipt, a preservice claim within a reasonable period appropriate to the medical circumstances but not later than 15 days, and a postservice claim within 30 days. You then have at least 180 days following receipt of an adverse benefit determination to appeal it.

How long do I have to request an external review?

Four months. 45 CFR 147.136(d)(2)(i) sets the deadline at within four months after the date of receipt of a notice of an adverse benefit determination or final internal adverse benefit determination. Preliminary review happens within five business days, and the independent review organisation must issue its decision within 45 days of receiving the request, or within no more than 72 hours for an expedited review.

Is the external reviewer's decision binding?

In effect, yes. 45 CFR 147.136 provides that upon receipt of a notice of a final external review decision reversing the adverse benefit determination, the plan or issuer immediately must provide coverage or payment. That is the practical value of the external review step: it is not a further internal opinion but an outside decision the plan must act on.

Who runs the external review, the state or the federal government?

It depends on the state and the plan. CMS states that plans and issuers must comply with the state's external review process or the federal external review process. States that meet or exceed the protections in the National Association of Insurance Commissioners' Uniform External Review Model Act run their own. Where HHS determines a state has not implemented the required consumer protections or a process meeting federal standards, issuers must use the HHS-administered process or contract with accredited independent review organisations.

What is short-term limited-duration insurance?

A category deliberately defined to sit outside the individual market rules. Under the federal rule applying to coverage periods beginning on or after 1 September 2024, the maximum initial contract term is three months and the maximum total duration including any renewals or extensions is four months. CMS states that individuals who enrol in it are not guaranteed key federal consumer protections, including prohibitions on health status discrimination, pre-existing condition protections, and limits on lifetime and annual dollar caps.

How do I find out which type of plan I have?

Read the plan document rather than the ID card. An insured plan is issued under a policy or certificate of insurance and names an insurance company as the entity assuming risk. A self-funded plan names the employer or plan sponsor as the payer and typically names a third party administrator that only processes claims. If the paperwork identifies an administrator rather than an insurer, that is the strongest single indicator that the plan is self-funded.
