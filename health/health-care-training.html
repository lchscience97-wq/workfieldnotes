Health Care Training: Who Pays and Who Sets the Hours

Two different things share this phrase, and only one of them is yours to buy. One category is training your employer is legally obliged to give you, at its cost, on paid time. The other is training you purchase in order to become employable at all.

Search results answer the second and are mostly written by people selling it. This page separates the two, sets out the federal hour floors that apply to the purchasable kind — 75 hours for nurse aides and home health aides alike, structured differently — and names the two public lists worth checking before any money changes hands.

Two categories that share a phrase

The first page of results for this term is a mix of private training companies and state workforce agency sites. Both are selling or listing the same thing: courses an individual enrols in. That is one honest meaning of the phrase.

The other meaning never appears in those results, because nobody markets it. It is the training a health care employer must deliver to its own staff because a federal rule says so — free, on the clock, and repeated on a schedule the employer does not control. Confusing the two is expensive in a specific way: people pay for courses their employer was already required to provide.

The sorting question is simple. Does a federal rule name this training and attach it to an employer? If yes, it is not a purchase. If no, it is, and the questions become which hour floor applies and who recognises the result.

Training the employer must pay for

Three federal regimes account for most of the mandatory training in American health care workplaces, and they are structured very differently from one another.

OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard is the most prescriptive: it names the audience, the frequency, the cost allocation, the time of day and fourteen specific content elements. HIPAA's privacy rule is the least: it names an audience and a timing standard and leaves content entirely to the covered entity. Medicare Advantage and Part D compliance sits in between, fixing an annual frequency and an orientation requirement without prescribing hours.

Under 42 CFR 422.503(b)(4)(vi)(C)(1), a Medicare Advantage organisation must establish and implement effective training and education for its compliance officer and employees, its chief executive and other senior administrators, managers and governing body members. Subparagraph (C)(2) sets the cadence: such training must occur at a minimum annually and must be made part of the orientation for a new employee and for a new appointment to a chief executive, manager or governing body role. Note who is in scope — the board is, explicitly.

The bloodborne pathogens rule, in detail

29 CFR 1910.1030(g)(2) is worth reading closely because it is the clearest statement of employee entitlement in this area.

Paragraph (g)(2)(i) requires training for each employee with occupational exposure, provided at no cost to the employee and during working hours. Paragraph (g)(2)(ii)(A) sets the first deadline at the time of initial assignment to tasks where occupational exposure may take place, and (g)(2)(ii)(B) requires repetition at least annually thereafter. Paragraph (g)(2)(iv) tightens that: annual training must occur within one year of the previous training, which is a rolling anniversary rather than a calendar-year box.

Paragraph (g)(2)(vii) then lists the minimum content across fourteen lettered elements, running from an accessible copy and explanation of the regulatory text, through epidemiology and symptoms, modes of transmission, the exposure control plan, task recognition, controls and personal protective equipment, hepatitis B vaccine information, emergency and exposure-incident procedures, post-exposure evaluation, and signs and labels. The final element, (N), is the one most online modules quietly fail: an opportunity for interactive questions and answers with the person conducting the session.

Read together, those clauses mean a recorded video with no live component, taken unpaid at home, purchased by the employee, does not satisfy the standard on at least three separate counts.

HIPAA's training clause is shorter than the industry around it

45 CFR 164.530(b)(1) says a covered entity must train all members of its workforce on the policies and procedures with respect to protected health information required by the subpart, as necessary and appropriate for the members of the workforce to carry out their functions. That is the whole standard.

There is no federal hour requirement, no mandated curriculum, no approved vendor and no federal certificate. What is fixed is timing, at (b)(2)(i): each workforce member by the compliance date; each new member within a reasonable period of time after joining; and each member whose functions are affected by a material change to the policies within a reasonable period after the change takes effect. Documentation of that training must be retained for six years from creation or from when it was last in effect, whichever is later, under (j)(2).

The practical consequence for a jobseeker is that a purchased HIPAA certificate proves nothing about a new employer's obligations. Training is tied to a specific entity's own policies, so a new employer must train you on theirs regardless of what you bring with you. Our guide to who moves your record covers the underlying rules those policies implement.

Training you buy: the federal hour floors

Two occupations have hour minimums written directly into federal regulation, and both land on 75 — by different routes.

For nurse aides working in facilities participating in Medicare or Medicaid, 42 CFR 483.152(a)(1) requires a state-approved programme of no less than 75 clock hours. At least 16 of those must be supervised practical training under (a)(3), and at least 16 hours of training must be completed before any direct contact with a resident under (b)(1). The curriculum is specified too: communication and interpersonal skills, infection control, safety and emergency procedures including the Heimlich manoeuvre, promoting residents' independence, respecting residents' rights, basic nursing skills, personal care skills, mental health and social service needs, care of cognitively impaired residents, and basic restorative services.

For home health aides, 42 CFR 484.80(b)(1) requires classroom and supervised practical training to total at least 75 hours, and (b)(2) requires a minimum of 16 hours of classroom training to precede a minimum of 16 hours of supervised practical training. The sequencing is the difference: the nurse aide rule gates direct resident contact, the home health aide rule gates the practical block itself.

Home health aides then carry a continuing obligation nurse aides do not. Under 484.80(d) an aide must receive at least 12 hours of in-service training during each 12-month period — which, being an employer condition of participation rather than a personal credential requirement, belongs in the employer-pays column.

What the labour statistics do and do not record

The Bureau of Labor Statistics classifies both medical assistants and phlebotomists as typically entered with a postsecondary nondegree award — the census category for a certificate below associate level.

Medical assistants had a median annual wage of $44,200 in May 2024, equivalent to $21.25 an hour, with 811,000 jobs in 2024, projected growth of 12 percent over 2024–34 adding 101,200 positions, and about 112,300 openings a year. Phlebotomists had a median of $43,660, 139,700 jobs, 6 percent projected growth and about 18,400 openings a year. The wage gap between the two is $540, or 1.2 percent — small enough that a training decision between them should not be made on pay.

The openings figures are more informative than the wages. Medical assistant openings equal roughly 13.8 percent of current employment every year; phlebotomy openings equal about 13.2 percent. Both are high-churn occupations where most vacancies come from people leaving rather than from growth, which is why entry training in these fields is sold continuously and why availability of a job is rarely the binding constraint.

BLS is also careful about requirements in a way that marketing is not. For medical assistants it states that some states require that medical assistants graduate from an accredited program, be licensed or certified, or meet other prerequisites, and that while most states do not require certification, employers may prefer or require it. For phlebotomists it states that states may require that phlebotomists complete an accredited training program, have a license or certification, or meet other requirements. In both cases the operative authority is a state, not the school. Our guide to the two tiers of health care jobs sets out where these roles sit against the rest of the field.

The two lists worth checking before you pay

The first is your state's eligible training provider list. Under 20 CFR 680.410 an eligible training provider is the only type of entity that receives funding for training services through an individual training account under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act. Eligible providers fall into three categories: institutions of higher education offering programmes leading to a recognised postsecondary credential, apprenticeship programmes registered under the National Apprenticeship Act, and other public or private providers including community-based organisations, joint labour-management organisations and adult education providers offering services combined with training.

The list is not a quality ranking, and absence from it proves nothing. But it is a free, state-maintained check that a provider has cleared a performance and reporting bar, and sometimes the route to having the training paid for.

The second is the state registry or licensing board for the specific role. For nurse aides that is the state nurse aide registry, which is where completion of an approved programme is actually recorded and where an employer will look. A course that does not result in a registry entry has not achieved the thing it was bought for.

What none of it certifies

A certificate is issued by a school for finishing a course. A certification is issued by a credentialing body after an examination and is usually renewed. Training marketing routinely uses the words interchangeably, and the difference is the entire question of whether anyone outside the school recognises what you bought.

None of these hour floors is a ceiling either. Federal minimums are floors that states routinely raise, so a 75-hour nurse aide programme is the shortest thing a state may approve, not the normal length. And no amount of entry training substitutes for the employer-side training in the first half of this page: a new job in this field will come with its own HIPAA and bloodborne pathogens sessions, on the employer's clock, whatever certificates you arrive holding. If you are weighing a longer route instead, our guide to where the degree name is legally load-bearing covers the one corner of this field where the programme title genuinely matters.

Questions people actually ask

Should I ever pay for bloodborne pathogens training?

Not if you are an employee with occupational exposure. OSHA's standard at 29 CFR 1910.1030(g)(2)(i) requires the employer to provide the training at no cost to the employee and during working hours. It must be given at the time of initial assignment to tasks where exposure may take place and at least annually thereafter, within one year of the previous session. Paying for it yourself, or taking it unpaid on a weekend, means the employer has not met the standard.

How many hours of training does a nurse aide need?

Federal rules set a floor of no less than 75 clock hours under 42 CFR 483.152(a)(1). At least 16 of those must be supervised practical training, and at least 16 hours of training must be completed before any direct contact with a resident. States may and often do require more, so 75 hours is the minimum a state-approved programme can be, not the typical length.

Is home health aide training the same 75 hours?

The total is the same but the structure differs. Under 42 CFR 484.80(b)(1) classroom and supervised practical training must total at least 75 hours, and 484.80(b)(2) requires a minimum of 16 hours of classroom training to precede a minimum of 16 hours of supervised practical training. Home health aides also carry an ongoing obligation nurse aides do not: at least 12 hours of in-service training during each 12-month period.

What does HIPAA actually require an employer to train?

Less than most people assume. 45 CFR 164.530(b)(1) requires a covered entity to train all workforce members on its own policies and procedures for protected health information, as necessary and appropriate for them to carry out their functions. There is no federal hour count, no named curriculum and no approved vendor. What is fixed is timing and paperwork: new workforce members within a reasonable period after joining, everyone affected by a material change within a reasonable period after it takes effect, and documentation retained for six years.

Is a certificate the same as a certification?

No, and the difference is what most training marketing blurs. A certificate is issued by a school for completing a course of study. A certification is issued by a credentialing body after an examination, and is typically renewed. A programme can award a certificate that no employer or state board recognises. The test is not the paper but whether a named body outside the school will accept it.

How do I check whether a training provider is recognised?

Check your state's eligible training provider list. Under 20 CFR 680.410 an eligible training provider is the only type of entity that can be paid for training services through an individual training account under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act. States publish those lists. A provider that has not cleared that bar is not automatically bad, but its absence is a question worth asking before you pay.

Does certification raise pay in these roles?

BLS does not publish a certified-versus-uncertified wage split, so any figure claiming one is not coming from the federal data. What BLS does record is that medical assistants had a median annual wage of $44,200 and phlebotomists $43,660 in May 2024, that both are typically entered with a postsecondary nondegree award, and that states may require an accredited programme, licence or certification. Treat certification as an access condition rather than a proven raise.
