Health Care Administrator: What the Job Is, What It Pays, What You Need

Last verified
3 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States
Wage data

A health care administrator plans, directs and coordinates the business side of a healthcare provider — budgets and billing, staffing and schedules, compliance with state and federal rules, and reporting upward to a board or owner. The job may cover an entire facility, one clinical department, or a group medical practice.

The single most useful thing to know before applying: only one version of this job legally requires a licence. Everything else is credentialled by choice, not by law — and the difference changes how you plan the route in.

What the job actually involves

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics calls this occupation medical and health services managers and notes that it is also called healthcare executive or healthcare administrator. All three names point at the same statistical category, which is why searching one term and finding data under another is normal rather than a sign you have the wrong page.

BLS lists the typical duties directly: setting goals for efficiency and quality of care; ensuring the facility complies with laws and regulations; preparing and monitoring budgets and managing finances including patient fees and billing; recruiting, training and supervising staff; creating work schedules; representing the facility at board or investor meetings; and keeping records of facility services such as inpatient bed use.

What that list conceals is the proportion. Most of the week is not strategy. It is the recurring administrative machinery of a place that never closes — the schedule, the budget variance, the vacancy, the regulator's request, the complaint. The strategic work exists but sits on top of that base.

BLS names three common specialisations. Nursing home administrators manage an entire facility including admissions, building maintenance and resident care. Clinical managers oversee a single department such as intensive care or physical therapy. Health information managers keep patient record databases complete, accurate and restricted to authorised staff — the management tier of the field covered in our guide to health information professionals.

The licence rule — the one thing worth checking first

This is where the field splits, and most career pages blur it.

BLS states the rule plainly: some medical and health services managers need a state-issued licence, and all states require licensure for nursing home administrators, with requirements varying by state. Some positions additionally require the holder to be licensed as a registered nurse or a social worker.

The practical consequence: a nursing home cannot lawfully operate without a licensed administrator, which makes that role's supply structurally constrained. A hospital can hire whoever it judges qualified. If you are choosing a track, that is a real difference in how the labour market behaves — not a detail.

What it pays, and where

The median annual wage for medical and health services managers was $117,960 in May 2024. The lowest ten per cent earned under $69,680; the highest ten per cent earned more than $219,080. Hourly, the median works out to $56.71.

Setting moves that number more than most people expect.

What you actually need to enter

BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry-level education, with less than five years of related work experience and no on-the-job training expected. Requirements vary: some employers hire candidates with an associate degree, others prefer a master's, and work experience can sometimes substitute for education.

Common majors are health administration, nursing, or business. Degrees that combine the two sides typically include medical terminology, hospital organisation and health information systems alongside accounting and budgeting.

The experience requirement is the part people underestimate. BLS notes that employers may require prior work in either an administrative or a clinical role in a hospital or other healthcare facility. Nursing home administrators often have years of experience as a registered nurse. Others start as medical records specialists, administrative assistants or financial clerks in a healthcare office and move up.

Advancement to top executive positions may require a master's degree. That is the honest reason MHA and MBA programmes exist in this field: not to enter, but to move past the department level.

The FACHE credential, and what it costs in effort

Certification is not required in this occupation. The most recognised voluntary credential is Fellow of the American College of Healthcare Executives (FACHE), and its requirements make clear that it is a mid-career marker rather than an entry ticket. Per ACHE, candidates must meet all of the following before sitting the exam:

  • Current ACHE membership with one year of tenure (student membership does not count)
  • A master's degree or other post-baccalaureate degree
  • Currently hold an executive healthcare management position and have a minimum of five years of healthcare management experience
  • 36 hours of healthcare-related continuing education within the last three years, of which at least 12 must be ACHE in-person education
  • Two examples of community or civic activity and two examples of healthcare-related activity within the last three years
  • Two references — the first a structured interview with a current Fellow, the second from another Fellow or a senior executive in your organisation

The final step is the Board of Governors Examination in Healthcare Management, a six-hour, 230-question multiple-choice exam. An approved application remains valid for two years, within which the exam must be passed.

Read the eligibility list before enrolling in anything sold as FACHE preparation. Without the master's degree, the executive-level post and five years of management experience, no amount of exam study makes you eligible. The exam is the last gate, not the first.

How to read a job posting in this field

Because the occupation has three interchangeable names and no standard titling, the posting itself is the only reliable guide to what the job is. Four things in the text tell you more than the title does.

The reporting line. A role reporting to a department head is a supervisory job with a manager's title. A role reporting to a chief operating officer or a board is an administrator's job in the sense BLS means. The salary band usually follows the reporting line rather than the title.

Budget authority. Look for whether the post owns a budget or contributes to one. Owning a budget is the single clearest marker separating the two halves of this occupation, and it is the thing most often absent from postings that use senior-sounding titles for coordinator-level work.

Named regulators. A posting that names specific accrediting or regulatory bodies is describing compliance-heavy work with a real audit calendar. One that mentions compliance generically usually is not.

Licence language. "Licensed nursing home administrator required" is a hard filter set by state law. "RN preferred" is a preference. "Certification preferred" almost always means FACHE or a specialty credential and is genuinely optional at entry level.

If a posting is vague on all four, the organisation may not have settled what the role is. That is worth asking about at interview rather than discovering in month three.

Where the jobs are

Medical and health services managers held about 616,200 jobs in 2024. The largest employers were hospitals at 29 per cent, offices of physicians at 14 per cent, nursing and residential care facilities at 9 per cent, government at 7 per cent, and outpatient care centres at 7 per cent.

That leaves roughly a third of the occupation outside those five categories — insurers, pharmaceutical companies, consultancies, home health services and health IT vendors among them. Those roles are frequently invisible to a search that looks only at hospital job boards, and they are a common route for people whose background is business rather than clinical.

The parts of the job that do not appear in the salary figure

Most administrators work full time and some work more than 40 hours a week. Evening or weekend work may be required in settings that operate around the clock, and being on call for emergencies is normal in hospital and nursing home roles.

One detail from BLS is genuinely surprising and rarely mentioned in career material: medical and health services managers have one of the highest rates of injuries and illnesses of all occupations. BLS attributes this to incidents such as slips, falls and overexertion, and notes that risk is reduced by following the same safety practices as clinical staff. The reason is simple once stated — the job is based inside a working clinical facility, not in a detached office.

Outlook

BLS projects employment to grow 23 per cent from 2024 to 2034, against 3 per cent for all occupations and 6 per cent for other management occupations. That is an increase of about 142,900 jobs, from 616,200 to a projected 759,100, with roughly 62,100 openings a year over the decade — many of them from replacing people who retire or move to other occupations.

BLS gives two drivers. An ageing population increases demand for healthcare services and therefore for managers to organise them. And healthcare delivery is becoming more complex through technology, shifting policy and evolving care models, which requires more managers to navigate it. The second driver matters for anyone weighing this field: the growth is not only volume, it is complexity — and complexity is what management roles are paid to absorb.

Questions people actually ask

What does a health care administrator do?

They plan, direct and coordinate the business side of a healthcare provider: budgets and billing, staffing and schedules, compliance with state and federal rules, and reporting to a board or owner. They may run an entire facility, one clinical department, or a group medical practice.

How much does a health care administrator make?

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a median annual wage of $117,960 for medical and health services managers in May 2024. The lowest ten per cent earned under $69,680 and the highest ten per cent earned more than $219,080.

Do you need a licence to be a health care administrator?

Only for one role. Every US state requires a licence for nursing home administrators, with requirements set by the state. For hospital, clinic and practice administration there is no licence requirement; credentials such as the FACHE are voluntary.

What degree do you need to be a health care administrator?

BLS lists a bachelor's degree as the typical entry-level education, though requirements vary. Some employers hire candidates with an associate degree and others prefer a master's. Work experience can sometimes substitute for education.

Is health care administration a growing field?

Yes. BLS projects employment of medical and health services managers to grow 23 per cent between 2024 and 2034, against 3 per cent for all occupations, adding about 142,900 jobs and averaging roughly 62,100 openings a year.

What is the difference between a health care administrator and a health care manager?

Nothing consistent. BLS treats healthcare administrator, healthcare executive and medical and health services manager as names for the same occupation. In job postings, administrator more often implies running a facility and manager more often implies running a department, but the usage is not standardised.

Which setting pays health care administrators the most?

Government, excluding state and local education and hospitals, at a median of $132,620 in May 2024, followed by hospitals at $130,690. Nursing and residential care facilities were lowest among the top industries at $99,250.
