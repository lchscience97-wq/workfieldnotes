Health Information Exchange: Who Moves Your Record

United States

Exchange now has a national structure, and it has a blind spot you can locate precisely. TEFCA lists six exchange purposes, of which the first three are treatment, payment and health care operations.

Those same three are the ones HIPAA excludes from an accounting of disclosures under 45 CFR 164.528. So the routes your record travels most often are the routes the accounting right does not itemise. What you can obtain instead is the record itself, on a 30-day clock, and a six-year accounting of everything outside those three.

The activity and the organisation

The results for this phrase mix federal policy pages, a regional exchange organisation and an encyclopaedia entry. That mix reflects a real ambiguity: the phrase names both a thing people do and a kind of company that does it.

ONC defines the activity: Electronic health information exchange (HIE) allows doctors, nurses, public health professionals, pharmacists, other healthcare providers, and patients to appropriately access and securely share a patient's vital medical information electronically—improving the speed, quality, safety, and cost of patient care.

The organisation is the entity that runs the plumbing for a region or a network. Those two senses are frequently conflated, and the conflation matters because your rights attach to the covered entity holding your record, not to the network moving it. Where the vocabulary itself comes apart — HIT against HIE against EHR — is set out in HIT, HIE, EHR sorted. This page is about the structure and what you can ask of it.

The national framework, and what a QHIN is

ONC describes TEFCA — the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement — as operating in the United States as a nationwide framework for health information sharing.

Its architecture is federated rather than central. QHINs are the individual networks that make up the backbone of TEFCA. Together, they create the network-of-networks that enables nationwide exchange of health information. The first Qualified Health Information Networks were designated in December 2023, which makes this a young structure by the standards of the systems it connects.

The design decision worth noticing is the purpose requirement. ONC states that TEFCA is designed to support exchange for any purpose, but that purpose must be indicated in each request for data. Requests are therefore labelled at the moment they are made, which is what makes the next section possible to reason about at all.

Six purposes, and where each one lands

This is the comparison no source runs, because it requires holding an ONC framework document and a HIPAA regulation open at the same time.

TEFCA exchange purposeTypical requesterItemised in an accounting of disclosures?
TreatmentA clinician caring for youNo — excluded
PaymentA plan or billerNo — excluded
Health care operationsThe organisation itselfNo — excluded
Public HealthA public health authorityGenerally yes
Government benefits determinationA benefits agencyGenerally yes
Individual access servicesYouNo — disclosures to the individual are excluded

Three of the six sit in the excluded group by rule. A fourth — individual access — is excluded for the obvious reason that you are the recipient. That leaves two of the six purposes as the ones an accounting is designed to surface.

This is not a defect being exposed. Treatment, payment and operations were excluded from the accounting right precisely because they are the ordinary, high-volume business of care, and itemising every one of them would produce a document nobody could use. But the practical consequence is worth stating plainly: if your question is "which clinicians pulled my record while treating me", the accounting of disclosures is not the instrument that answers it.

What the accounting right actually gives you

Under 45 CFR 164.528, an individual has a right to receive an accounting of disclosures of protected health information made by a covered entity in the six years prior to the date on which the accounting is requested.

The mechanics are specific and worth using correctly.

Timing. The covered entity must act on the individual's request for an accounting, no later than 60 days after receipt of such a request, with a possible 30-day extension where it cannot comply in the initial period.

Cost. The entity must provide the first accounting to an individual in any 12 month period without charge, and may impose a reasonable, cost-based fee for each subsequent request.

Scope. Six years back, minus the exclusions: treatment, payment and health care operations, disclosures to the individual, incidental disclosures, disclosures made under an authorisation, directory and care coordination purposes, national security and intelligence activities, correctional institution communications, limited data sets, and anything before the compliance date.

Used against the right question — a suspected disclosure to a third party outside ordinary care — this is a powerful and free instrument. Used against the wrong question, it returns an accurate document that does not contain what you were looking for.

The instrument that does answer most questions

For most people the more useful right is access to the record itself, at 45 CFR 164.524, and its clock is faster.

Thirty days. The covered entity must act on a request for access no later than 30 days after receipt of the request, with one extension of no more than 30 days on written notice inside the first period.

Your format, where possible. Access must be provided in the form and format requested by the individual, if it is readily producible in such form and format, and for an electronic request in the electronic form and format requested by the individual, if it is readily producible in such form and format.

A bounded fee. The entity may impose a reasonable, cost-based fee covering only labour for copying, supplies for paper or electronic media, postage where mailed, and the cost of preparing an agreed summary or explanation.

Two clocks, two instruments: 30 days for the record, 60 days for the accounting. Knowing which you want before you write saves the more common of the two failures, which is asking for the wrong one and waiting out the longer clock to find out. The practical mechanics of exercising access, including through a patient portal, are in your record and your right of access.

Why this is live now rather than theoretical

The infrastructure question has closed. As of 2024, more than 99 per cent of non-federal acute care hospitals and 91 per cent of office-based physicians had adopted a certified electronic health record.

With adoption effectively universal at the hospital end and an 8 percentage point gap remaining at the office end, the open questions are no longer about whether records exist electronically. They are about movement: under what purpose, through which network, and with what visibility to the person the record describes. That is exactly the territory TEFCA occupies, and it is why a framework designated as recently as December 2023 matters more than its age suggests.

What to do with this

Decide which question you have. "What is in my record" is 164.524, 30 days. "Who did this go to, outside ordinary care" is 164.528, 60 days, six years back.

Ask the covered entity, not the network. Both rights run against the covered entity holding the information. Which organisations are covered entities, and why the answer turns on electronic transmission rather than signage, is in a legal term, not a synonym.

Ask in the format you want. The electronic-format provision only helps if you invoke it in the request.

If something goes wrong, there is a free route with a deadline. A privacy complaint runs on a 180-day clock and needs no lawyer; the routing is in route before you search, and the statute this sits inside is one of ten mapped in ten statutes and who they bind. The people who administer these requests day to day are described in roles, pay and credentials.

Questions people actually ask

What is TEFCA and what is a QHIN?

TEFCA is the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement, described by ONC as a nationwide framework for health information sharing in the United States. QHINs are Qualified Health Information Networks, described as the individual networks that make up the backbone of TEFCA, together creating a network-of-networks that enables nationwide exchange. The first QHINs were designated in December 2023.

What purposes can my record be exchanged for?

ONC states that TEFCA is designed to support exchange for any purpose, but that the purpose must be indicated in each request for data. The initial exchange purposes are treatment, payment, health care operations, public health, government benefits determination and individual access services.

Can I get a list of everyone who received my record?

Only a partial one. Under 45 CFR 164.528 an individual has a right to an accounting of disclosures made in the six years prior to the request, but that accounting excludes disclosures for treatment, payment and health care operations, among others. Those three are also the first three TEFCA exchange purposes, so the busiest routes are the ones least visible in an accounting.

How long does an accounting of disclosures take and what does it cost?

The covered entity must act on the request no later than 60 days after receipt, with a possible 30-day extension if it cannot comply in time. It must provide the first accounting in any 12-month period without charge, and may impose a reasonable, cost-based fee for each subsequent request.

How do I get a copy of the record itself?

Under 45 CFR 164.524 the covered entity must act on a request for access no later than 30 days after receipt, with one extension of no more than 30 days on written notice. If you ask for an electronic copy it must be provided in the electronic form and format requested where readily producible, and any fee must be a reasonable, cost-based fee covering only copying labour, supplies, postage and agreed summaries.

What is the difference between an HIE the activity and an HIE the organisation?

The activity is the electronic sharing itself, which ONC describes as allowing clinicians and patients to appropriately access and securely share a patient's vital medical information electronically. The organisation is a legal entity that operates the plumbing for a region or a network, and many of the results for this phrase are the names of such entities rather than descriptions of the activity.

Is exchange actually happening at scale?

The underlying systems are effectively universal. As of 2024, more than 99 per cent of non-federal acute care hospitals and 91 per cent of office-based physicians had adopted a certified electronic health record, so the question has moved from whether records are electronic to how and under what purpose they move between organisations.
