Health Insurance 0 Deductible: The Dial Nobody Caps

The deductible is the one cost-sharing figure federal law does not cap. The paragraph of 45 CFR 156.130 that would carry an annual limitation on deductibles is reserved — it contains nothing. What federal law caps is the out-of-pocket maximum: $10,150 self-only and $20,300 other than self-only for 2026.

So a zero deductible moves the dial with no ceiling while leaving the one with a ceiling untouched. It also closes the health savings account door, because an HSA requires a minimum deductible of $1,700 self-only for 2026. This page sets out the four dials and what each is actually worth.

Four dials, one number

Cost sharing in an American health plan runs on four mechanisms that operate in sequence and interact. The deductible is what you pay before the plan begins paying its share. The copayment is a fixed amount per service. Coinsurance is a percentage of the allowed amount. The out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling on the total of all of them in a plan year.

A plan advertised on one of those four is telling you about one of four. The results for this search are written by carriers and benefits vendors, and a zero deductible is a legitimately attractive feature — but it is a feature, not a summary.

The only figure federal law caps

45 CFR 156.130(a) sets the annual limitation on cost sharing and provides that for plan years after 2014 it is increased by the premium adjustment percentage, which at 156.130(e) reflects the percentage by which average per capita premiums for the preceding calendar year exceed those for 2013.

For 2026, CMS set the premium adjustment percentage at approximately 1.6002 and the maximum annual limitation on cost sharing at $10,150 for self-only coverage and $20,300 for other than self-only coverage.

Paragraph (b) of the same section — the natural home for an annual limitation on deductibles — is reserved. There is nothing in it. That single fact reframes the whole search: the deductible is the number with no federal ceiling, which is precisely why it is the number carriers compete on and advertise.

The clause that undoes the cap

One further paragraph deserves attention before anyone treats the maximum as a guarantee. Under 45 CFR 156.130(c), cost sharing paid by or on behalf of an enrollee for benefits provided outside of such network is not required to count toward the annual limitation.

So the protection that makes a plan's worst case knowable is a protection about in-network spending. If the expensive event involves an out-of-network provider, the cap you were reasoning from may not apply to it. Buying a zero deductible while ignoring the network is optimising the unprotected dial and neglecting the protected one. Our guide to two meanings of network in one search box covers the other half of that problem.

What is already zero

A common reason for wanting a zero deductible is the expectation that routine care will otherwise be charged. For a defined set of services, that expectation is already satisfied at any deductible level.

45 CFR 147.130(a)(1) requires coverage without cost sharing for four categories: evidence-based items or services rated A or B by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force; immunizations for routine use in children, adolescents and adults recommended by the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices; evidence-informed preventive care and screenings in the HRSA-supported guidelines for infants, children and adolescents; and additional preventive care and screenings in HRSA guidelines for women.

The limit is network-shaped again: under 147.130(a)(3)(i) a plan with a network need not cover those services without cost sharing when delivered out of network, though (a)(3)(ii) requires coverage where no in-network provider is available.

Zero closes the HSA door

This is the trade-off least often stated at the point of sale, and it is not small.

Health savings account contributions require coverage under a high deductible health plan. For 2026 the IRS sets the HDHP minimum annual deductible at $1,700 for self-only and $3,400 for family coverage, with maximum annual out-of-pocket expenses of $8,500 and $17,000. The 2026 contribution limits are $4,400 self-only and $8,750 family.

A zero deductible plan cannot satisfy that definition, so the contribution capacity is forfeited for the year. Whether that matters depends on your tax position and whether you would have funded the account. But it is a real cost of the feature, and it is invisible in a premium comparison.

There is a second observation buried in those numbers. The HDHP out-of-pocket ceiling for self-only coverage is $8,500, while the general annual limitation on cost sharing is $10,150 — a difference of $1,650. An HSA-qualified plan is therefore required to have a lower worst case than the law otherwise permits, which cuts against the intuition that a high deductible plan is automatically the riskier choice.

Metal tiers measure the whole

Under 45 CFR 156.140 a bronze plan has an actuarial value of 60 percent, silver 70, gold 80 and platinum 90. The permitted de minimis variation is generally minus 4 to plus 2 percentage points, widening to minus 4 to plus 5 for bronze plans that cover major services before the deductible or qualify as high deductible health plans.

Actuarial value is the share of total costs the plan pays across a standard population. It says nothing about how that share is distributed among the four dials. Two gold plans can hold the same 80 percent and arrange it as a low deductible with high coinsurance, or a higher deductible with flat copays afterwards. The metal level tells you roughly how much protection you bought; the schedule tells you where it sits.

The silver variation most people miss

For eligible enrollees in silver Marketplace plans, cost-sharing reductions move the capped dial rather than the uncapped one, and they move it a long way.

For 2026 CMS set the reduced maximum annual limitation on cost sharing at $3,350 self-only and $6,700 other than self-only for households up to 200 percent of the federal poverty level, and $8,100 and $16,200 for households between 200 and 250 percent.

Against the standard $10,150, the lower tier reduces maximum in-network exposure by $6,800 — about 67 percent. No zero deductible on a bronze plan comes close to that, and the reduction is only available on silver. Anyone shopping for a zero deductible because they are worried about a bad year should check silver eligibility first. Comparing two plans honestly

Three numbers make the comparison real: the annual premium, the out-of-pocket maximum, and the deductible in that order of importance. Premium is certain, the maximum bounds the bad year, and the deductible only describes the shape of the middle.

Then ask two structural questions. Are the providers and facilities you actually use in network, given that out-of-network spending need not count toward the cap? And would you have funded an HSA, given that a zero deductible forecloses it? Answering those two usually settles the choice faster than any comparison of deductibles.

What zero does not mean

It does not mean free care, because copays and coinsurance survive it. It does not mean a lower worst case, because the worst case is set by the out-of-pocket maximum. It does not mean better preventive coverage, because the four preventive categories are already at zero cost sharing on every compliant plan. And it does not mean lower total cost, because the premium carries the difference.

What it does mean is genuine and narrow: predictable pricing from the first visit, with no threshold to cross before the plan participates. For someone with steady, moderate, in-network use who would not have funded an HSA, that predictability can be worth paying for. It is simply a different thing from protection, and the protection is measured somewhere else.

Questions people actually ask

Does a zero deductible mean I pay nothing?

No. The deductible is one of several cost-sharing mechanisms. Copayments, coinsurance and the annual out-of-pocket maximum all continue to operate. A plan can have a zero deductible and still leave you exposed up to the annual limitation on cost sharing, which for 2026 is $10,150 for self-only coverage and $20,300 for other than self-only coverage.

Does federal law cap deductibles?

For these plans, no. The paragraph of 45 CFR 156.130 that would carry an annual limitation on deductibles, paragraph (b), is reserved and contains no substantive requirement. What federal law does cap is the annual limitation on cost sharing at 156.130(a), indexed each year by the premium adjustment percentage. The deductible is the dial with no federal ceiling; the out-of-pocket maximum is the one with a ceiling.

What is already free regardless of my deductible?

Preventive services in four defined categories. Under 45 CFR 147.130(a)(1) plans must cover, without cost sharing, items and services rated A or B by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, immunizations recommended by the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, and the HRSA-supported guidelines for infants, children and adolescents and for women. These are already at zero on plans with high deductibles, so buying a zero deductible does not change them.

Can I keep an HSA with a zero deductible plan?

No. Health savings account contributions require coverage under a high deductible health plan, and for 2026 the IRS sets the minimum annual deductible at $1,700 for self-only and $3,400 for family coverage. A zero deductible plan cannot meet that definition, so choosing one forfeits the 2026 contribution limits of $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage.

Do metal tiers tell me the deductible?

No, they measure the whole. Under 45 CFR 156.140 a bronze plan has an actuarial value of 60 percent, silver 70, gold 80 and platinum 90, with a permitted de minimis variation generally of minus 4 to plus 2 percentage points. Actuarial value describes the share of total costs the plan pays across a standard population, so two plans at the same metal level can distribute that share very differently between deductible, copays and coinsurance.

Is there a cheaper way to cut my exposure than a zero deductible?

For eligible enrollees in silver Marketplace plans, cost-sharing reductions do more. For 2026 the reduced maximum annual limitation on cost sharing is $3,350 self-only and $6,700 other than self-only for households up to 200 percent of the federal poverty level, and $8,100 and $16,200 for households between 200 and 250 percent. Against the standard $10,150 limit, the lower tier cuts maximum exposure by $6,800.

Does out-of-network spending count toward my maximum?

Not necessarily. 45 CFR 156.130(c) provides that cost sharing paid by or on behalf of an enrollee for benefits provided outside the network is not required to count toward the annual limitation. That is the clause that most often defeats the intuition behind buying a zero deductible: the cap you were relying on may not apply to the spending that actually happened.
