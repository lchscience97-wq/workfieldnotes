Health Care Law: Ten Statutes and Who They Bind

Last verified
8 Aug 2026
Jurisdiction
United States, federal
Data sources
HHS OIG; CMS; 45 CFR; ONC
Sources
7 cited
Reading time
10 min

"Health care law" is a label, not a statute. It covers at least ten separate federal laws, and they point at different people. Five are fraud and abuse rules aimed at institutions and physicians. Three govern what hospitals and plans owe patients. Two govern information.

Sorting them by who is bound rather than by subject is what makes the field usable. Do that and a practical fact appears: of the ten, only two give an individual a direct route to act — a written privacy complaint on a 180-day clock, and the surprise-billing protections that apply automatically.

Why nobody lists them

The pages ranking for this phrase are law school programme descriptions, law firm practice pages and a discussion forum. Each is accurate about its own scope and silent about the field. A firm page tells you what that firm handles. A programme page tells you what that programme teaches. Neither sets the statutes side by side with their targets, because neither is organised around the question a reader actually has, which is: which of these applies to my situation?

This page answers that and nothing else. It does not give legal advice, and it does not rate or recommend any firm or programme. The companion pages on which kind of health care lawyer you need and route before you search deal with people and procedure; this one deals with the law itself.

Read the last column first. Eight of the ten are enforcement regimes: they exist to let a federal agency act against an institution, not to give a patient a lever. That is not a criticism of them; it is a structural fact that decides where a complaint should go.

The five fraud and abuse laws

HHS's Office of Inspector General groups these together, and they are the numerical bulk of the field. They are also the part that generates most of the specialist legal work.

False Claims Act, 31 U.S.C. 3729–3733. OIG states that it is illegal to submit claims for payment to Medicare or Medicaid that you know or should know are false or fraudulent, with fines of up to three times the programs' loss plus $11,000 per claim filed.

Anti-Kickback Statute, 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7b(b). It prohibits the knowing and willful payment of "remuneration" to induce or reward patient referrals or the generation of business involving any item or service payable by the Federal health care programs, with fines, jail terms, and exclusion and penalties of up to $50,000 per kickback plus three times the amount of the remuneration.

Physician Self-Referral Law, the Stark Law, 42 U.S.C. 1395nn. It prohibits physicians from referring patients to receive "designated health services" payable by Medicare or Medicaid from entities with which the physician or an immediate family member has a financial relationship.

Exclusion Statute, 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7. Where it applies, Medicare, Medicaid, and other Federal health care programs will not pay for items or services that you furnish. For a clinician this is the most consequential of the five, because it removes the payer rather than levying a sum.

Civil Monetary Penalties Law, 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7a, under which OIG may seek penalties that range from $10,000 to $50,000 per violation.

One distinction is worth holding on to, because it is the one non-specialists most often collapse. The Anti-Kickback Statute turns on intent — knowing and willful payment to induce referrals. The Stark Law turns on the existence of a financial relationship and a referral for designated health services. Two laws, similar territory, different triggers. The penalty figures above are those OIG publishes; monetary amounts in this area are set by statute and adjusted over time, so they should be read with their source rather than quoted as fixed.

The three laws about what you get

A second group governs the encounter between an institution and a patient.

EMTALA, section 1867 of the Social Security Act, enacted in 1986, requires Medicare-participating hospitals offering emergency services to provide a medical screening examination (MSE) when a request is made for examination or treatment for an emergency medical condition (EMC), including active labor, and to provide stabilizing treatment where one is found, with access assured regardless of ability to pay.

The No Surprises Act, in force since 2022, covers three situations: most emergency services, even if you get them out-of-network, non-emergency services from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities, and services from out-of-network air ambulance service providers. In those cases you can't be charged more than in-network cost-sharing.

The essential health benefit requirement obliges Marketplace plans to cover ten defined categories, from ambulatory patient services through to pediatric services including oral and vision care, plus birth control and breastfeeding coverage. The full list and what it does not promise are in what the law requires covered.

These three are the only place in the ten where a patient's experience is the direct subject of the rule. Everything else regulates institutional conduct and reaches the patient as a by-product. Which door each of these applies at is set out in which door, and what it must do.

The two laws about information

HIPAA is the one most people have heard of and the one whose scope is most often misstated. It binds covered entities, defined at 45 CFR 160.103 as (1) A health plan. (2) A health care clearinghouse. (3) A health care provider who transmits any health information in electronic form in connection with a transaction covered by this subchapter. The third limb is conditional: a provider who never transmits electronically is outside it. Which entities are and are not covered, and why the words provider and supplier are kept apart, is unpicked in a legal term, not a synonym.

HITECH is the statutory basis for federal health IT regulation. The ONC Health IT Certification Program operates under the authority granted by section 3001(c)(5) of the Public Health Service Act (PHSA) and as defined in the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act. Certification is voluntary; what it does and does not assert is examined in HIT, HIE, EHR sorted.

The two you can actually use

Strip out the enforcement regimes and two levers remain in an individual's hands.

The privacy complaint. OCR states that anyone can file a complaint. It must be filed in writing by mail, fax, e-mail, or via the OCR Complaint Portal, must name the covered entity or business associate, and must be filed within 180 days of when you knew that the act or omission complained of occurred, with OCR able to extend the 180-day period if you can show "good cause". There is no filing fee and no requirement to instruct anyone.

The billing protection. The No Surprises Act does not require you to invoke it. In the three covered situations, out-of-network cost-sharing is prohibited, so the protection operates on the bill itself. Your job is to recognise when a bill should not have been charged at out-of-network rates.

Both have the same practical shape: a defined trigger, a defined counterparty, and no professional gatekeeper. That is why they are worth knowing before anything else in the list.

Why this field pays what it does

The concentration of enforcement regimes explains the economics. Eight of the ten laws above create institutional exposure measured in multiples of programme loss, per-claim penalties, or exclusion from federal payment. An organisation facing any of those is buying advice against a quantified downside, which is a different purchasing decision from an individual buying help with a dispute.

That is the structural reason the specialist end of this field is oriented toward institutions, and the reason an individual searching for help often finds firms describing work that has nothing to do with their problem. The taxonomy of who these lawyers actually serve is in which kind you actually need.

Sorting a problem into the right law

Is it about a record? HIPAA, and the route is an OCR complaint on a 180-day clock.

Is it about a bill? Check the No Surprises situations first, then the plan's own rules. Coverage disputes route through the plan and its regulator, not through the fraud statutes.

Is it about being refused care in an emergency? EMTALA, which applies to Medicare-participating hospitals offering emergency services and covers screening and stabilisation only.

Is it about someone else's conduct toward a programme? That is the fraud and abuse group, and it is enforcement territory rather than a private remedy. The wider question of who pays and who decides sits in who pays your claim and how the system actually works.

Questions people actually ask

What is health care law?

It is not a single statute. It is a working label for at least ten separate federal laws with different targets: fraud and abuse rules aimed at institutions and physicians, access and coverage rules aimed at hospitals and plans, and information rules aimed at anyone holding health records. Knowing which one governs a situation is the whole of the practical question.

Which laws cover fraud and abuse?

OIG identifies five: the False Claims Act at 31 U.S.C. 3729-3733, the Anti-Kickback Statute at 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7b(b), the Physician Self-Referral Law or Stark Law at 42 U.S.C. 1395nn, the Exclusion Statute at 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7, and the Civil Monetary Penalties Law at 42 U.S.C. 1320a-7a.

What is the difference between the Anti-Kickback Statute and the Stark Law?

The Anti-Kickback Statute prohibits the knowing and willful payment of remuneration to induce or reward patient referrals or the generation of business payable by federal health care programs. The Stark Law prohibits physicians from referring patients for designated health services payable by Medicare or Medicaid to entities with which the physician or an immediate family member has a financial relationship.

Which of these laws can an individual actually use?

Two are directly usable by a patient without a lawyer. A privacy complaint goes to the HHS Office for Civil Rights, which must be filed in writing within 180 days of when you knew the act or omission occurred. And the No Surprises Act protections apply automatically to most emergency services and to out-of-network clinicians at in-network facilities.

Does EMTALA give me a right to treatment?

A narrow one. Section 1867 of the Social Security Act, enacted in 1986, requires Medicare-participating hospitals offering emergency services to provide a medical screening examination when a request is made for an emergency medical condition and to provide stabilising treatment where one is found, regardless of ability to pay. It is an access rule, not a payment rule, and it does not extend beyond screening and stabilisation.

Where does health IT regulation come from?

The ONC Health IT Certification Program operates under the authority granted by section 3001(c)(5) of the Public Health Service Act and as defined in the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act, known as HITECH. That is the statutory route by which certification criteria for health IT are set in federal regulation rather than by industry.

Why do law firm and law school pages not list the statutes?

Because each is describing a service or a curriculum rather than a field. A firm page lists what that firm handles; a programme page lists what that programme teaches. Neither has a reason to lay the statutes side by side with their targets, which is the view that tells you which law is relevant to a particular problem.
